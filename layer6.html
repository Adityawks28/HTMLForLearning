VLA Systems: the assembly

Vision-language-action models as complete, deployed systems: action heads derived from first principles, the training recipe, the latency war, dual-system architectures, honest evaluation, the landmark systems — and a blueprint for building your own. Everything from Layers 1–5 converges here.

Scroll to begin · 7 chapters

Five layers ago, a vector was a list of numbers. Now: those vectors became matrices and gradients (L1), stacked into trainable networks and transformers (L2), pretrained into eyes and an inner voice (L3), taught to act from demonstration and consequence (L4), and given a body with reflexes and a nervous system (L5). A VLA is what happens when you refuse to keep those achievements separate. This layer is systems engineering at its most honest: every design choice below is a collision between two earlier layers, resolved under a deadline measured in milliseconds.

Chapter 01

The VLA, Assembled

Layer 2's finale made a promise in one sentence: a VLA is a VLM with an action head. True — and like "a car is an engine with wheels," it hides all the engineering. This chapter opens the hood and names every part precisely.

1.1 · Anatomy of a robot brain

Definition — the vision-language-action model

A VLA is a single network mapping (images, proprioception, instruction) to actions:

$$\pi_\theta:\;\big(\,o_t^{\text{cam}},\;\mathbf q_t,\;\ell\,\big)\;\longmapsto\;\mathbf a_{t:t+H}$$

built as: a pretrained vision encoder (ViT/DINOv2/SigLIP — Layer 3 Ch 1–2) turning each camera frame into patch tokens; a pretrained language backbone (Layer 3 Ch 4–6) consuming those tokens plus the instruction; a small projection of proprioception ($\mathbf q_t$, the joint state — Layer 5 Ch 1) into the same token stream; and an action head emitting a chunk of $H$ future actions (Layer 4 Ch 7's chunking). Trained, at its core, by behavior cloning on teleoperated demonstrations (Layer 4 Ch 7) — maximum likelihood (Layer 1 Ch 5) with motors as the vocabulary.

Intuition — why the LLM is in the loop at all

Couldn't a plain policy network do this? For one rehearsed task, yes — ACT does. The LLM earns its gigaflops by importing the web's knowledge into the control loop: it has read what a sponge is for, that mugs hold liquid, that "the leftmost one" is a resolvable phrase. Layer 3 showed a tiny projector suffices to make image tokens legible to a language model; the VLA bet is that the same trick makes action legible — that manipulation is one more language the pretrained giant can learn to speak. The evidence (Ch 5–6) says: substantially yes for semantics, only partially for physics.

Follow one tick: 224×224×3 pixels become 196 patch tokens (Layer 3's tokenizer), join ~20 instruction tokens and a few proprioception tokens in the backbone, and exit as a 50×7 action chunk. Click each stage — the readout names its shape, its parameter count, and the chapter that built it. A VLA is not mysterious; it is traceable.

1.2 · Observation & action spaces — the quiet decisions

Two design choices shape everything downstream. Observations: how many cameras (wrist cameras see contact, scene cameras see context — most systems use both), image resolution (quadratic token cost — Layer 3 Ch 1), history length (one frame is nearly memoryless; stacking frames buys the Markov property of Layer 4 Ch 1 at token cost). Actions: the space itself — end-effector pose deltas (workspace-native, embodiment-agnostic, needs Layer 5's IK underneath) vs. joint positions (direct, embodiment-specific) — plus gripper state; the control rate (10–50 Hz); and the chunk length $H$ (Layer 4 Ch 7's compounding-error dial: long chunks are smooth but briefly blind). Cross-embodiment training (Ch 3) pushes hard toward EE-space actions, because "move the hand 2 cm left" means the same thing on every robot, while "joint 3 plus 0.1 rad" does not.

Chapter 02

Action Heads — the Central Design Choice

How should a network emit an action? The obvious answer — regress the numbers with MSE — fails on real demonstration data for a reason you can prove in three lines. That theorem is the fork in the road from which every modern action head descends.

2.1 · The multimodality theorem — why regression crashes

Derivation — MSE predicts the mean, and the mean is a collision
1

What does MSE training actually produce? For a fixed observation, the optimal prediction $a^{\ast}$ minimizes $\mathbb{E}\big[(A-a)^2\big]$ over the demonstrated actions $A$. Expand with Layer 1 Ch 4's variance shortcut:

$$\mathbb{E}\big[(A-a)^2\big]=\operatorname{Var}(A)+\big(\mathbb{E}[A]-a\big)^2$$

The first term is fixed; the second is minimized at $a^{\ast}=\mathbb{E}[A]$. MSE regression is condemned to output the conditional mean.

2

Now the data. Human demonstrations are multimodal: facing an obstacle, half your teleoperators veered left and half veered right — both correct. The action distribution has two peaks, and its mean lies between them: straight into the obstacle. The average of two good actions is routinely a terrible action — averaging "grasp the handle from above" and "from the side" yields a pose that grasps nothing.

3

The cure is structural, not more data: the head must represent a distribution over actions and sample from it — committing to one mode — rather than predict a point. Every serious action head is one answer to "how do we put a samplable, multimodal distribution on a 7-DOF action chunk?" Three answers follow.

Interactive

Mode collapse, demonstrated — three heads, one obstacle

The faint trails are the training demonstrations — half pass left, half right. Sample the MSE head and watch every rollout take the average path, straight through the pillar: the theorem, colliding. The token and flow heads commit — each sample picks a side. This single picture is why the field abandoned plain regression for anything contact-rich.

2.2 · Answer one — actions as tokens (RT-2, OpenVLA)

Definition — action tokenization

Quantize each action dimension into $K$ bins (typically $K=256$) over its observed range; an action becomes a short string of discrete tokens — e.g. 7 dimensions → 7 tokens, often spliced into rarely-used slots of the LLM's existing vocabulary. Train with the LLM's own next-token cross-entropy (Layer 3 Ch 4 — unchanged); act by autoregressive decoding (Layer 3 Ch 6).

Intuition — the bargain

The elegance: the LLM never learns a new skill — actions are just more words, the softmax over bins is a genuine multimodal distribution (two peaks, no averaging), and every piece of LLM infrastructure (loss, decoding, KV cache) works untouched. RT-2's signature emergent feats — "move the banana to the sum of two plus one" — come from this seamlessness: web reasoning flows into motor tokens with no seam to cross. The bills: quantization error (256 bins ≈ coarse millimeters), and autoregressive decoding of 7×H tokens per chunk — slow, which Chapter 4 will weigh exactly.

2.3 · Answer two — diffusion, and flow matching (π0, GR00T)

Keep actions continuous, but model their full distribution: Layer 2 Ch 7's generative trick, pointed at action chunks. Diffusion Policy trains a network to denoise action chunks (noise in, demonstrated chunk out, conditioned on observations) and samples by iterative denoising. Its modern streamlining — used by π0 — is flow matching, simple enough to derive completely:

Derivation — flow matching in four steps
1

Draw a straight path between noise and data. Sample noise $\mathbf x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ and a real demonstrated chunk $\mathbf x_1$, and connect them by linear interpolation:

$$\mathbf x_t=(1-t)\,\mathbf x_0+t\,\mathbf x_1,\qquad t\in[0,1]$$
2

The path's velocity is trivial. Differentiate (Layer 1 Ch 3 — it's linear in $t$):

$$\frac{d\mathbf x_t}{dt}=\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_0$$

constant along the whole path: the straight-line direction from this noise to this data point.

3

Regress it. Train a network $v_\theta$ to predict that velocity from the interpolated point, the time, and the observation — plain MSE (legal here! the velocity field average is exactly what we want; the multimodality lives in which $\mathbf x_0$ you start from):

$$\mathcal L=\mathbb E_{t,\,\mathbf x_0,\,\mathbf x_1}\Big\|\,v_\theta(\mathbf x_t,\,t,\,o)-(\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_0)\,\Big\|^2$$
4

Sample by following the arrows. At inference, draw fresh noise and integrate the ODE $\;d\mathbf x/dt=v_\theta(\mathbf x,t,o)\;$ from $t{=}0$ to $1$ — Layer 5 Ch 9's integrators, ~10 Euler steps because the learned paths are nearly straight (diffusion's curved paths need 5–10× more). Different noise → different mode: left-around or right-around, each committed and clean. One network, one MSE loss, full multimodality, fast continuous actions — it is easy to see why this head won the 2024–25 generation of VLAs.

Interactive

Flow matching, watched — noise becoming decisions

Gray cloud at $t{=}0$: pure Gaussian noise (a hundred candidate action chunks, undecided). Drag $t$ forward and each point rides its straight arrow; by $t{=}1$ the cloud has condensed onto the two demonstrated modes — every sample a committed decision, none an average. Training teaches the arrows; inference just follows them. New noise re-rolls fate: same field, different commitments.

Where this came from

Answer three, for completeness: ACT's CVAE head (a latent variable picks the mode, a transformer decodes the chunk) — historically the proof that chunking + multimodal heads crack fine manipulation. All three answers obey the theorem; they differ in speed, smoothness, and how naturally they cohabit with an LLM. Chapter 6 scores them system by system.

Chapter 03

The Training Recipe

A VLA's capability is mostly a property of its diet. The recipe that emerged across labs has three courses, served in order, each solving the previous one's deficiency — and its central tension (keep the web knowledge while learning the robot) has a precise, familiar mathematical shape.

3.1 · The data pyramid

Definition — the three-course diet

Base — internet scale: the vision-language pretraining of Layer 3 (billions of image-text pairs, all of text). Teaches what things are. Zero robots involved. Middle — cross-embodiment robot data: pooled demonstrations from many labs and robot types — Open X-Embodiment aggregates 1M+ episodes across 20+ embodiments. Teaches what manipulation is like: approach, contact, grasp, the grammar of moving matter. Tip — target data: hundreds-to-thousands of teleoperated demos on your robot, your tasks (collected exactly as Layer 5 Ch 8 hinted: drive the robot, ros2 bag record). Teaches the specifics that pay the bills.

Intuition — a surgeon's education

The pyramid is a curriculum (fittingly): the base is school — language, anatomy, the world; the middle is residency — thousands of hours of generic procedure across many patients; the tip is the fellowship — this operation, this hospital's equipment. Skip the base and your surgeon can't read a chart (the robot can't parse "the leftmost mug"); skip the middle and every motion is learned from scratch; skip the tip and competence stays generic. Scaling-law thinking (Layer 3 Ch 5) applies with a twist the field keeps re-learning: in robot data, diversity of scenes/objects/embodiments buys generalization faster than raw hours of one cell — the $D$ that matters is measured in distinct situations.

3.2 · Co-training, and the forgetting problem

Derivation — why fine-tuning alone erases the base of the pyramid
1

Naive recipe: take the pretrained VLM, fine-tune purely on robot data. Gradient descent (Layer 1 Ch 3) optimizes only the loss it is shown; nothing anchors the weights that encoded web knowledge, so they drift wherever robot-loss reduction points. The VLM's language and visual breadth — never represented in the new loss — decays: catastrophic forgetting. Symptom: the robot grasps beautifully but no longer understands "the one next to the apple."

2

The standard fix is a mixed objective — co-training:

$$\mathcal L=\lambda\,\mathcal L_{\text{robot}}+(1-\lambda)\,\mathcal L_{\text{web}}$$

keep serving vision-language batches alongside action batches, so the web knowledge keeps defending itself in the gradient. Recognize the shape: it is Layer 1 Ch 7's regularization (a second term restraining the first), and Layer 3/4's KL-leash instinct — stay near the distribution that made you capable — implemented as data rather than as a penalty. RT-2 named this co-fine-tuning; some form of it is in every serious recipe since.

3

The dial $\lambda$ trades the pyramid's levels against each other, and the trade is real: heavier robot mixing → better task success, weaker instruction-following and novel-object behavior; lighter → the reverse. Feel the (stylized) frontier below.

3.3 · Post-training — past the teacher

The pyramid produces a generalist imitator, capped (Layer 4 Ch 7) at its teachers' quality. The post-training stage borrows Layer 3 Ch 6's alignment playbook and Layer 4's machinery to push past: SFT on curated excellence (filter the demos — clone only the clean, fast episodes; quality-weighted behavior cloning); advantage-weighted imitation where a success signal exists (Layer 4 Ch 5's advantage, used to re-weight the clone loss: copy the good demos harder); RL fine-tuning in simulation for skills with crisp rewards (with Layer 5 Ch 9's randomization bridging back to metal); and, increasingly, preference-based tuning — human picks the better of two rollouts, Bradley-Terry → DPO (Layer 3 Ch 6, verbatim) on robot trajectories. The strict analogy to the LLM stack — pretrain, SFT, preferences — is not a coincidence; it is the same problem wearing a gripper.

Chapter 04

Inference & Deployment — the Latency War

A chatbot may ponder for a second; a robot mid-motion may not. Reconciling billion-parameter brains with millisecond reflexes is this chapter — and it starts, as all good engineering does, with arithmetic.

4.1 · The latency budget, derived

Derivation — can a 3B model control at 50 Hz? Do the math.
1

From the PyTorch supplement: a forward pass costs ≈ $2N$ FLOPs per token for an $N$-parameter model. A VLA tick processes roughly $T\approx300$ tokens (196 image patches + instruction + proprioception). One inference:

$$\text{FLOPs}\approx 2NT = 2\cdot(3\times10^{9})\cdot300\approx1.8\ \text{TFLOPs}$$

On robot-side hardware delivering ~30 effective TFLOP/s (an embedded GPU, real-world efficiency): $t_{\text{inf}}\approx60$ ms — call it 10–15 Hz, best case, for the full model.

2

50 Hz single-action control is therefore out — the deadline is 20 ms and we cost 60. But Layer 4 Ch 7's chunking changes the equation: emit $H$ actions per inference, execute them at $f_{\text{ctrl}}$, and the deadline relaxes to the chunk's duration:

$$t_{\text{inf}}\;\lt\;\frac{H}{f_{\text{ctrl}}}\qquad\text{e.g. }H=50,\ f_{\text{ctrl}}=50\text{ Hz}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{deadline}=1\,\text{s}\;\gg\;60\,\text{ms}$$

Chunking converts an impossible per-step problem into a comfortable per-second one — and is why π0 runs 50 Hz control from a 3B brain. (Token heads pay extra: autoregressively decoding 7×H action tokens multiplies the decode cost — one concrete reason the flow generation pulled ahead.)

3

The residual cost is reactivity: within a chunk the policy is open-loop (Layer 4 Ch 7's blindness). Real systems replan before chunks expire and blend overlapping chunks (temporal ensembling); and when even that's too slow for the task's dynamics, you split the brain — next section. Supporting cast from earlier books, all on duty here: bf16/int8 quantization, KV-cache reuse of the static prompt, torch.compile, distillation (Layer 2) into a smaller student.

Interactive

The budget calculator — feasibility you can slide

Indigo bar: inference time. Teal marker: the chunk deadline $H/f_{\text{ctrl}}$. Slide $H$ to 1 and watch almost every configuration go red — per-step inference from a big model is simply not a thing. Then find the other failure: huge $H$ at high rates is green on latency but the readout warns about the seconds of open-loop blindness you just bought. Deployment is navigating between those two reds.

4.2 · Dual-system designs — a brain with two clocks

Definition — System 2 / System 1 architectures (Helix, GR00T N1)

Split the policy by timescale. System 2: the full VLM, running slow (~1–10 Hz), consuming images + instruction, emitting a compact latent goal $\mathbf z$ — "what we're doing right now," as a vector. System 1: a small fast policy (tens of millions of params, 50–200 Hz) consuming fresh proprioception + $\mathbf z$, emitting actions. Trained jointly end-to-end, so $\mathbf z$ learns to be whatever interface the fast half needs. Figure's Helix pairs a 7B S2 with an S1 at 200 Hz; GR00T N1 pairs a VLM with a diffusion-transformer action module.

Intuition — and why it's principled, not a hack

This is Layer 5 Ch 10's frequency hierarchy, learned end-to-end: slow layers think, fast layers act, each ~10× the rate of the one above. The deep justification is timescale separation in the signal itself: task semantics ("we are pouring into the red mug") changes over seconds and deserves the expensive brain; contact dynamics change over milliseconds and need only a reflex that knows the current intent. The latent $\mathbf z$ is the contract between them — and notice it's the same shape as Layer 3's projector trick: a small learned interface welding two pretrained competences. You are also, of course, looking at the standard cognitive-science cartoon of yourself: deliberate System 2, reflexive System 1. The robots converged on it for the same reason evolution did — physics bills by the millisecond, understanding bills by the second.

Chapter 05

Generalization & Evaluation

What does a VLA actually generalize to — and how would you honestly know? The first question has a crisp empirical answer; the second has a statistical one that most robot demos quietly flunk.

5.1 · What transfers, and what doesn't

Intuition — semantics ride the web; physics rides the demos

The consistent finding across systems: semantic generalization is the web pretraining's gift and it is real — novel objects ("pick up the toy dinosaur" never demonstrated), novel phrasings, relational instructions ("the one left of the bowl"), even light reasoning (RT-2 moving a can to "2+1"). The VLM half recognizes and resolves; the action half executes a familiar motion on the new referent. Motor generalization is the demos' jurisdiction and it is narrow: a genuinely new skill — a wrist twist never demonstrated, an unfamiliar contact regime, much heavier dynamics — does not materialize from language. The rule of thumb the field operates by: new nouns come free; new verbs cost demonstrations. This is exactly the pyramid of Ch 3 talking: each capability generalizes about as far as its course of the diet was diverse.

5.2 · Failure modes — and the cage that catches them

Know the taxonomy and you can read any demo video critically. Perception failures: wrong object, missed object, lighting shift (Layers 2–3's domain gap). Grounding failures: hallucinated affordances — the VLM "knows" drawers open, so the policy tugs confidently on a painted-on handle; language priors overruling pixels. Compounding drift: Layer 4 Ch 7's quadratic, live — a slightly off grasp becomes an out-of-distribution state becomes flailing. Instruction ambiguity: "put it away" resolved differently than the operator meant. Seams: chunk boundaries (a hesitation every $H$ steps), S2/S1 disagreement during sudden change. None of these is hypothetical; all appear in every lab's blooper reel. The defense is Layer 5's stack working as a cage: joint/velocity/force limits in ros2_control that no network output can exceed, workspace fences, collision-checking the emitted chunk against the scene (Layer 5 Ch 7) before execution, watchdogs that freeze on perception dropout. A VLA is deployed inside classical robotics, never instead of it.

5.3 · Evaluation — with the statistics it deserves

Derivation — how many trials before you may claim a success rate?
1

An evaluation is $n$ Bernoulli trials (Layer 1 Ch 5's coin!) with true success probability $p$; you report $\hat p=k/n$. Its standard deviation comes straight from the binomial:

$$\sigma_{\hat p}=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$$

and a 95% confidence interval is $\hat p\pm1.96\,\sigma_{\hat p}$ (Layer 1 Ch 4's Gaussian, via the CLT).

2

Invert it: to pin $p$ within $\pm\varepsilon$ at 95% confidence you need

$$n\;=\;\frac{1.96^{2}\,p(1-p)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad p=0.8,\ \varepsilon=0.05:\;\;n\approx246\ \text{trials per task}$$

Worst case ($p=0.5$): $n\approx385$. Real numbers for real claims.

3

Now the uncomfortable part: at the $n=10$ typical of robot papers and launch videos, $\sigma_{\hat p}\approx0.13$ at $p=0.8$ — a genuinely-80% policy will routinely measure 60% or 100%. Two methods 10 points apart on 10 trials are statistically indistinguishable. Hence the field's evaluation crisis: real-robot trials cost minutes each (so $n$ stays small), simulators are cheap but carry their own gap (Layer 5 Ch 9), and there is no ImageNet moment yet. Read every robot result with this derivation in hand — and run the demo to feel just how loudly small-$n$ evals lie.

Interactive

Statistics is not pedantry here; it is the difference between knowledge and vibes.

6.2 · Open problems — the honest frontier

What the lineage has not solved, stated plainly. Dexterity: multi-finger, in-hand, deformables — demonstration data is scarce exactly where physics is hardest. Long horizon: VLAs execute skills; chaining thirty of them through a kitchen still leans on scripting, behavior trees, or an LLM planner above (and errors compound across the chain — Layer 4's theorem at the task level). Memory: most policies are nearly Markov in the last frames; "where did I put the spoon" is unsolved. Sample efficiency: hundreds of teleop demos per skill is the going rate; the bet on world models (Layer 4 Ch 8) and video pretraining is precisely to crush this constant. Evaluation (Ch 5): no shared benchmark commands belief. Safety certification: a stochastic 3B-parameter policy cannot be exhaustively verified; the practical answer remains Layer 5's cage plus statistics, and regulators have noticed. Each of these is a thesis, a startup, or both.

Chapter 07

Build One

Knowledge becomes a checklist. Here is the complete end-to-end blueprint for building a VLA-driven robot — every step a chapter you have already read — followed by a live closed loop, and a map of the whole journey.

7.1 · The end-to-end blueprint

Workflow — from empty bench to data flywheel
1

Hardware & stack (Layer 5). Arm + gripper + wrist and scene cameras; URDF written; ros2_control running joint controllers at 100–1000 Hz; TF tree publishing; the cage configured — joint/velocity/force limits, workspace fence, e-stop. Verify with classical control first: if a PID can't move it safely, a network certainly shouldn't.

2

Collect demonstrations (Layers 4–5). Teleoperate (leader arm, spacemouse, or VR) through the gravity-compensated robot; record everything — ros2 bag record on cameras, joint states, actions — at the control rate. Hundreds of episodes, varied deliberately: object poses, lighting, distractors, both elbow branches. Convert bags to a training dataset (LeRobot's format is the open standard).

3

Train (PyTorch supplement, Layers 2–4). Fine-tune a pretrained VLA (π0-class or OpenVLA-class via LeRobot) on your demos — or co-train with web data if you must preserve open-vocabulary behavior (Ch 3). bf16, torch.compile, cosine schedule, checkpoints; overfit one batch first to prove the plumbing.

4

Deploy as a node (Layer 5 Ch 8, Ch 4 here). The policy is one more ROS 2 node: subscribe images + joint states, infer a chunk, publish setpoints into ros2_control; chunk deadline checked against the latency budget (Ch 4.1); collision-check chunks before execution. Skeleton below.

5

Evaluate honestly (Ch 5). Define success crisply; randomize initial conditions; run enough trials for the claim you want to make ($n\approx250$ for ±5%); log failures by taxonomy.

6

Iterate — the flywheel. Deployment failures become tomorrow's training data: collect corrections on the failure modes (DAgger's insight, Layer 4 Ch 7), retrain, re-evaluate. Every serious robotics effort is this loop with better logistics.

# vla_node.py — the policy as one more ROS 2 node (skeleton)
class VLAPolicy(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('vla_policy')
        self.policy = load_pretrained('pi0_finetuned.ckpt').to('cuda').eval()
        self.create_subscription(Image, '/wrist_cam/image', self.on_img, qos_sensor)
        self.create_subscription(JointState, '/joint_states', self.on_q, 10)
        self.pub = self.create_publisher(JointTrajectory, '/arm_controller/command', 10)
        self.create_timer(1.0, self.replan)          # chunk cadence: H/f_ctrl (Ch 4)

    def replan(self):
        obs = pack(self.last_img, self.last_q, self.instruction)
        with torch.no_grad(), torch.autocast('cuda', dtype=torch.bfloat16):
            chunk = self.policy(obs)                 # (H, 7) — flow head, ~10 Euler steps
        if collision_free(chunk):                    # the cage checks the network (Ch 5)
            self.pub.publish(to_trajectory(chunk))   # ros2_control tracks it at 1 kHz

Every line is a chapter: QoS (L5 §8.5), no_grad + autocast (supplement), the chunk deadline (Ch 4.1), the cage (Ch 5.2).

7.3 · The whole curriculum, one picture

The whole curriculum, in one paragraph

A robot sees: pixels become patch tokens through a vision transformer (L3) whose attention (L2) is matrix multiplication and softmax (L1). It understands: an instruction meets those tokens inside a language model trained by maximum likelihood (L1) on the web (L3), aligned by preferences (L3/L4). It decides: an action head — built to defeat the multimodality theorem (L6) — emits a chunk (L4) by following a learned flow (L2/L6). It acts: the chunk passes a collision check (L5), becomes setpoints for a 1 kHz PID (L5) computed through Jacobians (L5) on hardware described in a URDF, wired by ROS 2 topics (L5), trained in PyTorch (supplement), rehearsed in simulators whose integrators you can stability-analyze (L5), improved by a data flywheel (L4/L6), and evaluated with confidence intervals (L1/L6). Nothing in that sentence is a black box to you anymore.
