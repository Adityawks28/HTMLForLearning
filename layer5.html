Robotics & VLA Curriculum · Layer 5

Robotics: the body

Kinematics, dynamics, control, sensing, motion planning, ROS 2, and simulation — the complete classical toolkit that turns mathematics into motion. Every formula derived from scratch; every concept something you can drag, tune, or crash.

Scroll to begin · 10 chapters

Four layers of mathematics and learning have been waiting for a body. This layer supplies it — and with it, the discipline that hardware enforces. Pixels forgive; payloads don't. The classical robotics in these chapters (transforms, Jacobians, PID, Kalman filters, planners) is not what learned policies replaced — it is what they stand on: the safety layer, the vocabulary, and very often still the inner loops of every modern robot, VLAs included. We build it all, derive it all, and wire it together with ROS 2.

Chapter 01

The Machine

Before mathematics: metal. A robot is a chain of rigid pieces, motors that bend the chain, and sensors that report what happened. Naming these parts precisely is the first step, because every equation in this layer is about them.

1.1 · Links, joints, actuators, sensors

A manipulator is an alternating chain of links (rigid bodies) and joints (the connections that allow motion). Two joint types dominate: revolute (rotation about an axis — an elbow) and prismatic (sliding along an axis — a piston). The chain ends in the end-effector: gripper, suction cup, welding tip — the part that touches the world. The list of all current joint values, $\mathbf{q}=(q_1,\dots,q_n)$, is the robot's configuration — one number per joint, and the single most important vector in this layer.

Each joint is driven by an actuator — almost always an electric motor plus a gearbox. The gearbox trades speed for torque (ratio $N$: output torque $\times N$, output speed $\div N$) and is a genuine design fork: high ratios (harmonic drives, ~100:1) give precision and strength but make the joint stiff and "numb" to contact; low ratios (quasi-direct drive, as in many legged robots) let the motor feel the world through the transmission — crucial when your job is touching things. Sensors live everywhere: encoders on every joint (measuring $q$ — the robot always knows its own pose far better than it knows the world), IMUs, force/torque sensors, cameras. Chapter 6 treats them properly.

Intuition — your arm, inventoried

Hold out your arm: shoulder ≈ 3 revolute joints sharing a point, elbow 1, wrist 3 — seven joints to place a 6-DOF hand, one joint spare (that spare is redundancy: keep your fingertip fixed on the table and you can still swing your elbow). Muscles are the actuators; tendons the transmission; proprioception the encoders. Every concept in this chapter has lived in your sleeve all along.

1.2 · Degrees of freedom & workspace

Definition — DOF and workspace

The degrees of freedom (DOF) of a robot is the number of independent joint variables — the dimension of its configuration space. A free rigid body in 3-D has 6 DOF (3 position + 3 orientation), so placing an object arbitrarily needs at least a 6-DOF arm; 7+ adds redundancy. The workspace is the set of all points the end-effector can reach — the image of all configurations under the kinematics of Chapter 2.

Derivation — the workspace of a 2-link arm is an annulus
1

Links of length $L_1, L_2$, both joints free. The farthest reach is both links aligned outward: radius $L_1+L_2$. The nearest is the second link folded fully back: radius $|L_1-L_2|$.

2

Every radius between is achievable by partially folding the elbow (the reach $r(\theta_2)=\sqrt{L_1^2+L_2^2+2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2}$ — the law of cosines, Layer 1 Ch 1 — sweeps continuously from $|L_1-L_2|$ to $L_1+L_2$ as $\theta_2$ goes from $\pi$ to $0$), and any direction by spinning the shoulder. Workspace: the ring

$$|L_1-L_2|\;\le\;\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\;\le\;L_1+L_2$$

with a dead zone in the middle whenever $L_1\neq L_2$ — a region the robot literally cannot touch, however clever its software. Geometry is destiny; feel it below.

Make the links equal and the dead zone closes (the arm can touch its own shoulder); make them unequal and a forbidden disk opens. Chapter 02

Forward Kinematics

The first real question of robotics: given the joint angles, where is the hand? The answer is a small machine built from Layer 1's rotation matrices — and the habit of thinking in coordinate frames that it teaches is the single most transferable skill in this field (it returns as ROS's TF tree in Chapter 8).

2.1 · Frames & homogeneous transforms

Definition — rotation matrix and homogeneous transform (2-D)

A frame rotated by $\theta$ has basis vectors $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ and $(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$ — and Layer 1 Ch 2 taught that the columns of a matrix are where the basis lands, so the rotation matrix writes itself:

$$R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$$

To also carry a translation $\mathbf{t}$ in one multiplication, embed both in a homogeneous transform acting on $(x,y,1)$:

$$T=\begin{pmatrix}R(\theta)&\mathbf{t}\\ \mathbf{0}&1\end{pmatrix}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad T\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{p}\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}R\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{t}\\1\end{pmatrix}$$

The padding 1 is the whole trick: it lets the translation ride inside a matrix product, so chains of frames become chains of multiplications — Layer 1's "matrix multiplication is composition," now load-bearing. (3-D is identical with $3\times3$ rotations in a $4\times4$ box.)

Intuition — frames are sentences with a point of view

"The cup is at (0.3, 0.1)" is meaningless until you say in whose coordinates — the camera's? the gripper's? the table's? A transform $T_{AB}$ is a translator: it rewrites a point expressed in frame $B$ into frame $A$'s language. Chains compose like relayed translations: $T_{\text{world,hand}}=T_{\text{world,shoulder}}\,T_{\text{shoulder,elbow}}\,T_{\text{elbow,hand}}$. Half of practical robotics bugs are sentences attributed to the wrong narrator — which is why ROS dedicates an entire subsystem (TF2) to tracking who's speaking.

2.2 · Forward kinematics, derived

Derivation — the 2-link arm, joint angles → hand position
1

Walk the chain. The elbow sits at the end of link 1, which points in direction $\theta_1$:

$$\mathbf{p}_{\text{elbow}}=\big(L_1\cos\theta_1,\;\,L_1\sin\theta_1\big)$$
2

Link 2 is mounted on link 1, so its absolute direction is the sum $\theta_1+\theta_2$ (rotations about the same axis compose by addition — multiply two $R(\theta)$'s and the angle-sum identities pop out). Add its contribution:

$$x=L_1\cos\theta_1+L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\qquad y=L_1\sin\theta_1+L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)$$
3

That's forward kinematics: a recipe of cosines, one term per link — and exactly the matrix chain $T_{01}(\theta_1)T_{12}(\theta_2)$ multiplied out. For real 6-DOF arms the principle is unchanged; only bookkeeping grows, which is why the field standardized it: Denavit–Hartenberg parameters assign each link four numbers and a canonical transform, and the URDF files of Chapter 8 are this same chain written as XML. FK is always: multiply the chain.

Chapter 03

Inverse Kinematics

Robotics' questions usually arrive backwards: not "where is the hand?" but "the cup is there — what angles put my hand on it?" Inverting the cosine recipe is harder than running it forward, in instructive ways: solutions can be multiple, absent, or infinite, and each case teaches something a robot programmer needs in their bones.

3.1 · The inverse problem and its personalities

Definition — inverse kinematics (IK)

Given a desired end-effector pose $\mathbf{x}_d$, find joint values $\mathbf{q}$ with $\text{FK}(\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{x}_d$. Unlike FK (always one answer, cheap), IK can have: multiple solutions (a 2-link arm reaches most points two ways — elbow-up and elbow-down), no solution (target outside the Chapter 1 annulus), or infinitely many (redundant arms: your 7-DOF arm holding a point while the elbow orbits).

Intuition — why backwards is harder

FK is evaluating a function; IK is solving an equation — and Layer 1 taught the asymmetry: evaluating $\cos$ is trivial, inverting mixtures of cosines is not. The multiplicity isn't a nuisance, it's choice: elbow-up vs elbow-down differ in clearance, joint limits, and how close they sit to a singularity, and motion planners (Ch 7) actively exploit the options. The no-solution case is why every grasp pipeline first asks "is it even reachable?"

3.2 · The analytic solution, derived completely

Derivation — closed-form IK for the 2-link arm
1

Find $\theta_2$ from the distance alone. The shoulder, elbow, target form a triangle with sides $L_1$, $L_2$, and $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. The law of cosines (Layer 1, Ch 1 — the same identity that proved the dot product) on the elbow's interior angle gives, after using $\cos(\pi-\theta_2)=-\cos\theta_2$:

$$\cos\theta_2=\frac{x^2+y^2-L_1^2-L_2^2}{2L_1L_2}=:D$$

Immediately the existence test falls out: a solution exists iff $|D|\le1$ — which is algebraically exactly the annulus condition of Chapter 1. The workspace was never a separate fact; it is IK's solvability condition.

2

Two elbows. $\cos\theta_2=D$ has two solutions:

$$\theta_2=\pm\;\text{atan2}\!\big(\sqrt{1-D^2},\,D\big)$$

the $+$ and $-$ being elbow-down and elbow-up — the two mirror triangles over the shoulder–target line. (Why atan2 and not $\arccos$? atan2$(y,x)$ returns the angle of the point $(x,y)$ with correct quadrant — robotics uses it everywhere angles are recovered from coordinates.)

3

Then $\theta_1$ by subtracting the wedge. The line to the target sits at angle $\text{atan2}(y,x)$. The first link doesn't point at the target; it points off by the small interior angle at the shoulder, whose tangent we can read from components along/perpendicular to link 1 — the target in link-1's frame is $(L_1+L_2\cos\theta_2,\;L_2\sin\theta_2)$. So:

$$\theta_1=\text{atan2}(y,x)-\text{atan2}\!\big(L_2\sin\theta_2,\;L_1+L_2\cos\theta_2\big)$$

Done: a complete, exact, microsecond-fast solver. Industrial 6-DOF arms have analogous closed forms (their last three axes intersecting at the wrist splits the problem into position + orientation halves); when geometry is less kind, we go numerical — next.

3.3 · Numerical IK — when there is no formula

Redundant arms, joint limits, extra objectives ("…and keep the camera level") break closed forms. The general tool is Layer 1's favorite move — linearize and iterate. The next chapter builds the Jacobian $J$, the matrix with $\dot{\mathbf{x}}=J\dot{\mathbf{q}}$; numerical IK simply inverts that relationship in small steps:

$$\Delta\mathbf{q}=J^{+}\,\big(\mathbf{x}_d-\text{FK}(\mathbf{q})\big)\qquad\text{repeat until converged}$$

where $J^{+}$ is the pseudo-inverse (least-squares inverse — Layer 1 Ch 6's normal equations, reborn). It is gradient-descent's sibling: follow the local linear map toward the target. Practical solvers add damping ($J^\top(JJ^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}$ — ridge regression! Layer 1 Ch 7) to behave near singularities, and project secondary objectives into the redundant null space. Every modern manipulation stack (MoveIt's KDL/TRAC-IK solvers, Ch 8) is this loop with engineering armor.

Chapter 04

The Jacobian

One matrix runs the show between geometry and motion. The Jacobian answers "if I nudge the joints, how does the hand move?" — Layer 1's derivative, grown up into robotics — and by a beautiful duality it also answers "if the hand pushes, what do the motors feel?"

4.1 · Velocities — differentiate the kinematics

Derivation — the 2-link Jacobian
1

Differentiate the FK of Ch 2 with respect to time, chain rule throughout (Layer 1 Ch 3, in both variables):

$$\dot x=-L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1-L_2\sin(\theta_1{+}\theta_2)(\dot\theta_1{+}\dot\theta_2)$$$$\dot y=\;\;\,L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1+L_2\cos(\theta_1{+}\theta_2)(\dot\theta_1{+}\dot\theta_2)$$
2

Collect into a matrix — joint rates in, hand velocity out:

$$\dot{\mathbf{x}}=J(\mathbf{q})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}},\qquad J=\begin{pmatrix}-L_1 s_1-L_2 s_{12} & -L_2 s_{12}\\[2pt] \;\;L_1 c_1+L_2 c_{12} & \;\;L_2 c_{12}\end{pmatrix}$$

(shorthand $s_{12}=\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)$ etc.). $J$ depends on $\mathbf{q}$: the same joint twitch moves the hand differently in different poses — the Jacobian is the local linear portrait of the arm, recomputed wherever you stand. Layer 1's "smooth maps are locally linear," wearing aluminum.

4.2 · Singularities — where the portrait collapses

Derivation — det J, and what its zero means
1

Compute the determinant (expand, apply the sine-difference identity; the cross terms collapse):

$$\det J=L_1L_2\sin\theta_2$$
2

Layer 1 Ch 2: determinant = area scaling. $\det J=0$ at $\theta_2=0$ or $\pi$ — arm fully stretched or fully folded — where the matrix flattens the space of hand velocities: both joints can only swing the hand sideways; no combination of joint rates moves it along the arm. A whole direction of motion is lost, exactly where the two IK branches merged in Ch 3's demo. Near (not at) the singularity it's worse in practice: producing modest speed in the dying direction demands enormous $\dot{\mathbf q}$ — the $J^{-1}$ blowing up that damped IK exists to tame.

3

The honest picture is the manipulability ellipsoid: feed unit joint speeds in all directions through $J$ and the output velocities trace an ellipse whose axes are $J$'s singular directions (the eigen-story of Layer 1 Ch 2, via $JJ^\top$). Round ellipse = agile pose; needle = one good direction; line = singular. Watch it breathe below.

Interactive

The manipulability ellipse — agility, drawn at the fingertip

The ellipse is every hand-velocity reachable with unit joint effort. Drive $\theta_2\to0$: the ellipse starves into a needle, det J → 0, and the readout turns coral. Real arm controllers monitor exactly this number and steer trajectories around its zeros — and now you know why videos of robot arms sometimes show a strange detour mid-reach.

4.3 · Forces — the transpose duality

Derivation — τ = JᵀF from one line of virtual work
1

Let the hand press on the world with force $\mathbf{F}$ while the joints hold torques $\boldsymbol\tau$. In equilibrium, any imagined tiny motion $\delta\mathbf{q}$ does zero net work:

$$\boldsymbol\tau^{\top}\delta\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{F}^{\top}\delta\mathbf{x}=0$$
2

But motions aren't independent — the kinematics ties them: $\delta\mathbf{x}=J\,\delta\mathbf{q}$. Substitute and factor:

$$\big(\boldsymbol\tau^{\top}-\mathbf{F}^{\top}J\big)\,\delta\mathbf{q}=0\ \ \text{for all }\delta\mathbf{q}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boldsymbol\tau=J^{\top}\mathbf{F}$$

The same matrix, transposed, converts hand forces to motor torques. No new derivation needed, ever: compute $J$ once, get velocity control and force control both. This duality is why force-controlled assembly, impedance control, and the "feel" of quasi-direct-drive legs all route through the Jacobian — and (Layer 1 Ch 2 foreshadowed it) why the transpose keeps showing up wherever a linear map runs backwards, gradients included.

Chapter 05

Dynamics & Control

Kinematics is geometry; the world runs on forces. Dynamics asks what torques produce what motions — and control asks the inverse under uncertainty: what torques, applied by feedback, make the robot do what we want despite gravity, friction, and our imperfect models. The star is the humble PID loop, running right now in every joint of every robot on Earth.

5.1 · Equations of motion — the Lagrangian shortcut

Intuition — energy bookkeeping beats force bookkeeping

Newton's $F=ma$ works but demands you track every internal constraint force pulling link on link — misery for chains. Lagrange's insight: write only the energies (kinetic $T$, potential $V$), and one turn-the-crank equation produces the motion, constraint forces never appearing. For learning-flavored readers: it is the same spirit as Layer 4's log-derivative trick — choose the formulation in which the hard terms cancel themselves.

Derivation — a 1-link arm under gravity, from energies to motion
1

Point mass $m$ at the end of a massless rod of length $l$, angle $\theta$ from straight-down, motor torque $\tau$. Energies:

$$T=\tfrac12 m l^2\dot\theta^{2}\qquad V=-mgl\cos\theta\qquad \mathcal{L}=T-V$$
2

The Euler–Lagrange equation (with the applied torque on the right):

$$\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot\theta}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta}=\tau$$

Compute each piece: $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\dot\theta=ml^2\dot\theta$, its time-derivative $ml^2\ddot\theta$; and $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta=-mgl\sin\theta$. Assemble:

$$ml^{2}\,\ddot\theta+mgl\sin\theta=\tau$$

Inertia times acceleration, plus a gravity torque that is worst ($mgl$) with the arm horizontal and zero hanging straight down — matching every intuition you have about holding a weight out sideways.

3

Multi-link arms follow the identical crank and land in the universal form

$$M(\mathbf{q})\,\ddot{\mathbf q}+C(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q})\,\dot{\mathbf q}+\mathbf g(\mathbf q)=\boldsymbol\tau$$

— configuration-dependent inertia $M$, velocity-coupling (Coriolis/centrifugal) $C$, gravity $\mathbf g$. Two practical readings: gravity compensation (command $\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf g(\mathbf q)$ and the arm floats, weightless under your hand — how teach-by-demonstration teleop works, the very data source of Layer 4 Ch 7), and why control is needed at all: $M$ and $\mathbf g$ are never known exactly, so pure feedforward always misses. Feedback eats the miss.

5.2 · PID control, derived term by term

Definition — the PID law

With error $e(t)=\theta_d-\theta(t)$:

$$\tau(t)=K_p\,e(t)\;+\;K_i\!\int_0^t e\,dt\;+\;K_d\,\dot e(t)$$

Proportional: push harder the wronger you are. Integral: accumulate grudges against persistent error. Derivative: brake against approach speed.

Derivation — what each gain provably does
1

P alone cannot finish the job. Suppose P-control settles the gravity arm at rest at some $\theta$: then $\ddot\theta=\dot\theta=0$, and the equation of motion demands torque balance,

$$K_p(\theta_d-\theta)=mgl\sin\theta\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;e_{ss}=\frac{mgl\sin\theta}{K_p}\neq0$$

A pure spring must stretch to hold a load: steady-state error is structural, shrinking only as $K_p\to\infty$ — which buys oscillation instead.

2

PD is a virtual spring–damper. Read $\tau=K_p(\theta_d-\theta)-K_d\dot\theta$ into the dynamics: the closed loop is literally a mass on a spring of stiffness $K_p$ with damper $K_d$, anchored at the target —

$$ml^2\ddot\theta+K_d\dot\theta+K_p\theta\approx K_p\theta_d\;(+\,\text{gravity})$$

a second-order system whose character you can choose: natural frequency $\omega_n=\sqrt{K_p/ml^2}$, damping ratio $\zeta=K_d/(2\sqrt{K_p\,ml^2})$. $\zeta\lt1$: overshoot and ring; $\zeta=1$: critical — fastest with no overshoot; $\zeta\gt1$: sluggish. Gain tuning is just sculpting this one ratio.

3

I erases the bias. At any steady state the integral term keeps growing unless $e=0$ — so the only equilibrium PID accepts is zero error: the integrator quietly learns the constant torque gravity demands and supplies it. Cost: integrators add lag and can wind up during saturation (hence anti-windup clamps in every real implementation). The full loop runs at 1 kHz per joint, in firmware, beneath everything — when Layer 6's VLA "moves the arm," its actions are setpoints handed to exactly this controller.

Interactive

Chapter 06

Sensing & State Estimation

Control assumed we know $\theta$. We never quite do. Every sensor lies a little, each in its own style — and the art of estimation is fusing several styles of lie into one good guess. Its crown jewel, the Kalman filter, is Layer 1's Bayes-with-Gaussians turned into an algorithm, and we derive it completely.

6.1 · The sensor suite, and how each one lies

SensorMeasuresIts style of lying
Joint encoderjoint angle $q$Almost honest — quantization only. Robots know their own pose superbly.
IMU (gyro + accelerometer)angular rate, specific forceFast and smooth, but integrating it drifts: bias × time accumulates without bound.
Wheel odometrydistance rolledDrifts with slip — confidently wrong after a long hallway.
Camera (RGB)appearanceRich but indirect: no depth, lighting-dependent — needs Layers 2–3 to interpret.
Depth / stereo / LiDARrange to surfacesGeometric truth with dropouts: glass, black surfaces, sunlight.
Force/torque, tactilecontact wrench, pressureNoisy and local — but the only sensors that feel.
Intuition — complementary liars

Notice the pattern: odometry/IMU are smooth but drifting (excellent short-term, hopeless long-term); GPS/vision landmarks are jittery but anchored (noisy each instant, unbiased forever). One lies slowly, the other lies loudly. Fusion isn't averaging two bad signals — it's pairing liars whose lies are orthogonal, taking smoothness from one and truth from the other. The Kalman filter is the provably-best way to do it when the lies are Gaussian.

6.2 · The Kalman filter, derived from Bayes

Derivation — 1-D Kalman: predict, then update (it's all Layer 1)
1

Beliefs are Gaussians. Track position as $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$. Predict: the robot commands a move $u$ with process noise $q^2$ (wheels slip). Shift the mean, and — variances of independent errors add (Layer 1 Ch 4) — inflate the uncertainty:

$$\mu^{-}=\mu+u\qquad \sigma^{2-}=\sigma^{2}+q^{2}$$

Prediction always loses certainty. That is drift, stated probabilistically.

2

Update = Bayes with two Gaussians. A measurement $z$ arrives with noise $r^2$. Posterior ∝ prior × likelihood, and the product of two Gaussians is Gaussian with precisions adding and means precision-weighted:

$$\frac{1}{\sigma^{2+}}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2-}}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\qquad \mu^{+}=\sigma^{2+}\Big(\frac{\mu^{-}}{\sigma^{2-}}+\frac{z}{r^{2}}\Big)$$

(One completes the square in the exponents — Layer 1 Ch 4 algebra; each observer votes with weight = its confidence.)

3

Rearrange into the famous gain form. Define $K=\dfrac{\sigma^{2-}}{\sigma^{2-}+r^{2}}\in[0,1]$ and the update becomes

$$\mu^{+}=\mu^{-}+K\,(z-\mu^{-})\qquad \sigma^{2+}=(1-K)\,\sigma^{2-}$$

Read it like prose: move toward the measurement by a fraction K equal to how much of the total uncertainty is yours. Confident prediction, noisy sensor → $K\approx0$, ignore the blip. Drifted prediction, sharp sensor → $K\approx1$, snap to it. The filter continuously re-derives its own trust — and the multivariate version (matrices replacing scalars, $J$'s as measurement maps) is the same three lines wearing Layer 1 Ch 2's linear algebra. Add landmarks to the state and you get SLAM; this loop is the beating heart of every drone, vacuum, and self-driving stack.

The RMSE readout keeps score — the fusion beats both parents every time.

Chapter 07

Motion Planning

Between "I want the hand there" and "send these joint angles" lies a genuinely deep problem: find a whole path that never hits anything, through a space you can't even draw. This chapter builds planning completely — the configuration-space idea that reframes it, the graph search that solves small versions exactly, the sampling methods that conquer high dimensions, and the time-parameterization that turns a path into motion.

7.1 · Configuration space — the reframe that makes planning possible

Definition — C-space, free space, and the planning problem

The configuration space $\mathcal{C}$ is the set of all joint vectors $\mathbf q$ — for our 2-link arm, the $(\theta_1,\theta_2)$ square (a torus, really: both angles wrap). Workspace obstacles map into it: the C-obstacle $\mathcal{C}_{obs}$ is every configuration in which any part of the robot's body intersects an obstacle, and $\mathcal{C}_{free}=\mathcal C\setminus\mathcal C_{obs}$. Motion planning, stated cleanly at last:

$$\text{find a continuous path }\;\mathbf q(s)\in\mathcal{C}_{free},\;\;\mathbf q(0)=\mathbf q_{start},\;\;\mathbf q(1)=\mathbf q_{goal}$$
Intuition — the robot becomes a point

In the workspace, planning is awful: an arm is a jointed shape sweeping through clutter, and "does this motion collide?" tangles geometry with mechanics. The C-space move shifts all of that complexity into the map: the robot collapses to a single point, obstacles inflate into strange C-obstacle continents, and planning becomes the oldest problem there is — walk a point through a maze. The cost is honest: the maze lives in $n$ dimensions (one per joint) and its walls have shapes nobody can write formulas for; we can only query them ("does configuration $\mathbf q$ collide? yes/no"). Every planner below is a strategy for exploring a maze you can only tap with a cane.

7.2 · Graph search — Dijkstra, A*, and the optimality proof

Discretize $\mathcal C_{free}$ into a grid (or any graph): nodes are configurations, edges connect neighbors, edge costs are distances. Dijkstra's algorithm expands nodes in order of cost-from-start $g(n)$ — guaranteed optimal, but it floods in every direction, knowing nothing about where the goal is. A* adds a compass:

Definition — A*

Expand open nodes in order of $f(n)=g(n)+h(n)$, where $h(n)$ estimates remaining cost to the goal. $h$ is admissible if it never overestimates: $h(n)\le$ true remaining cost (straight-line distance is the classic choice — the real path can't beat a straight line).

Derivation — admissible h ⇒ A* returns an optimal path
1

Let $C^{\ast}$ be the true optimal cost. Suppose, for contradiction, A* terminates by expanding the goal via a suboptimal route: $f(\text{goal})=g(\text{goal})>C^{\ast}$ (at the goal $h=0$, so $f=g$ there).

2

The optimal path exists, so at this moment some node $n$ on it sits in the open set with its optimal $g(n)$. Then

$$f(n)=g(n)+h(n)\;\le\;g(n)+\big(\text{true remaining from }n\big)=C^{\ast}\;<\;f(\text{goal})$$

using admissibility in the middle step.

3

But A* always expands the open node of smallest $f$ — so it must pick $n$ before the goal. Contradiction; the suboptimal termination can't happen. Moral of the proof: the heuristic may be optimistic but never pessimistic — optimism only makes you check promising routes first; pessimism can make you abandon the truth. (Layer 4's UCB rang the same bell: optimism is safe; the better $h$ hugs the truth, the fewer nodes you touch, with $h\equiv0$ collapsing A* back into Dijkstra.)

Click cells to draw walls, then search. The tinted flood is the explored set, shaded by expansion order; the indigo line is the returned path — identical length for both algorithms (the proof guaranteed it), but count the flood: Dijkstra soaks the whole map while A*'s flood leans hungrily toward the goal. The heuristic doesn't change the answer; it changes the bill.

7.3 · Sampling-based planning — RRT and friends

Derivation — why grids die, and sampling survives
1

Grid resolution $k$ cells per joint, $n$ joints: $k^{n}$ cells. A modest arm — 7 joints, 100 cells each — is $100^{7}=10^{14}$ cells. At a nanosecond per collision check that is days just to build the map, for one obstacle layout. The curse of dimensionality (Layer 1 Ch 7's nemesis) kills explicit maps outright.

2

The escape: never build the map. Sample random configurations, keep the collision-free ones, connect near neighbors with collision-checked segments — a sparse graph that probes only where queries actually go. Cost scales with the complexity of the solution path, not the volume of the space.

Definition — the RRT algorithm (Rapidly-exploring Random Tree)

Grow a tree from $\mathbf q_{start}$. Repeat: (1) sample a random configuration $\mathbf q_{rand}$ (with a small bias, ~5%, toward the goal); (2) find the tree's nearest node; (3) steer from it one step $\epsilon$ toward $\mathbf q_{rand}$; (4) if the step is collision-free, add it. Stop when a node lands near the goal; the path is read off by walking parents back to the root.

Intuition — why "rapidly exploring" is earned

The magic is in step (2): the probability a tree node is selected for extension is proportional to the volume of space closest to it — its Voronoi cell. Nodes on the frontier of unexplored territory own gigantic cells, so the tree is automatically yanked toward the unknown; interior nodes, crowded by neighbors, are left alone. Exploration pressure, for free, with no map and no gradient. RRT is probabilistically complete (a solution, if one exists, is found with probability → 1) but not optimal — its paths jag. RRT* repairs that by rewiring the tree as better routes appear (asymptotically optimal); PRM builds a reusable roadmap once when many queries will share one world. These three are the workhorses inside MoveIt (Ch 8), planning real 7-DOF reaches in milliseconds.

Interactive

Watch an RRT think — a tree yanked toward the unknown

The tree feels its way around the obstacles — note how growth concentrates at the frontier, exactly the Voronoi argument — until a branch touches the goal and the path lights up. Re-run: a different tree, a different jagged path, same success. Tiny $\epsilon$ creeps; huge $\epsilon$ lunges and gets rejected at walls. The jaggedness is real and is precisely what the next section exists to fix.

7.4 · From path to trajectory — adding the clock

A planner returns geometry: waypoints with no notion of time. Motors need a trajectory — position as a function of time respecting velocity and acceleration limits. (First, practical systems shortcut the jagged path: repeatedly try connecting random pairs of waypoints directly, keeping straight segments that miss obstacles.) Then time-parameterize:

Derivation — the trapezoidal velocity profile, completely
1

Travel distance $d$ with limits $v_{\max}, a_{\max}$. The greedy optimum: accelerate flat-out, cruise, brake flat-out — velocity vs. time a trapezoid. Ramp time and ramp distance:

$$t_a=\frac{v_{\max}}{a_{\max}}\qquad d_a=\tfrac12 a_{\max}t_a^{2}=\frac{v_{\max}^{2}}{2a_{\max}}$$
2

Case A (long move, $d\ge 2d_a$): a cruise phase exists, covering $d-2d_a$ at $v_{\max}$. Total time:

$$T=2t_a+\frac{d-2d_a}{v_{\max}}=\frac{d}{v_{\max}}+\frac{v_{\max}}{a_{\max}}$$

— the ideal time $d/v_{\max}$ plus one full ramp as the price of obeying physics.

3

Case B (short move, $d\lt 2d_a$): no room to reach $v_{\max}$ — the trapezoid degenerates to a triangle. Accelerate to a peak $v_p$ then immediately brake; each half covers $d/2=v_p^{2}/2a_{\max}$, so

$$v_p=\sqrt{a_{\max}\,d}\qquad T=\frac{2v_p}{a_{\max}}=2\sqrt{\frac{d}{a_{\max}}}$$

Run this per segment (or with quintic polynomials when smooth jerk matters), feed the result to Chapter 5's PID as a stream of setpoints, and the pipeline is complete: plan → shortcut → time → track. That four-beat rhythm is what Nav2 and MoveIt industrialize in the next chapter.

Where you'll meet this again

C-space is where Layer 4's policies actually live (a policy maps observations to points in C-space); A*'s admissibility echoes UCB's optimism; and Layer 6's VLAs increasingly skip explicit planning for short horizons — yet ship with a classical planner underneath as the safety net and long-horizon scaffold. Knowing both sides is the working roboticist's edge.

Chapter 08

ROS & ROS 2

A real robot is dozens of programs — camera driver, estimator, planner, controller, logger — written by different people, running at different rates, needing to talk. ROS (the Robot Operating System — actually middleware, not an OS) is how the field solved that plumbing once, together. This chapter teaches it properly: the concepts, your first nodes line by line, the complete build-and-run workflow, ROS 1 vs ROS 2, QoS, and the big application stacks.

8.1 · What ROS actually is

Intuition — a nervous system in software

Without ROS, every robot project rewrites the same glue: serial drivers, message formats, logging, visualization, "how does the planner call the controller." ROS standardizes the glue: programs become nodes, conversations become named topics carrying typed messages, and a constellation of tools (record, replay, visualize, introspect) works on any robot speaking the protocol. The payoff is an ecosystem: a LiDAR vendor ships a driver node, a lab ships a SLAM node, you wire them together at lunch. ROS 1 (born 2007 at Willow Garage) carried the field for 15 years and reached end-of-life in May 2025; ROS 2 — a ground-up rebuild on the DDS industrial messaging standard, fixing real-time, multi-robot, security, and the single-point-of-failure master — is the present and future, in LTS releases like Humble (2022) and Jazzy (2024). Everything below is ROS 2.

8.2 · Core concepts, one by one

Definition — the seven nouns of ROS 2

Node: one process with a job ("camera_driver", "planner"). Topic: a named, typed broadcast channel ("/scan", "/cmd_vel") — publishers send, subscribers receive, neither knows the other exists. Message: the typed payload (geometry_msgs/Twist has linear and angular velocity fields). Service: request/reply for quick questions ("/spawn_entity"). Action: a long-running goal with feedback and cancellation ("navigate_to_pose" — the right tool when the answer takes 30 seconds and you want progress reports). Parameter: a named config value on a node, changeable at runtime. Launch file: a Python script that starts and wires a whole constellation at once.

Intuition — pub/sub is the load-bearing decision

Why broadcast channels instead of function calls? Decoupling. The camera node publishes images whether one node listens or five; the planner subscribes to /map without caring if the map comes from SLAM, a file, or a simulator. Swap any component without touching the others; record any conversation (ros2 bag) and replay it later — which is, notice, exactly how robot demonstration datasets for Layer 4's imitation learning get collected. The graph of nodes-and-topics is the architecture diagram, live and inspectable.

Two more pieces complete the core. TF2 is the transform system: every node publishes the frames it knows (map → odom → base_link → camera_link → gripper...) into one tree, and any node may ask "where was the gripper in map coordinates, 80 ms ago?" — Chapter 2's homogeneous transforms as a queryable, time-traveling service, and the single most useful subsystem in ROS. URDF is the robot description: an XML file of links, joints, masses, and meshes — Chapter 2's kinematic chain as data, consumed by TF, RViz, simulators, and MoveIt alike:

<robot name="two_link_arm">
  <link name="base"/>
  <link name="upper_arm"/>
  <joint name="shoulder" type="revolute">
    <parent link="base"/>  <child link="upper_arm"/>
    <axis xyz="0 0 1"/>  <limit lower="-3.14" upper="3.14" effort="10" velocity="2"/>
  </joint>
</robot>

Chapter 2, serialized: the kinematic chain as XML that every ROS tool can read.

Interactive

A live computation graph — echo topics, kill nodes, see decoupling

This is a small mobile-robot system: drivers feeding perception feeding planning feeding motors. Click /scan or /cmd_vel to watch live messages stream (that's ros2 topic echo). Click the planner node to kill it: its outputs go silent, the motor node starves — but the camera and SLAM keep humming, oblivious. Restart it and flow resumes. That graceful partial failure is pub/sub decoupling, the reason ROS systems survive their own bugs.

8.3 · Your first nodes, line by line

# talker.py — publishes a string at 2 Hz
import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from std_msgs.msg import String

class Talker(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('talker')                          # node name on the graph
        self.pub = self.create_publisher(String, '/chatter', 10)   # type, topic, queue
        self.create_timer(0.5, self.tick)                    # call tick() at 2 Hz
        self.n = 0
    def tick(self):
        msg = String(); msg.data = f'hello {self.n}'; self.n += 1
        self.pub.publish(msg)

def main():
    rclpy.init()
    rclpy.spin(Talker())      # hand the node to the executor: run callbacks forever
# listener.py — prints whatever arrives
class Listener(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('listener')
        self.create_subscription(String, '/chatter', self.on_msg, 10)
    def on_msg(self, msg):
        self.get_logger().info(f'heard: {msg.data}')

Read the shape, because every ROS node ever written has it: inherit Node → declare what you publish/subscribe/serve in __init__ → do all work in callbacks → spin. There is no main loop of your own; rclpy.spin() runs an executor that fires your callbacks as timers tick and messages arrive (single-threaded by default — a slow callback blocks the others, the first performance lesson everyone learns the hard way). The two processes never reference each other: they discover one another through the topic name and type, via DDS, automatically.

8.4 · The complete workflow, step by step

Workflow — from empty folder to running system (the loop you'll run daily)
1

Make a workspace. A workspace is just a folder with a src/:

mkdir -p ~/ros2_ws/src && cd ~/ros2_ws
2

Create a package — the unit of ROS code (a folder with manifest + build config):

cd src
ros2 pkg create my_bot --build-type ament_python --dependencies rclpy std_msgs

Drop talker.py/listener.py into my_bot/my_bot/, and register them as executables in setup.py's entry_points ('talker = my_bot.talker:main').

3

Build with colcon, then source the result. Building generates an environment; sourcing makes your packages visible:

cd ~/ros2_ws && colcon build
source install/setup.bash      # every new terminal — yes, really, every one

(Forgetting to source is the #1 beginner error; "package not found" almost always means this.)

4

Run, in two terminals:

ros2 run my_bot talker
ros2 run my_bot listener       # prints: heard: hello 0, hello 1, ...
5

Introspect — the superpower. From any third terminal, interrogate the living system:

ros2 node list                  # /talker /listener
ros2 topic list                 # /chatter ...
ros2 topic echo /chatter        # tap the wire
ros2 topic hz /chatter          # measure: ~2 Hz
ros2 bag record -a              # record EVERYTHING for replay
6

Scale up with a launch file (Python) that starts the whole constellation, parameters and remappings included:

def generate_launch_description():
    return LaunchDescription([
        Node(package='my_bot', executable='talker'),
        Node(package='my_bot', executable='listener'),
    ])

ros2 launch my_bot demo.launch.py — and from here, RViz to visualize, Gazebo to simulate, real systems being this exact loop with more nodes.

8.5 · ROS 1 vs ROS 2 — and QoS, the new dial that matters

ROS 1 (2007–2025)ROS 2 (the present)
Discoverycentral roscore master — one process dies, everything diespeer-to-peer DDS discovery — no master, no single point of failure
Transportcustom TCPROSDDS (Fast DDS / Cyclone) — an industrial standard with QoS
Real-time / embeddednot reallydesigned for it; micro-ROS reaches microcontrollers
Build / launchcatkin · XML launchcolcon · Python launch
Node lifecycle, securityad hocmanaged lifecycle states; DDS security (auth/encryption)
Definition — Quality of Service (QoS)

Every ROS 2 publisher/subscriber carries a QoS profile; the two settings you'll actually tune: reliability — reliable (retransmit until delivered) vs best-effort (drop and move on) — and durability — whether late joiners receive the last message (transient-local, perfect for maps and robot descriptions) or only new ones (volatile). The doctrine: high-rate sensor streams go best-effort (a stale camera frame is worthless — drop it, send the fresh one); commands and state changes go reliable (a dropped "stop" is a safety incident). Mismatched QoS between pub and sub means silent non-connection — the second-most-classic ROS 2 debugging hour, after forgetting to source.

8.6 · The application stacks — this layer, productionized

Three frameworks turn this book's chapters into deployable systems, and you now possess the theory under each. Nav2 is Chapter 7 industrialized for mobile robots: costmaps (C-space, rasterized, with obstacles inflated by the robot's radius), a planner server (A*/hybrid search on the costmap), a controller server (local trajectory tracking), recovery behaviors, all orchestrated by a behavior tree — talk to it through one action: navigate_to_pose. MoveIt 2 is Chapters 3–7 for arms: IK solvers, OMPL's sampling planners (RRT-Connect by default), trajectory time-parameterization, collision checking against the URDF and sensed scene. ros2_control is Chapter 5 made pluggable: a controller manager runs your PID/effort controllers at 100–1000 Hz against standardized hardware interfaces, so the same controller code drives simulation and metal. And the modern bridge: a learned policy — up to a full VLA — typically lives as one more node, subscribing to images and joint states, publishing setpoints into ros2_control, with Nav2/MoveIt as fallback and safety scaffolding. That node is what Layer 6 builds.

Chapter 09

Simulation & Sim-to-Real

Layer 4 argued that imagined experience is the only affordable kind at scale; robotics took that argument literally and built an industry of hand-crafted world models — physics simulators. They run on a numerical heartbeat with failure modes you can derive exactly, and the gap between their physics and reality is the last boss of this layer.

9.1 · Physics engines — the heartbeat and its flaw

A simulator holds state $(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q})$, computes accelerations from Chapter 5's dynamics, and advances time in discrete steps $\Delta t$ — thousands per second. The whole craft hides in how it steps. (Contact — the moment a foot strikes ground — is the genuinely hard part: forces become discontinuous and stiff, which is why simulators differ most in their contact models, and why MuJoCo's smooth contact made it the RL community's favorite. Isaac Lab's trick is throughput: thousands of environments stepping in parallel on one GPU — Layer 4's PPO needs exactly that firehose.)

Derivation — explicit Euler injects energy (exactly, provably)
1

The obvious integrator (explicit Euler) follows the local slope — Layer 1 Ch 3's tangent line, taken literally:

$$x_{t+1}=x_t+\Delta t\,v_t\qquad v_{t+1}=v_t+\Delta t\,a(x_t)$$

Taylor's theorem says the truth is $x(t+\Delta t)=x+\Delta t\,\dot x+O(\Delta t^2)$ — so each step commits an $O(\Delta t^2)$ error, accumulating to $O(\Delta t)$ over a fixed duration. Small, surely harmless?

2

Not harmless — biased. Test on the harmonic oscillator $a=-\omega^2 x$ (every joint near equilibrium looks like one) and track the energy $E\propto \omega^2x^2+v^2$. Substitute the update and expand:

$$\omega^2x_{t+1}^2+v_{t+1}^2=\omega^2(x+\Delta t\,v)^2+(v-\omega^2\Delta t\,x)^2=(1+\omega^2\Delta t^2)\big(\omega^2x^2+v^2\big)$$

(the cross terms cancel exactly). Energy multiplies by $1+\omega^2\Delta t^2$ every step — exponential growth. Your simulated pendulum doesn't just drift; it provably winds itself up until it explodes.

3

The one-character fix — semi-implicit Euler: update velocity first, then position with the new velocity. This integrator is symplectic: it conserves a slightly-wrong energy exactly, so oscillations stay bounded forever — which is why every game and robotics engine uses it (or better, like RK4's $O(\Delta t^4)$ accuracy). Two morals: timestep choices are stability choices (stiff contacts force small $\Delta t$), and "it's just numerical error" is never a complete sentence — errors have structure.

Interactive

Three integrators, one pendulum — watch the flaw you just derived

Same pendulum, same start, no friction: true motion should swing forever at constant amplitude. The coral trace (explicit Euler) grows each cycle by exactly the factor you derived; the indigo (semi-implicit) breathes but stays bounded; teal (RK4) hugs the truth. Crank $\Delta t$ and watch explicit Euler go from "subtly wrong" to airborne in seconds — and remember this picture whenever a simulation "blows up."

9.2 · The reality gap — and randomization, the blunt cure

Definition — the sim-to-real problem, and domain randomization

A policy trained in simulator dynamics $\hat f$ meets reality $f\neq\hat f$ — unmodeled friction, latency, backlash, soft contacts. This is precisely Layer 4 Ch 8's model error $\varepsilon$, with its compounding bound now charged against a physical robot. Domain randomization attacks it by refusing to commit: train across a distribution of simulators,

$$\max_\theta\;\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim p(\xi)}\;\mathbb{E}\big[\,R\mid \text{dynamics }\xi\,\big]$$

randomizing masses, frictions, latencies, motor strengths, visuals. If reality's $\xi^{\ast}$ lies inside the support of $p(\xi)$, the policy has — in effect — already practiced on the real world without knowing which day that was.

Intuition — train a thousand slightly different robots

A policy fit to one simulator overfits to its quirks — Layer 1 Ch 7's disease, with dynamics as the training set. Randomization is regularization in dynamics-space: the policy can't exploit any single simulator's lies, so it learns strategies that work across the whole family — necessarily more conservative, measurably more transferable. It is how simulation-trained locomotion first walked on real legs, and (turned up to anime levels: randomized colors, textures, lighting) how sim-trained vision transfers too. The complementary tool is system identification — measure reality, shrink $p(\xi)$ around it — spending engineering effort to buy back the performance conservatism costs.

Interactive

The gap, felt — one controller, many possible realities

The aggressive gains are superb at mass 1.0× — the world they were tuned in — and disintegrate by 2×: the reality gap as an overshooting, ringing trace. The randomization-tuned gains are slightly slower at 1.0× (the conservatism tax, visible) and composed everywhere. Slide the mass back and forth in each mode; the whole sim-to-real literature is the struggle to shrink that tax while keeping that composure.

The Robot Stack

10.1 · Putting it together — loops within loops

Loops within loops, each ~10× faster than the one above, all wired through ROS 2 topics. Slow layers think; fast layers protect.

The frequency hierarchy is the deepest design fact in robotics: each layer runs about an order of magnitude faster than the one above, and faster loops backstop slower ones. The 1 kHz controller keeps the arm stable while the 10 Hz planner thinks; the planner keeps motion sensible while the 1 Hz VLA deliberates. When Layer 6 inserts a learned policy, it slots into this hierarchy rather than replacing it — typically at 10–50 Hz, between perception and control, with the classical layers as its reflexes and its safety cage. A VLA without this stack is a brain in a jar; with it, the brain has a spine.

The whole layer, in one paragraph

The machine set the vocabulary — links, joints, configurations — and geometry fixed the workspace before any code ran. Forward kinematics was matrix composition; inverse kinematics its harder mirror, solved exactly by a triangle and generally by iteration. The Jacobian — one derivative — gave velocities, exposed singularities through its determinant, and by a one-line virtual-work duality gave forces too. Dynamics came from energy bookkeeping, and PID was derived term by term: the spring that droops, the damper that calms, the integrator that holds grudges until error dies. The Kalman filter was Bayes-with-Gaussians re-deriving its own trust each tick. Planning collapsed the robot to a point in configuration space, searched it with provably-optimal A*, escaped dimensionality with randomly grown trees, then put motion on a clock with a trapezoid. ROS 2 wired it all into a living graph of decoupled nodes you can echo, record, and kill. And simulation closed the loop back to Layer 4 — hand-built world models with derivable flaws, bridged to reality by refusing to trust any single one of them. Classical robotics is not the opposite of learned robotics; it is its skeleton.
