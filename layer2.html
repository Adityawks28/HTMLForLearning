Chapter 01

Neural Network Fundamentals

A neural network is a function. A large, flexible, parameterized function that we bend into shape by showing it data. To understand the whole, we start with its atom: the neuron.

1.1 · The artificial neuron

The biological metaphor is loose but useful. A neuron receives signals on its dendrites, sums them, and "fires" if the total exceeds a threshold. The artificial version keeps exactly this skeleton: take a vector of inputs, form a weighted sum, add a bias, and pass the result through a nonlinear activation.

Definition — the neuron

Given an input vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$, weights $\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$, and a bias $b\in\mathbb{R}$, a neuron computes a pre-activation (or logit) $z$ and an activation $a$:

$$z=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+b=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i x_i + b,\qquad a=\phi(z)$$

where $\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is a fixed nonlinear function. The weights and bias are the learnable parameters.

The weighted sum $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}$ is a projection: it measures how strongly the input aligns with the direction $\mathbf{w}$. The bias shifts the threshold. Geometrically, the set of points where $z=0$ is a hyperplane — a flat $(n{-}1)$-dimensional surface slicing the input space in two. Everything a single neuron can "decide" lives on one side of that plane or the other.

The perceptron and the limits of linearity

The earliest model, the perceptron (Rosenblatt, 1958), used a step activation: fire ($a=1$) if $z\ge 0$, else $a=0$. It can perfectly classify any dataset that is linearly separable — one where a single hyperplane cleanly divides the classes. The famous wall it hits is the XOR problem: there is no single straight line that separates the points $\{(0,0),(1,1)\}$ from $\{(0,1),(1,0)\}$. A lone neuron, no matter how its weights are tuned, cannot solve it. The escape is depth: stack neurons so that later ones operate on features computed by earlier ones.

1.2 · Layers and the multilayer perceptron

Stack neurons side by side and you get a layer; the weighted sums become a matrix–vector product. Stack layers in sequence and you get a multilayer perceptron (MLP), also called a fully-connected or dense network.

Definition — a feedforward layer

Layer $\ell$ with $m$ outputs and $n$ inputs has a weight matrix $W^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and bias $\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$. Writing the previous layer's activations as $\mathbf{a}^{(\ell-1)}$:

$$\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}=W^{(\ell)}\mathbf{a}^{(\ell-1)}+\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)},\qquad \mathbf{a}^{(\ell)}=\phi\!\big(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}\big)$$

with $\mathbf{a}^{(0)}=\mathbf{x}$ the input and $\phi$ applied element-wise. An $L$-layer network is the composition $f(\mathbf{x})=\phi^{(L)}\!\circ\cdots\circ\phi^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})$.

Why does the nonlinearity matter so much? Because without it the whole tower collapses. If every $\phi$ were the identity, then $\mathbf{a}^{(L)}=W^{(L)}\cdots W^{(1)}\mathbf{x}+(\text{const})$, and the product of matrices is just another matrix. A hundred linear layers would be exactly as expressive as one. The nonlinearity is the only reason depth buys you anything.

Intuition — why depth works

Each layer warps space. A nonlinear $\phi$ lets a layer bend, fold, and stretch its input space so that the next layer sees a representation in which the problem is easier — ideally, eventually, linearly separable. Early layers learn simple features; later layers compose them into complex ones. Depth is compositional reuse.

Theorem — universal approximation

A feedforward network with a single hidden layer containing a finite number of neurons and a non-polynomial activation can approximate any continuous function on a compact domain to arbitrary accuracy (Cybenko 1989; Hornik 1991). The theorem promises that a solution exists — it says nothing about how wide the layer must be, or whether gradient descent will find it. In practice, deep narrow networks represent many functions far more efficiently than shallow wide ones.

The sigmoid derivative deserves a quick derivation because the trick recurs everywhere:

Derivation — the sigmoid's derivative
1

Write $\sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}$ and differentiate with the chain rule:

$$\sigma'(z)=-(1+e^{-z})^{-2}\cdot(-e^{-z})=\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^{2}}$$
2

Split the fraction and recognise the pieces:

$$\sigma'(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}=\sigma(z)\cdot\big(1-\sigma(z)\big)$$
3

The derivative is expressible in the output itself. Its maximum is $0.25$ at $z=0$, and it decays toward $0$ as $|z|\to\infty$ — the seed of the vanishing gradient problem we will meet in deep and recurrent networks.

1.4 · The mathematics of loss functions

Learning needs a target to minimise: a scalar loss measuring how wrong the network's prediction $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is versus the truth $\mathbf{y}$. The choice of loss is not folklore — each standard loss is the negative log-likelihood of a specific probabilistic assumption about the data. Maximising the likelihood of what we observed is the same as minimising the loss. Let us derive the two workhorses from that single principle.

Regression — mean squared error from Gaussian noise

Derivation — MSE is Gaussian maximum likelihood
1

Assume each target is the model's prediction plus zero-mean Gaussian noise of variance $\sigma^2$: $y_i=\hat{y}_i+\varepsilon_i$, with $\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$. The likelihood of one observation is the Gaussian density:

$$p(y_i\mid\hat{y}_i)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$
2

Take the log-likelihood over $N$ independent examples (the log turns the product into a sum):

$$\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\hat{\mathbf{y}})=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2-\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$$
3

The second term is constant in the parameters. Maximising the log-likelihood is therefore minimising the sum of squared errors — which, averaged, is exactly MSE:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\hat{y}_i-y_i\big)^2$$

So "minimise squared error" literally means "assume Gaussian noise and maximise likelihood." Its gradient with respect to a prediction is beautifully simple: $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\hat{y}_i=\tfrac{2}{N}(\hat{y}_i-y_i)$ — proportional to the error itself.

Classification — cross-entropy from maximum likelihood

For classification the network outputs a probability distribution over classes via the softmax, and we score it with cross-entropy.

Definition — softmax & cross-entropy

For logits $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}$, the softmax produces a distribution; cross-entropy compares it to the one-hot truth $\mathbf{y}$:

$$\hat{y}_k=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_k=\frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{z_j}},\qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}y_k\log \hat{y}_k$$
Derivation — why this exact form, three ways
1

As maximum likelihood. The model says the probability of the true class $c$ is $\hat{y}_c$. The likelihood of the whole dataset is $\prod_i \hat{y}_{c_i}$. Maximising its log is minimising $-\sum_i\log\hat{y}_{c_i}$ — and since $\mathbf{y}$ is one-hot, $-\sum_k y_k\log\hat{y}_k=-\log\hat{y}_c$. Cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood of the correct class.

2

As KL divergence. The distance from the true distribution $\mathbf{y}$ to the predicted $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is the Kullback–Leibler divergence, which splits into entropy plus cross-entropy:

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{y}\,\|\,\hat{\mathbf{y}})=\underbrace{\sum_k y_k\log y_k}_{-\,\text{entropy of }\mathbf{y}\text{ (constant)}}-\sum_k y_k\log\hat{y}_k$$

The truth's entropy is fixed, so minimising KL divergence is exactly minimising cross-entropy.

3

Binary case. With one output probability $\hat{y}=\sigma(z)$ and label $y\in\{0,1\}$, the same logic gives binary cross-entropy:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}=-\big[y\log\hat{y}+(1-y)\log(1-\hat{y})\big]$$

Notice the penalty diverges to $+\infty$ as a confident prediction turns out wrong ($\hat{y}\to0$ when $y=1$) — cross-entropy punishes confident mistakes far more harshly than MSE would.

1.5 · Backpropagation

We can measure error and we can compute the network's output. The missing piece is: how should each of the millions of weights change to reduce the loss? The answer is the gradient $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W^{(\ell)}$ for every layer — and backpropagation is simply the chain rule, organised so that we compute all of these gradients in a single backward sweep, reusing work.

Intuition — credit assignment

A deep network is a long chain of operations. To know how the loss responds to a weight buried in layer 2, we must trace how a nudge there ripples forward through every later layer to the output. The chain rule does this, but a naive computation would repeat the same sub-products for every weight. Backprop computes one quantity per layer — the sensitivity of the loss to that layer's pre-activations — and reuses it. That turns an exponential cost into a linear one.

Define the central quantity, the error signal of layer $\ell$, as the gradient of the loss with respect to that layer's pre-activations:

$$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)}\;\equiv\;\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}}$$
Derivation — the four equations of backprop
1

Output layer error. By the chain rule, the loss depends on $\mathbf{z}^{(L)}$ through the activation $\mathbf{a}^{(L)}=\phi(\mathbf{z}^{(L)})$:

$$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{a}^{(L)}}\odot\phi'\!\big(\mathbf{z}^{(L)}\big)$$

where $\odot$ is the element-wise (Hadamard) product.

2

Recursion — propagate the error backward. The pre-activations of layer $\ell$ influence the loss only through layer $\ell{+}1$, whose pre-activations are $\mathbf{z}^{(\ell+1)}=W^{(\ell+1)}\phi(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)})+\mathbf{b}^{(\ell+1)}$. Applying the chain rule:

$$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)}=\Big(\big(W^{(\ell+1)}\big)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell+1)}\Big)\odot\phi'\!\big(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}\big)$$

The error flows backward through the transpose of the forward weight matrix — multiplication in reverse — then is gated by the local activation slope.

3

Weight gradient. Since $z^{(\ell)}_i=\sum_j W^{(\ell)}_{ij}a^{(\ell-1)}_j+b^{(\ell)}_i$, we have $\partial z^{(\ell)}_i/\partial W^{(\ell)}_{ij}=a^{(\ell-1)}_j$, so:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{(\ell)}}=\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)}\big(\mathbf{a}^{(\ell-1)}\big)^{\top}$$

an outer product: the error at the output side times the activation at the input side.

4

Bias gradient. Because $\partial z^{(\ell)}_i/\partial b^{(\ell)}_i=1$:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}}=\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)}$$

That is the entire algorithm. Run the input forward, caching every $\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}$ and $\mathbf{a}^{(\ell)}$; compute $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)}$ at the top; sweep backward with equation 2 to get every $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)}$; read off the gradients with equations 3 and 4. Modern frameworks (PyTorch, JAX) do this automatically via reverse-mode automatic differentiation, which is exactly this idea generalised to arbitrary computational graphs.

A beautiful simplification

When softmax is paired with cross-entropy — the standard classification setup — the output error collapses to something stunningly simple.

Derivation — softmax + cross-entropy gradient
1

With $\mathcal{L}=-\sum_k y_k\log\hat{y}_k$ and $\hat{y}_k=e^{z_k}/\sum_j e^{z_j}$, compute $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z_i$. First the derivative of softmax itself:

$$\frac{\partial \hat{y}_k}{\partial z_i}=\hat{y}_k\big(\mathbb{1}[k=i]-\hat{y}_i\big)$$
2

Substitute into the chain rule $\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i}=-\sum_k \dfrac{y_k}{\hat{y}_k}\dfrac{\partial \hat{y}_k}{\partial z_i}$:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i}=-\sum_k \frac{y_k}{\hat{y}_k}\,\hat{y}_k\big(\mathbb{1}[k=i]-\hat{y}_i\big)=-\sum_k y_k\big(\mathbb{1}[k=i]-\hat{y}_i\big)$$
3

Distribute and use $\sum_k y_k=1$ (one-hot truth):

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i}=-y_i+\hat{y}_i\sum_k y_k=\hat{y}_i-y_i$$
4

In vector form, the output error is just prediction minus truth:

$$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{y}$$

No activation derivative, no softmax Jacobian survives. This clean signal is one reason the softmax–cross-entropy pairing is universal in classification.

Chapter 02

Training Dynamics

We have a loss and we have its gradient. Learning is the act of using that gradient to walk the parameters downhill. How we take that walk — the step size, the memory, the bookkeeping — is the difference between a model that trains in an hour and one that never converges at all.

2.1 · Gradient descent

The loss is a scalar function of all the parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ (every weight and bias gathered into one giant vector). Its gradient $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}$ points in the direction of steepest ascent. To descend, we step the opposite way.

Definition — the gradient descent update

With learning rate $\eta>0$, each step updates every parameter:

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\eta\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)$$

Computing the gradient over the entire dataset (batch gradient descent) is accurate but slow. Using one example at a time (stochastic gradient descent, SGD) is noisy but fast. The practical compromise is the mini-batch: average the gradient over a small random subset (say 32–512 examples) each step. The noise from mini-batching is not purely a nuisance — it helps the optimizer escape sharp, brittle minima and tends to find flatter ones that generalise better.

The learning rate is the master dial

Too small and training crawls, possibly stalling in a poor region. Too large and each step overshoots the valley floor, the loss oscillates or explodes, and the parameters diverge to infinity. There is a critical rate above which descent becomes ascent — you can watch it happen in the widget below.

2.2 · Momentum, RMSProp, and Adam

Plain SGD struggles in ravines — loss surfaces that are steep in one direction and shallow in another. It bounces across the steep walls while creeping along the gentle floor. The fix is to give the update memory.

Momentum

Accumulate an exponentially-decaying running average of past gradients — a "velocity" — and step along it. Oscillations in the steep direction cancel out; consistent progress in the shallow direction accumulates.

$$\mathbf{v}_{t}=\beta\,\mathbf{v}_{t-1}+(1-\beta)\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t),\qquad \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_t-\eta\,\mathbf{v}_t$$

with $\beta\approx0.9$. Nesterov momentum sharpens this by evaluating the gradient at the look-ahead point $\boldsymbol{\theta}_t-\eta\beta\mathbf{v}_{t-1}$ rather than at $\boldsymbol{\theta}_t$.

Adaptive rates and Adam

Different parameters may need different step sizes. AdaGrad and RMSProp scale each parameter's step by a running estimate of its gradient magnitude, so rarely-updated parameters take bigger steps. Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2014) combines this idea with momentum and is the default optimizer for most deep learning today.

Definition — Adam

Maintain a first moment $\mathbf{m}$ (mean of gradients) and second moment $\mathbf{v}$ (mean of squared gradients), with $g_t=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)$:

$$\mathbf{m}_t=\beta_1\mathbf{m}_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_t,\qquad \mathbf{v}_t=\beta_2\mathbf{v}_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_t^{2}$$

Because $\mathbf{m},\mathbf{v}$ start at zero they are biased toward zero early on; correct it:

$$\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t=\frac{\mathbf{m}_t}{1-\beta_1^{\,t}},\qquad \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t=\frac{\mathbf{v}_t}{1-\beta_2^{\,t}}$$

then take the adaptive step ($\epsilon\approx10^{-8}$ guards against division by zero):

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_t-\eta\,\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t}+\epsilon}$$

Typical defaults: $\beta_1=0.9,\ \beta_2=0.999$.

Derivation — why bias correction is needed
1

Unrolling the first-moment recursion from $\mathbf{m}_0=0$ gives a weighted sum of past gradients:

$$\mathbf{m}_t=(1-\beta_1)\sum_{i=1}^{t}\beta_1^{\,t-i}g_i$$
2

If gradients were drawn from a stationary distribution with mean $\mathbb{E}[g]$, take expectations. The geometric series of weights sums to $1-\beta_1^{\,t}$:

$$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{m}_t]\approx \mathbb{E}[g]\,(1-\beta_1)\sum_{i=1}^{t}\beta_1^{\,t-i}=\mathbb{E}[g]\,(1-\beta_1^{\,t})$$
3

So $\mathbf{m}_t$ underestimates the true mean by the factor $(1-\beta_1^{\,t})$, badly so when $t$ is small (at $t=1$ with $\beta_1=0.9$ it is a $10\times$ underestimate). Dividing by $(1-\beta_1^{\,t})$ removes the bias exactly. The same argument fixes $\mathbf{v}_t$.

AdamW — decoupled weight decay

A subtle but important fix. Adding L2 regularization through the gradient interacts badly with Adam's per-parameter scaling. AdamW instead applies the weight decay directly to the parameters, separate from the adaptive step: $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_t-\eta(\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t/(\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t}+\epsilon)+\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)$. This is the standard optimizer for training Transformers.

2.3 · Weight initialization

Before the first gradient step, every weight has to start somewhere. The choice is not cosmetic. Initialize too large and activations explode as they pass through layers; too small and they shrink to zero — in both cases the gradients that come back are unusable. The goal is to keep the variance of activations (and gradients) roughly constant across layers.

Derivation — Xavier/Glorot & He initialization
1

Consider one pre-activation $z=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{in}}}w_i x_i$ with weights and inputs independent, zero-mean. The variance of a sum of independent zero-mean products is:

$$\operatorname{Var}(z)=n_{\text{in}}\operatorname{Var}(w)\operatorname{Var}(x)$$
2

To keep $\operatorname{Var}(z)=\operatorname{Var}(x)$ — signal neither growing nor shrinking — we need $n_{\text{in}}\operatorname{Var}(w)=1$, i.e. $\operatorname{Var}(w)=1/n_{\text{in}}$. Accounting for the backward pass too, Glorot averages fan-in and fan-out:

$$\operatorname{Var}(w)=\frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}}}\quad\text{(Xavier/Glorot)}$$
3

ReLU zeroes half its inputs, halving the variance. He initialization compensates with an extra factor of 2 — the right default for ReLU networks:

$$\operatorname{Var}(w)=\frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}\quad\text{(He)}$$

2.4 · Normalization layers

Even with good initialization, the distribution of each layer's inputs shifts as training progresses (training the layers below changes what the layer above sees). Normalization layers actively re-center and re-scale activations so the network trains faster and more stably.

Definition — Batch & Layer Normalization

Both compute a mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ over some set of values, standardise, then apply a learnable scale $\gamma$ and shift $\beta$:

$$\hat{x}=\frac{x-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}},\qquad y=\gamma\hat{x}+\beta$$

BatchNorm computes $\mu,\sigma^2$ across the batch dimension (per feature) — so it depends on other examples in the batch, and keeps running averages for inference. LayerNorm computes them across the feature dimension of a single example — independent of batch size, which is why it is the normalization of choice inside Transformers and RNNs.

Intuition — why it helps

Normalization smooths the loss landscape: it decouples the scale of a layer's output from the layers below, so each layer can be optimized more independently. The learnable $\gamma,\beta$ ensure the network can still represent any scale it needs — normalization restricts nothing, it only re-parameterizes.

2.5 · Regularization & gradient control

A model with millions of parameters can memorize its training data and fail on anything new — overfitting. Regularization is the family of techniques that trade a little training accuracy for much better generalization.

  • L2 / weight decay. Add $\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert^2$ to the loss. This pulls weights toward zero, preferring simpler, smoother functions. Its gradient contribution is just $\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}$.
  • L1. Penalize $\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_1$ instead; this drives many weights to exactly zero, yielding sparse models.
  • Dropout. During training, randomly zero each activation with probability $p$, then scale survivors by $1/(1-p)$ so the expected sum is unchanged (inverted dropout). Each forward pass trains a different thinned sub-network; at test time the full network behaves like an ensemble average. It is a remarkably cheap and effective regularizer.
  • Early stopping. Monitor a held-out validation loss and stop when it begins to rise, even if training loss is still falling.
  • Gradient clipping. When a gradient's norm exceeds a threshold $\tau$, rescale it: $g\leftarrow g\cdot\tau/\lVert g\rVert$. This is the standard guard against exploding gradients, especially in recurrent networks.

Underlying all of this is the bias–variance tradeoff from classical ML: too little capacity (or too much regularization) underfits — high bias; too much capacity unchecked overfits — high variance. The art of training is finding the sweet spot, and validation curves are your map.

Chapter 03

Convolutional Networks

An image has structure that a dense layer ignores: nearby pixels are related, and a cat is a cat wherever it appears. Convolutional networks bake these two facts — locality and translation — directly into the architecture.

3.1 · The convolution

A dense layer connecting a modest 224×224×3 image to a 1000-unit layer needs over 150 million weights — and it would have to re-learn what an edge looks like separately for every position. A convolutional layer instead slides a small kernel (a tiny weight matrix) across the image, computing a weighted sum at each location. The same kernel is reused everywhere — weight sharing — so it learns one position-independent feature detector with a handful of parameters.

Definition — 2D convolution (cross-correlation)

For an input $X$ and a $k\times k$ kernel $K$, the output feature map $S$ at position $(i,j)$ is:

$$S(i,j)=\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}X(i+m,\,j+n)\,K(m,n)$$

(Deep learning libraries implement cross-correlation and call it convolution; the only difference from the textbook convolution is a kernel flip, which is immaterial since $K$ is learned.)

Intuition — what a kernel "sees"

A kernel responds most strongly where the local image patch matches its pattern. A vertical-edge kernel lights up along vertical edges; a blob kernel lights up on blobs. Stack convolutional layers and the receptive field grows: early layers detect edges, the next combine edges into corners and textures, deeper ones assemble parts into objects. This hierarchy is learned, not designed.

3.2 · Stride, padding, and pooling

Three knobs control the geometry. Stride $s$ is how far the kernel jumps each step — stride 2 halves the output resolution. Padding $p$ adds a border of zeros so that output size can match input size and edge pixels aren't under-counted. Dilation spreads the kernel's taps apart to enlarge the receptive field cheaply. The output spatial size follows a single formula:

$$o=\left\lfloor\frac{n+2p-k}{s}\right\rfloor+1$$

Pooling downsamples a feature map by summarizing each small region — max pooling takes the largest value (keeping the strongest activation), average pooling the mean. Pooling grants a little translation invariance and shrinks computation. A convolutional layer typically also has many channels: it applies a whole bank of kernels, each producing one feature map, and stacks them into a multi-channel output that the next layer consumes.

3.3 · Residual networks

Stacking many convolutional layers should help — but beyond a point, very deep plain networks got worse, even on training data. The culprit was optimization: gradients degraded over dozens of layers. The residual connection (He et al., 2015) fixed this with one idea: let each block learn a correction to its input rather than a fresh transformation.

$$\mathbf{y}=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{x}$$

The extra additive path is a gradient highway: during backprop, the $+\mathbf{x}$ term passes the gradient straight through unchanged, so even hundred-layer networks train cleanly. This single trick — a skip connection that adds the input back — is so effective that it reappears, unchanged, inside every Transformer block in the next chapter.

Chapter 04

Sequence Models

Language, sensor streams, and robot trajectories are sequences: order matters and length varies. Recurrent networks process them one step at a time, carrying a memory forward. Their strengths and — more importantly — their weaknesses are exactly what motivated the Transformer.

4.1 · Recurrent neural networks

A recurrent neural network (RNN) maintains a hidden state $\mathbf{h}_t$ that it updates at each timestep from the current input and the previous state. Crucially, the same weights are applied at every step — parameter sharing across time, the temporal cousin of weight sharing in CNNs.

Definition — the vanilla RNN
$$\mathbf{h}_t=\tanh\!\big(W_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}+W_{xh}\mathbf{x}_t+\mathbf{b}\big),\qquad \mathbf{y}_t=W_{hy}\mathbf{h}_t$$

The hidden state is a running summary of everything seen so far. Training "unrolls" the recurrence across time and applies backprop to the unrolled graph — backpropagation through time (BPTT).

Derivation — why gradients vanish through time
1

The gradient of a loss at step $t$ with respect to an early state $\mathbf{h}_\tau$ must travel back through every intermediate step. By the chain rule it is a product of Jacobians:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}{\partial \mathbf{h}_\tau}=\prod_{s=\tau+1}^{t}\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_s}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{s-1}}=\prod_{s=\tau+1}^{t}\operatorname{diag}\!\big(\tanh'\big)\,W_{hh}$$
2

This is a repeated matrix product. If the largest singular value of $W_{hh}$ (times the activation slope) is below 1, the product shrinks geometrically toward zero; if above 1, it explodes. Over long sequences one of the two almost always happens.

3

Consequence: the network cannot learn dependencies more than a handful of steps apart — the gradient carrying that information has either vanished or blown up. Exploding gradients are patched with clipping; vanishing gradients need an architectural fix.

4.2 · LSTM and GRU

The Long Short-Term Memory network (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997) introduces a separate cell state $\mathbf{c}_t$ that information can travel along almost untouched, regulated by multiplicative gates — small sigmoid networks that learn what to forget, what to write, and what to read.

Definition — the LSTM cell

With $\sigma$ the sigmoid and $[\,\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_t\,]$ the concatenation of previous state and current input:

$$\mathbf{f}_t=\sigma(W_f[\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_t]+\mathbf{b}_f)\quad\text{(forget)}$$ $$\mathbf{i}_t=\sigma(W_i[\cdots]+\mathbf{b}_i),\quad \mathbf{o}_t=\sigma(W_o[\cdots]+\mathbf{b}_o)\quad\text{(input, output)}$$ $$\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t=\tanh(W_c[\cdots]+\mathbf{b}_c)\quad\text{(candidate)}$$ $$\mathbf{c}_t=\mathbf{f}_t\odot\mathbf{c}_{t-1}+\mathbf{i}_t\odot\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t,\qquad \mathbf{h}_t=\mathbf{o}_t\odot\tanh(\mathbf{c}_t)$$

The key line is the cell update. The path from $\mathbf{c}_{t-1}$ to $\mathbf{c}_t$ is additive, gated by $\mathbf{f}_t$ — when the forget gate stays near 1, the gradient flows back through the cell state nearly undiminished. That additive, gated highway is precisely what the bare RNN lacked. The GRU is a streamlined cousin with two gates instead of three and no separate cell state; it often matches the LSTM with fewer parameters.

Why this chapter is a bridge, not a destination

RNNs are inherently sequential: step $t$ cannot be computed until step $t{-}1$ finishes, so they cannot exploit modern parallel hardware over the time axis. And even LSTMs strain over very long contexts. Both problems — limited parallelism and limited range — are solved at a stroke by a mechanism that looks at the entire sequence at once and lets every position attend directly to every other. That mechanism is attention, and it is the subject of the next chapter.

Chapter 05 · The keystone

Transformers & Attention

If you learn one architecture deeply, make it this one. The Transformer powers large language models, modern vision backbones, and the vision-language-action models this curriculum builds toward. Its engine is a single, elegant operation: attention.

5.1 · Attention from first principles

Imagine each position in a sequence asking a question and every position offering an answer. Attention is a soft, differentiable dictionary lookup: a position's query is compared against every position's key; the better a key matches the query, the more that position's value contributes to the output. Nothing is sequential — every position consults every other in parallel.

Definition — queries, keys, values

From input vectors $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ ($n$ tokens, dimension $d$), three learned linear maps produce queries, keys, and values:

$$Q=XW^Q,\qquad K=XW^K,\qquad V=XW^V$$

with $W^Q,W^K\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_k}$ and $W^V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_v}$. Each row of $Q,K,V$ is one token's query, key, and value vector.

Definition — scaled dot-product attention

The attention output is a weighted average of value vectors, with weights from a softmax over scaled query–key similarities:

$$\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$

The matrix $A=\operatorname{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ holds the attention weights: $A_{ij}$ is how much token $i$ attends to token $j$, and every row sums to 1.

Read it left to right. $QK^\top$ is an $n\times n$ matrix of dot products — raw similarity scores between every query and every key. The softmax turns each row into a probability distribution over positions. Multiplying by $V$ then blends the value vectors according to those probabilities. The result, for each token, is a fresh representation gathered from wherever in the sequence is most relevant.

Why divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$?

That scaling factor is not cosmetic — without it, training breaks at high dimension. Here is the reason.

Derivation — the scaling factor
1

Suppose the components of a query $\mathbf{q}$ and key $\mathbf{k}$ are independent, mean 0, variance 1. Their dot product is $\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_k}q_i k_i$.

2

Each term $q_i k_i$ has mean 0 and variance 1 (variance of a product of independent unit-variance, zero-mean variables). Summing $d_k$ independent such terms:

$$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}]=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k})=d_k$$
3

So the dot products have standard deviation $\sqrt{d_k}$, which grows with dimension. Large scores push the softmax into its saturated corners, where one weight is ≈1 and the rest ≈0 — and there its gradient is almost zero. Learning stalls.

4

Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ rescales the scores back to unit variance, keeping the softmax in its responsive regime regardless of $d_k$. That is the whole justification.

Self-attention vs. cross-attention

When $Q,K,V$ all come from the same sequence, it is self-attention — every token relating to its own context. When the queries come from one sequence and the keys/values from another, it is cross-attention — the mechanism by which, say, a language decoder reads from an image encoder. Cross-attention is the literal seam where vision meets language inside a VLM.

5.2 · Multi-head attention

One attention operation can only express one kind of relationship at a time. Multi-head attention runs several attention operations in parallel — each with its own learned $W^Q,W^K,W^V$ — so different heads can specialise: one tracks syntax, another long-range coreference, another local adjacency.

Definition — multi-head attention

With $h$ heads, each of dimension $d_k=d/h$:

$$\text{head}_i=\operatorname{Attention}(XW_i^Q,\,XW_i^K,\,XW_i^V)$$ $$\operatorname{MultiHead}(X)=\operatorname{Concat}(\text{head}_1,\dots,\text{head}_h)\,W^O$$

The heads' outputs are concatenated and mixed by a final projection $W^O\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. Splitting into $h$ heads of size $d/h$ keeps the total computation the same as a single full-size head.

5.3 · Positional encoding

Attention is permutation-equivariant: shuffle the input tokens and the outputs shuffle the same way — the operation itself has no notion of order. For language or trajectories, order is everything. We inject it by adding a positional encoding to each token's embedding.

Definition — sinusoidal positional encoding

For position $\text{pos}$ and embedding dimension index $i$:

$$PE_{(\text{pos},2i)}=\sin\!\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10000^{2i/d}}\right),\qquad PE_{(\text{pos},2i+1)}=\cos\!\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10000^{2i/d}}\right)$$
Derivation — why sinusoids encode relative position
1

Each dimension is a sinusoid of a different frequency, from very fast (small $i$) to very slow (large $i$) — like the hands and gears of a clock, together giving every position a unique fingerprint.

2

The defining property: for any fixed offset $k$, the encoding at $\text{pos}+k$ is a linear function of the encoding at $\text{pos}$. Using the angle-addition identities,

$$\sin(\omega(\text{pos}+k))=\sin(\omega\,\text{pos})\cos(\omega k)+\cos(\omega\,\text{pos})\sin(\omega k)$$

which is a fixed rotation by angle $\omega k$ that does not depend on $\text{pos}$.

3

So the model can attend to "three tokens back" with a single learned linear map applied to positional encodings — relative position becomes easy to express. This relational view is taken further by modern schemes like RoPE (rotary embeddings), which rotate the query and key vectors themselves by position-dependent angles.

5.4 · The Transformer block

Now assemble the parts. A Transformer block wraps multi-head attention and a position-wise feedforward network, each in a residual connection with layer normalization — exactly the residual highway from Chapter 3 and the LayerNorm from Chapter 2, reused.

Definition — a (pre-norm) Transformer block
$$\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{x}+\operatorname{MultiHead}(\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf{x}))$$ $$\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{z}+\operatorname{FFN}(\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf{z})),\qquad \operatorname{FFN}(\mathbf{u})=W_2\,\phi(W_1\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{b}_1)+\mathbf{b}_2$$

The FFN expands to a wider hidden dimension (typically $4d$), applies a nonlinearity $\phi$ (usually GELU), and projects back. Attention mixes information across tokens; the FFN processes each token independently. Alternating the two, block after block, is the whole network.

Encoders, decoders, and masking

An encoder uses full self-attention — every token sees every other — ideal for understanding a complete input (the basis of BERT and of vision Transformers). A decoder generates one token at a time and must not peek at the future, so it uses masked (causal) self-attention: before the softmax, all scores above the diagonal are set to $-\infty$, forcing each position to attend only to itself and earlier positions. This is the basis of GPT-style language models.

The cost, and why it still won

Attention compares every token with every other, so its compute and memory scale as $O(n^2 d)$ in sequence length $n$ — the central bottleneck for long contexts, and the target of a huge body of "efficient attention" research. Yet Transformers dominate because they parallelize fully over the sequence, capture arbitrarily long-range relationships in one hop, and — most importantly — keep improving predictably as you scale data and parameters. That scaling behavior is what makes the large pretrained models of Layer 3 possible.

Chapter 06

Representations & Embeddings

Networks do not operate on words, pixels, or robot joints — they operate on vectors. How raw symbols become meaningful vectors, and what structure those vectors acquire, is one of the deepest ideas in the field. It is also the common language that lets a single model fuse vision, text, and action.

6.1 · Distributed representations

The naive way to feed a word to a network is a one-hot vector: a length-$V$ vector (vocabulary size $V$) that is 1 at the word's index and 0 everywhere else. It is wasteful — enormous and sparse — and worse, it is meaningless: every pair of words is equally distant, so "cat" is no closer to "dog" than to "thermodynamics".

A distributed representation instead maps each symbol to a dense, low-dimensional vector — an embedding — where meaning is spread across all coordinates and similar things land near each other. These vectors are learned: an embedding layer is simply a lookup table $E\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}$ whose rows are trained by gradient descent like any other weights.

Definition — an embedding layer

For a token with index $k$, its embedding is row $k$ of the matrix $E$:

$$\mathbf{e}_k=E_{k,:}=\text{one-hot}(k)^{\top}E\;\in\mathbb{R}^d$$

Equivalently it is the matrix product of a one-hot vector with $E$ — which is why an embedding lookup is just a (very efficient) linear layer.

Intuition — the manifold hypothesis

Real data of interest — natural images, sentences, sensor readings — does not fill its high-dimensional space uniformly. It clusters on a much lower-dimensional curved surface, a manifold. Good representation learning discovers coordinates on that manifold, so that moving smoothly in the learned space corresponds to smooth, meaningful changes in the data (a face slowly turning, a sentence's sentiment shifting).

6.2 · Word2Vec and the geometry of meaning

The distributional hypothesis states that words appearing in similar contexts have similar meanings — "you shall know a word by the company it keeps." Word2Vec (Mikolov et al., 2013) turns this into a precise learning problem. Every word $w$ gets two learned vectors: a "center" vector $\mathbf{v}_w$ and a "context" vector $\mathbf{u}_w$. In the skip-gram variant the model uses a center word to predict the words around it (CBOW does the reverse).

Definition — the skip-gram objective

The probability that word $o$ appears in the context of center word $c$ is a softmax over the whole vocabulary $V$, using the dot product of context and center vectors as the score:

$$p(o\mid c)=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{u}_o^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c)}{\sum_{w=1}^{V}\exp(\mathbf{u}_w^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c)}$$

Training maximises the log-probability of the true context words across the corpus — equivalently, minimises $-\sum\log p(o\mid c)$.

The dot product $\mathbf{u}_o^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c$ is the similarity knob: pushing it up for words that co-occur, down for those that do not, is what arranges the geometry. But that denominator sums over the entire vocabulary — hundreds of thousands of words — for every single training pair. It is hopelessly expensive. Negative sampling replaces the giant softmax with a cheap binary problem.

Derivation — negative sampling
1

Reframe: instead of "which of all words is the context?", ask "is this pair real or fake?" Label real (center, context) pairs as 1 and randomly drawn pairs as 0, and model the probability a pair is real with a sigmoid of the dot product:

$$P(\text{real}\mid c,o)=\sigma(\mathbf{u}_o^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c)$$
2

For each true pair, draw $K$ "negative" words $w_j$ from a noise distribution $P_n$ (a smoothed unigram frequency). Maximise the probability the real pair is real and the $K$ fakes are fake. Taking negative log gives the per-pair loss:

$$\mathcal{L}=-\log\sigma(\mathbf{u}_o^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c)-\sum_{j=1}^{K}\log\sigma(-\mathbf{u}_{w_j}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c)$$
3

Now each step touches only $K{+}1$ vectors instead of $V$. The gradient for the center vector is intuitive — using $\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)$, the update for the positive term is

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_c}=\big(\sigma(\mathbf{u}_o^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c)-1\big)\mathbf{u}_o+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sigma(\mathbf{u}_{w_j}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c)\,\mathbf{u}_{w_j}$$

Read it: the factor $(\sigma(\cdot)-1)$ is negative for a true pair, so the step pulls $\mathbf{v}_c$ toward $\mathbf{u}_o$; each negative term pushes it away from a random word. Co-occurring words drift together, unrelated words drift apart.

Why straight-line analogies fall out

Because similarity is measured by a dot product, the objective is satisfied when words sharing a context have aligned vectors. A relation like gender or pluralization shows up in roughly the same contexts regardless of the base word, so the displacement encoding that relation becomes a nearly constant vector. The offset "king→queen" ends up parallel to "man→woman," which is precisely why subtraction and addition of vectors recovers analogies.

The famous payoff: relationships become directions you can add and subtract.

$$\mathbf{v}_{\text{king}}-\mathbf{v}_{\text{man}}+\mathbf{v}_{\text{woman}}\;\approx\;\mathbf{v}_{\text{queen}}$$

6.3 · Transfer learning & pretrained representations

Here is the lever that makes modern AI economical. A network trained on a huge generic task learns representations in its hidden layers that are broadly useful — edge and texture detectors in vision, syntactic and semantic features in language. Transfer learning reuses those features for a new task with far less data:

  • Feature extraction: freeze the pretrained network, train only a small new head on top.
  • Fine-tuning: continue training some or all of the pretrained weights on the new task, usually at a low learning rate.
  • Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA, adapters): freeze the giant model and train only a tiny set of injected weights — the practical way to adapt billion-parameter models, and exactly how a pretrained VLM is turned into a robot policy.
Why this chapter matters for VLA

Embeddings are a universal interface. A patch of an image becomes a vector; a word becomes a vector; a robot's joint configuration becomes a vector; even a chunk of future actions can be tokenized into vectors. Once everything is a vector in a shared space, a single Transformer can attend across all of them at once. That is the architectural trick at the heart of vision-language-action models — and it only works because representation learning gives every modality a common currency.

Chapter 07

Generative Models

A discriminative model learns a boundary, $p(y\mid x)$. A generative model learns the data itself, $p(x)$ — enough to dream up new samples. This is not a side quest: the action decoder inside today's leading robot policies is a generative model, so the ideas here flow directly into the VLA chapters ahead.

7.1 · Autoencoders

The simplest generative idea is compression. An autoencoder is two networks back to back: an encoder $f$ squeezes input $\mathbf{x}$ into a low-dimensional latent code $\mathbf{z}$, and a decoder $g$ reconstructs the input from it. Train both to minimise reconstruction error:

$$\mathbf{z}=f(\mathbf{x}),\quad \hat{\mathbf{x}}=g(\mathbf{z}),\qquad \mathcal{L}=\lVert\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\rVert^2$$

The bottleneck forces the network to keep only what matters, learning a compact representation. But a plain autoencoder's latent space has holes and discontinuities — sample a random $\mathbf{z}$ and the decoder usually produces garbage. To generate, we need the latent space to be well-behaved. That is what the VAE provides.

7.2 · Variational autoencoders

A variational autoencoder (VAE) treats generation as a probabilistic model: a latent $\mathbf{z}$ is drawn from a simple prior $p(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, and a decoder network defines $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})$. We would like to maximise the data likelihood $p_\theta(\mathbf{x})=\int p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})p(\mathbf{z})\,d\mathbf{z}$ — but that integral is intractable. The way around it is one of the most important derivations in deep learning: the evidence lower bound (ELBO).

Derivation — the ELBO
1

Introduce an approximate posterior $q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})$ — the encoder, outputting a distribution over latents. Start from the log-likelihood and insert $q_\phi$:

$$\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x})=\log\int p_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,d\mathbf{z}=\log\,\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})}{q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}\right]$$
2

Apply Jensen's inequality ($\log$ is concave, so $\log\mathbb{E}[\cdot]\ge\mathbb{E}[\log\cdot]$):

$$\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x})\;\ge\;\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})}{q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}\right]\;\equiv\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}$$
3

Expand $p_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})p(\mathbf{z})$ and split the log into two interpretable terms:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\!\big[\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}}-\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\,\|\,p(\mathbf{z})\big)}_{\text{regularizer}}$$
4

Maximising the ELBO does two things at once: reconstruct the input well, and keep the encoder's latent distribution close to the prior so the space stays smooth and samplable. The KL term has a closed form when both are Gaussian, making it cheap to compute.

The reparameterization trick

We need to backpropagate through the random sampling $\mathbf{z}\sim q_\phi$. The trick: instead of sampling $\mathbf{z}$ directly, sample noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and compute $\mathbf{z}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_\phi(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_\phi(\mathbf{x})\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$. Now the randomness is an external input and the gradient flows cleanly through $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$.

7.3 · Generative adversarial networks

A GAN (Goodfellow et al., 2014) skips likelihoods entirely and stages a contest. A generator $G$ maps noise to fake samples; a discriminator $D$ tries to tell real data from fakes. They train against each other in a minimax game:

$$\min_G\max_D\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log D(\mathbf{x})]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_z}[\log(1-D(G(\mathbf{z})))]$$
Derivation — the optimal discriminator
1

For a fixed generator $G$, find the $D$ maximising the objective. Writing $p_g$ for the generator's distribution, the integrand at each point $\mathbf{x}$ is $p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})\log D+p_g(\mathbf{x})\log(1-D)$.

2

Set the derivative with respect to $D$ to zero: $\frac{p_{\text{data}}}{D}-\frac{p_g}{1-D}=0$, which solves to

$$D^*(\mathbf{x})=\frac{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})+p_g(\mathbf{x})}$$
3

Substituting $D^*$ back, the generator's objective reduces to the Jensen–Shannon divergence between $p_{\text{data}}$ and $p_g$, minimised exactly when $p_g=p_{\text{data}}$ — the generator has perfectly matched the data. In practice the game is delicate: it can oscillate, or suffer mode collapse where $G$ produces only a few sample types. GANs make razor-sharp images but are notoriously hard to train — which is part of why the field shifted toward diffusion.

7.4 · Diffusion models

Diffusion models — behind most state-of-the-art image generation and the action experts in modern robot policies — turn generation into iterative denoising. The idea is disarmingly simple: gradually destroy data by adding noise (the easy forward direction), then train a network to reverse it step by step.

Definition — the forward (noising) process

Start from clean data $\mathbf{x}_0$ and add Gaussian noise over $T$ steps with a schedule $\beta_t$:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_t;\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\,\beta_t I\big)$$
Derivation — closed form for any timestep
1

Let $\alpha_t=1-\beta_t$ and $\bar{\alpha}_t=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s$. Because the sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian, the many small noising steps compose into a single one — we can jump straight to step $t$ without iterating:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_0)=\mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_t;\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0,\,(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)I\big)$$
2

Equivalently, with $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$:

$$\mathbf{x}_t=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$$

As $t\to T$, $\bar{\alpha}_t\to0$ and $\mathbf{x}_T$ becomes pure noise. This closed form is what makes training efficient — we can sample any noise level in one shot.

3

A network $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)$ is trained to predict the noise that was added. The objective, remarkably, simplifies to a plain mean-squared error:

$$\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},t}\Big[\big\lVert\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\,t)\big\rVert^2\Big]$$
4

Sampling: start from pure noise $\mathbf{x}_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and iteratively subtract the predicted noise, denoising from $t=T$ down to $0$, to arrive at a fresh sample. Predicting the noise is equivalent to estimating the score $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x})$ — the gradient field that points toward higher-density (more realistic) regions.

Why more — and more varied — dog photos make better dogs

The reverse process can only undo noise toward images it has seen the shape of during training. Each training photo is one sample from the true distribution of "dog images," $p_{\text{data}}(\text{dog})$. The network's job is to learn the score — at any noisy point, which direction leads back toward realistic dogs. With only a handful of photos, it learns a spiky, overfit estimate: it can essentially only reproduce those few dogs, and noise denoises toward blurry averages or copies. Flow matching — the modern successor

Flow matching generalises the idea: instead of a fixed noising chain, learn a continuous velocity field that transports samples from a simple noise distribution to the data distribution along smooth paths (an ordinary differential equation). It trains more stably and samples in fewer steps than diffusion, which is why it is the action-generation method inside frontier robot policies such as π0. When you reach the VLA chapters, the "action expert" producing a robot's continuous motions is doing exactly this — flow matching over action trajectories, conditioned on what the robot sees and is told.

Chapter 08

Self-Supervised Learning

Labels are scarce and expensive; raw data is infinite and free. Self-supervised learning extracts a training signal from the structure of the data itself — and it produced CLIP, the model that taught machines to connect images and language, and the visual backbone beneath today's vision-language-action systems.

8.1 · Learning without labels

The core move is to invent a pretext task whose "labels" come for free from the data. Hide part of the input and predict it from the rest. Two landmark examples define entire modeling families:

  • Masked language modeling (BERT): blank out random words in a sentence and train the model to fill them in. Predicting the missing word forces a deep understanding of context — and the labels are simply the original words.
  • Masked image modeling (MAE): hide most patches of an image and reconstruct them. The model must learn what objects and scenes look like to inpaint the gaps.

After pretraining on a mountain of unlabeled data, the learned representations transfer (Chapter 6) to downstream tasks with only a small labeled set — the recipe behind nearly every large model today.

8.2 · Contrastive learning

A second family learns by comparison. Pull representations of related things together; push unrelated things apart. Two augmented views of the same image should map to nearby vectors (a positive pair); views of different images should map far apart (negatives). The standard objective is InfoNCE.

Definition — the InfoNCE loss

For an anchor $\mathbf{z}_i$ with one positive $\mathbf{z}_j$ among $N$ candidates, using similarity $\text{sim}$ (cosine) and temperature $\tau$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{i}=-\log\frac{\exp(\text{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{z}_j)/\tau)}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\exp(\text{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{z}_k)/\tau)}$$
Intuition — it's a softmax classifier in disguise

Look closely: InfoNCE is exactly cross-entropy over a softmax (Chapter 1) whose "classes" are the candidate items, with the true class being the positive. Minimising it means: among all candidates, assign the highest similarity to the genuine match. The temperature $\tau$ controls how sharply it concentrates on the hardest negatives. SimCLR applied this to images using aggressive data augmentations to manufacture positive pairs, and matched supervised performance with no labels.

8.3 · CLIP — bridging vision and language

CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) applies contrastive learning across modalities. Train an image encoder and a text encoder jointly on hundreds of millions of (image, caption) pairs scraped from the web. The objective: the embedding of an image and the embedding of its true caption should be close, while mismatched pairs are pushed apart — a symmetric InfoNCE over a batch, contrasting images against texts and texts against images.

The result is a shared embedding space where a picture of a dog and the words "a photo of a dog" land in nearly the same place. This unlocks zero-shot classification: to classify an image among arbitrary categories, embed the candidate labels as text, embed the image, and pick the nearest — no task-specific training required.

The thread that ties the curriculum together

CLIP-style training gives vision and language a common coordinate system. A vision-language model (your next layer) is built on exactly this: a visual encoder whose patches live in the same space the language model reasons over. A vision-language-action model adds a third axis — actions, tokenized or generated by the diffusion/flow-matching decoder of Chapter 7 — into that same shared space, so a single Transformer can read pixels, parse an instruction, and emit motor commands in one forward pass. Every idea on this page reappears there: attention fuses the modalities, embeddings are the currency, contrastive pretraining builds the perception, and a generative head produces the actions.

Chapter 09 · Where it all converges

Vision-Language-Action Models

Everything on this page was a means to an end. A vision-language-action (VLA) model is a single neural network that looks at the world through cameras, reads a natural-language instruction, and outputs motor commands for a robot. It is the most complete demonstration of why the Transformer matters — and it is built, almost entirely, from the parts you have just studied.

9.1 · How VLA descends from attention

The conceptual leap that made VLAs possible is the one from Chapter 5: attention does not care what its tokens represent. Self-attention is a general mechanism for letting a set of vectors exchange information based on content. It was invented for words, but nothing about it is linguistic. If you can turn something — an image patch, a word, a robot's joint angles, a chunk of future motion — into a vector, attention can relate it to everything else in the sequence.

The single idea behind VLA

Tokenize every modality into the same embedding space (Chapter 6), concatenate them into one sequence, and let a Transformer's attention (Chapter 5) fuse them. Vision tokens, language tokens, and action tokens sit side by side; every layer of attention lets the instruction reshape how the model reads the image, and lets the image ground what the instruction means. "Pick up the red block" works because the word "red" can attend to the red pixels. That cross-modal attention — the same operation, now spanning senses — is the VLA.

This is why VLAs arrived on the heels of large language models rather than before them. The recipe is: take a vision-language model (VLM) already pretrained on internet-scale image–text data — which gives it broad visual and semantic knowledge via exactly the contrastive and self-supervised methods of Chapter 8 — and teach it a new "language": actions. RT-2 (2023) made the point vividly by transferring web knowledge directly into robotic control, so a robot could act on concepts it had only ever read about.

9.2 · Anatomy of a VLA

Formally, a VLA is a policy $\pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}\mid o,\ell)$ that maps an observation $o$ (one or more camera images, sometimes proprioceptive state) and a language instruction $\ell$ to an action $\mathbf{a}$ — usually an action chunk, a short sequence of future control steps predicted at once for smoother motion. Three components, each a direct lift from earlier chapters:

  • The vision encoder — a Vision Transformer or CLIP-style backbone (Chapters 5, 8) cuts each image into patches and embeds them as tokens. Pretraining gave it rich, transferable visual features (Chapter 6).
  • The language model — a Transformer (Chapter 5) that reads the instruction and reasons over the fused sequence. This is where the model's "common sense" lives.
  • The action decoder — turns the fused representation into actions, in one of two ways below.

9.3 · Fast and slow: dual-system designs

A robot must react in milliseconds, but a big VLM that reasons about "the red block, behind the cup, that I was asked to move" is slow. The leading designs borrow the System 1 / System 2 idea from cognitive science: a large, slow vision-language model (System 2) sets intent at low frequency, while a small, fast policy (System 1) produces high-frequency motor commands. Figure's Helix splits a ~7B-parameter reasoning VLM from a fast action head; π0-style hierarchies and NVIDIA's GR00T N1 pair a ~2B VLM with a high-rate diffusion action expert. The constraint is real: on-device, real-time control forces the action-producing part to stay small — which is why so much VLA research is about efficiency, not just capability.

9.4 · Data, generalization, and the road on

VLAs are trained by imitation learning — behavior cloning from teleoperated demonstrations — typically on large cross-robot datasets such as Open X-Embodiment, which pools demonstrations across many robot types so one policy can transfer across embodiments. Crucially, models are often co-trained on ordinary web image–text data alongside robot data, so they retain the semantic breadth of a VLM instead of forgetting it. This is the same transfer-learning logic from Chapter 6: start from broad pretrained knowledge, then adapt with parameter-efficient fine-tuning.
