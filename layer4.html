Robotics & VLA Curriculum · Layer 4

Decision Making: learning to act

Everything so far perceives and describes. This layer adds consequences: Markov decision processes, value functions, Q-learning, the policy-gradient theorem, PPO, and the imitation-learning toolkit that actually trains robot hands. The mathematics of trial, error, and improvement.

Scroll to begin · 9 chapters

A language model predicts and is done; nothing it says comes back to change its world. An agent is different: each action alters the situation the next decision faces, and the feedback — reward — arrives late, sparse, and without explanation. This layer builds the calculus of that setting. The payoff is the missing half of the VLA story: Layer 3 built a model that can say "pick up the red block"; Layer 4 is about what it takes to do it, and to get better at doing it.

Chapter 01

The Agent Loop

Reinforcement learning begins by being honest about what acting is like: you see a situation, you choose, the world answers with a new situation and a number, and you never get to ask "what would have happened otherwise?" One formalism captures all of it.

1.1 · Acting under consequences

At each time step $t$ the agent observes a state $s_t$, picks an action $a_t$, and the environment replies with a reward $r_{t+1}$ and a next state $s_{t+1}$. Loop forever (or until a terminal state). Three things make this harder than the supervised learning of Layers 1–3:

  • No labels. Nobody says which action was correct — only a scalar score, possibly long after the fact.
  • Consequences. Actions change future states. A bad move now can poison every option later. Data is not i.i.d.; you generate your own dataset by behaving.
  • Credit assignment. The win arrives at move 40; which of the 39 earlier moves deserves the credit?
Intuition — the slot machine bolted to a chessboard

Supervised learning is studying with an answer key. Reinforcement learning is a casino where the slot machine is bolted to a chessboard: every pull both pays out and rearranges the pieces. You must learn the payouts and the rearrangements at once, from a single stream of irreversible experience. Every concept in this layer — values, exploration, policy gradients — is a coping strategy for that situation.

1.2 · Markov decision processes

Definition — MDP

A Markov decision process is the tuple $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma)$: states, actions, transition dynamics $P(s'\mid s,a)$, reward function $R(s,a)$, and a discount $\gamma\in[0,1)$. The Markov property is the load-bearing assumption:

$$P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t,\,s_{t-1},a_{t-1},\dots)=P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$$

— the present state summarizes everything the past could tell you about the future. A policy $\pi(a\mid s)$ is the agent's behavior: a distribution over actions in each state.

Intuition — the board is the memory

In chess you never need the game's move history to choose well — the current board position contains it all. That is Markov-ness: state = sufficient summary. When it fails (a robot whose camera can't see behind itself), the fix is to enrich the state — stack recent frames, add memory — until it holds again. Notice the design pressure this puts on Layers 2–3: a VLA's "state" is whatever its vision encoder distills from pixels, so perception quality literally determines whether the world looks Markov to the policy.

1.3 · Returns and the discount

Definition — return

The agent maximizes not the next reward but the return — the discounted sum of everything still to come:

$$G_t=r_{t+1}+\gamma\, r_{t+2}+\gamma^2 r_{t+3}+\cdots=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\,r_{t+k+1}$$
Derivation — why $\gamma\lt1$ tames infinity, and sets a horizon
1

With bounded rewards $|r|\le r_{\max}$, is the infinite sum even finite? Let $S=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}$. Multiply by $\gamma$ and subtract — every term cancels but the first:

$$S-\gamma S=1\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;S=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}$$

so $|G_t|\le r_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$: finite, well-defined. (Layer 1's limit arguments, earning their keep.)

2

The same number is the effective horizon: the weights $\gamma^k$ sum to $1/(1-\gamma)$, so the agent cares about roughly that many future steps. $\gamma=0.9$ → ~10 steps of foresight; $\gamma=0.99$ → ~100. The discount is an impatience dial.

3

One more reading: $\gamma$ as per-step survival probability — a future reward is worth less because you might not get there. All three readings (convergence trick, horizon, hazard) are the same geometry. Feel it in the demo.

Interactive

The impatience dial — how far into the future an agent can see

Each bar is a future reward of 1; its height is what it's worth today, $\gamma^k$. The teal marker sits at the effective horizon $1/(1-\gamma)$. At $\gamma=0.5$ the agent is a goldfish; at $0.99$ it plans a hundred steps out — and pays for it, because credit assignment over 100 steps is brutally noisy. Choosing $\gamma$ is choosing how long a story your agent can follow.

Chapter 02

Value & the Bellman Equations

The central trick of reinforcement learning is to replace "what will my total future be?" — unknowable — with a number attached to each state: its value. One recursive equation defines that number, and nearly every RL algorithm is a way of solving it.

2.1 · Value functions

Definition — state value and action value
$$V^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[\,G_t \mid s_t=s\,\big] \qquad\quad Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[\,G_t \mid s_t=s,\,a_t=a\,\big]$$

$V$ scores a situation under policy $\pi$; $Q$ scores a situation and a first move, then following $\pi$. The best achievable versions are $V^{\ast}$, $Q^{\ast}$, and an optimal policy is simply $\pi^{\ast}(s)=\arg\max_a Q^{\ast}(s,a)$ — know $Q^{\ast}$ and acting well is a lookup.

Intuition — altitude on the landscape of futures

Value is altitude: standing in state $s$, how high does the expected future pile up from here? Rewards are scattered coins; value is the smell of coins on the wind. A chess engine's evaluation function, a commuter's sense that this on-ramp is a mistake, your hunch that an argument is going badly — all are value functions: compressed summaries of futures too long to enumerate.

2.2 · The Bellman equations

Derivation — value today = reward now + discounted value tomorrow
1

Factor one $\gamma$ out of the return's tail — pure algebra on the definition:

$$G_t=r_{t+1}+\gamma\big(r_{t+2}+\gamma r_{t+3}+\cdots\big)=r_{t+1}+\gamma\,G_{t+1}$$

The infinite future telescopes into one step plus the same problem, one step later.

2

Take expectations under $\pi$ and the dynamics (Layer 1's tower of expectations):

$$V^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi,\;s'\sim P}\big[\,R(s,a)+\gamma\,V^{\pi}(s')\,\big]$$

— the Bellman expectation equation. A self-consistency condition: each state's value must equal the average of (immediate reward + discounted value of where you land).

3

For the best behavior, choose the maximizing action instead of averaging over $\pi$:

$$V^{\ast}(s)=\max_{a}\;\mathbb{E}_{s'}\big[\,R(s,a)+\gamma\,V^{\ast}(s')\,\big] \qquad Q^{\ast}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{s'}\big[\,R(s,a)+\gamma\,\max_{a'}Q^{\ast}(s',a')\,\big]$$

the Bellman optimality equations. One unknown function, defined in terms of itself — and that recursion is an instruction: iterate it. Start with any guess $V_0$, apply the right-hand side repeatedly ($V_{k+1}=\mathcal{T}V_k$), and the iterates converge — the backup is a $\gamma$-contraction, each sweep shrinking the worst-case error by $\gamma$. Watch exactly this in the demo: value flows backward from the reward like heat.

Interactive

Value iteration — watch value ripple backward from the goal

The grid is a tiny world: a goal worth $+1$, a pit worth $-1$, walls in gray. Each sweep applies the Bellman backup once to every cell. Sweep 1: only the goal's neighbors learn anything. Sweep 2: their neighbors. Value propagates outward at one step per sweep — the future leaking backward into the present — and the arrows (the greedy policy) organize themselves into a route long before the numbers converge. Lower $\gamma$ and watch the glow shrink: a short-sighted agent literally cannot see the goal from far away.

Where you'll meet this again

Layer 1's fixed-point thinking and Layer 2's recursive structures were rehearsals for this equation. Everything in the next chapter — TD learning, Q-learning, and by extension the value heads inside modern robot policies — is the Bellman backup, applied with progressively less knowledge of the world.

Chapter 03

Learning from Experience

Value iteration cheated: it used the dynamics $P$ — a map of the world. A real agent has no map; it has only the road. This chapter removes the map, one assumption at a time, and arrives at the first algorithm that learns to act purely from lived experience.

3.1 · Temporal-difference learning

Derivation — the TD update from a sampled Bellman backup
1

The Bellman equation says $V(s)$ should equal $\mathbb{E}[r+\gamma V(s')]$. Without $P$, we can't compute the expectation — but we can sample it: live one transition $(s,a,r,s')$ and use $r+\gamma V(s')$ as a one-sample estimate of the target (Layer 1, Ch 5: a sample is an unbiased stand-in for its mean).

2

Nudge the current guess toward the sampled target — Layer 1's gradient step on a squared error, with step size $\alpha$:

$$V(s)\;\leftarrow\;V(s)+\alpha\,\underbrace{\big[\,r+\gamma V(s')-V(s)\,\big]}_{\delta,\ \text{the TD error}}$$

The bracket $\delta$ is the TD error: surprise — the gap between what just happened (plus the value of where you landed) and what you believed.

3

Note what we did: the target $r+\gamma V(s')$ contains our own current guess $V(s')$. We are updating a guess toward a guess — bootstrapping. The alternative (Monte Carlo) waits for the episode to end and uses the true return $G_t$: unbiased but high-variance and slow. TD trades a little bias for a lot of variance reduction and learns during the episode. (Dopamine neurons in the brain famously fire in proportion to a TD-error-like signal — this equation is one of machine learning's great convergences with neuroscience.)

Intuition — revising your ETA mid-journey

You leave work guessing "home in 30 minutes." Ten minutes in, traffic is light; you now guess 15 more. Monte Carlo says: wait until you're home, then update the original guess with the actual total. TD says: update right now — your 10-minutes-in estimate is already better information than your at-the-desk estimate. Propagating corrections from better guesses to earlier guesses, while still driving, is bootstrapping. It is how value information travels backward through time without waiting for endings.

3.2 · Q-learning — and an agent you can train

Definition — Q-learning

Apply the same sampled-backup idea to the optimality equation for $Q$ (so no model and no fixed policy is needed — the $\max$ supplies the "best behavior" part):

$$Q(s,a)\;\leftarrow\;Q(s,a)+\alpha\Big[\,r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')-Q(s,a)\,\Big]$$

Act however you like (typically ε-greedy: best-known action with probability $1-\varepsilon$, random otherwise), and the table still converges to $Q^{\ast}$ under mild conditions — Q-learning is off-policy: it learns the optimal answer while behaving sub-optimally enough to keep exploring.

The dial between them is the subject of the next chapter.

Where you'll meet this again

Replace the table with a neural network and you have DQN — deep RL's 2013 breakthrough (Atari from pixels). The bootstrap target then needs stabilizing with a slowly-updated target network: precisely Layer 3's EMA-teacher trick (DINO, Ch 2) — keep a calmer copy of yourself to learn from, or the bootstrap chases its own tail.

Chapter 04

Explore vs. Exploit

Strip away states and transitions until only one decision remains, repeated: which slot machine do I pull? This is the multi-armed bandit — the exploration problem in its purest form, small enough to reason about exactly and important enough that its answer shapes every RL system above it.

4.1 · Bandits and the price of not knowing

Definition — the bandit problem and regret

$K$ arms; arm $a$ pays out with unknown mean $\mu_a$. At each pull choose an arm, observe a sample. Performance is measured by regret — what you lost to ignorance:

$$\text{Regret}(T)=T\,\mu^{\ast}-\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{a_t}\Big],\qquad \mu^{\ast}=\max_a \mu_a$$

Always exploiting a wrong favorite → regret grows linearly forever. Good algorithms achieve logarithmic regret: mistakes become vanishingly rare.

Intuition — the restaurant problem

You know a reliably good restaurant. A new one opened. Every dinner is one pull: exploit the known 8/10, or explore the unknown that might be a 9 — or a 4? Order your favorite forever and you may die never knowing the best restaurant in town was next door; try a new place nightly and you eat a lot of bad dinners. Every learning agent faces this — including a robot deciding whether to attempt a grasp the familiar way or a promising new way, and including the ε dial you just turned in the Q-learning demo.

4.2 · Optimism in the face of uncertainty

Derivation — the UCB rule from a confidence interval
1

After $n_a$ pulls, the sample mean $\hat\mu_a$ estimates $\mu_a$ — with error bars. Hoeffding's inequality (the quantitative cousin of Layer 1's CLT) bounds the surprise: $P\big(\mu_a>\hat\mu_a+u\big)\le e^{-2 n_a u^2}$. Demand this failure probability shrink like $1/t$ (so total lifetime failures stay bounded) and solve for $u$:

$$e^{-2n_a u^2}=t^{-c}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;u=\sqrt{\frac{c\,\ln t}{2\,n_a}}$$
2

So with high confidence the truth lies below $\hat\mu_a+u$. The UCB rule: act as if every arm is as good as it could plausibly be —

$$a_t=\arg\max_a\;\hat\mu_a+\sqrt{\frac{2\ln t}{n_a}}$$
3

Read the two terms as a built-in conscience: the first votes for known quality (exploit), the second is large exactly for under-tried arms (explore) and shrinks automatically as evidence accumulates — no ε to hand-tune. Either the optimistic bet pays off (great) or it corrects your error bars (also great). Optimism converts uncertainty itself into the exploration signal, and yields the logarithmic regret ε-greedy can't.

Interactive

Then race the strategies on fresh machines and watch the regret curves: ε-greedy keeps paying a constant exploration tax (linear regret, forever), while UCB's curve bends flat as its confidence bounds tighten. Optimism, with receipts.

Chapter 05

Policy Gradients

Q-learning learns values and reads the policy off them with an argmax. But a robot arm's action is seven continuous joint torques — you cannot argmax over a continuum at 50 Hz. The fix is the most direct idea in RL: parameterize the policy itself and ascend the gradient of expected return. The derivation is three honest steps, and it powers everything from robot locomotion to RLHF.

5.1 · Why learn the policy itself

Definition — the policy-optimization objective

A stochastic policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ (for a robot: e.g. a Gaussian over torques whose mean and spread a network outputs — Layer 2 machinery). Maximize expected return over trajectories $\tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,\dots)$:

$$J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\big[R(\tau)\big],\qquad R(\tau)=\sum_t \gamma^t r_{t+1}$$

The puzzle: $\theta$ affects $J$ only by reshaping a distribution we sample from. The reward function may be a black box (a success detector, a human thumbs-up). How can we differentiate through that?

5.2 · REINFORCE, derived

Derivation — the log-derivative trick
1

Write the expectation as an integral and push the gradient inside:

$$\nabla_\theta J=\nabla_\theta\!\int\! P(\tau;\theta)\,R(\tau)\,d\tau=\int \nabla_\theta P(\tau;\theta)\,R(\tau)\,d\tau$$

Stuck: $\nabla P$ is not an expectation — we can't estimate it by sampling.

2

The trick (one line of Layer 1 calculus, $\nabla\log f=\nabla f/f$, rearranged): $\;\nabla_\theta P=P\,\nabla_\theta\log P$. Substitute — and an expectation reappears:

$$\nabla_\theta J=\int P(\tau;\theta)\,\nabla_\theta\log P(\tau;\theta)\,R(\tau)\,d\tau=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\big[\,R(\tau)\,\nabla_\theta\log P(\tau;\theta)\,\big]$$
3

Expand $\log P(\tau;\theta)=\log p(s_0)+\sum_t\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)+\sum_t\log P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$. The dynamics terms don't contain $\theta$ — their gradient vanishes. The world's physics drops out of the gradient:

$$\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\,R(\tau)\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,\Big]$$

Estimate by sampling rollouts; ascend. That is REINFORCE. Read it aloud: make the actions of high-reward trajectories more probable, of low-reward ones less. Trial, error, and reinforcement — now a theorem.

Intuition — evolution at the speed of gradients

REINFORCE never asks why a trajectory scored well — it has no model of the world, no labels, no critique. It simply breeds: behaviors correlated with reward get amplified, the rest fade. Note the price of such generality: a whole trajectory's worth of decisions shares one scalar grade, so the gradient estimate is extremely noisy. The rest of policy-gradient research — baselines, critics, PPO — is a war on that variance.

5.3 · Baselines — grading on a curve

Derivation — subtracting a baseline changes nothing (except everything)
1

Claim: replacing $R(\tau)$ with $R(\tau)-b$ for any action-independent $b$ leaves the gradient's expectation unchanged. The cross-term is:

$$\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta}\big[\,b\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,\big]=b\sum_a \pi_\theta\,\frac{\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta}{\pi_\theta}=b\,\nabla_\theta\underbrace{\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}_{=\,1}=0$$

Probabilities sum to one; the gradient of a constant is zero. Unbiased.

2

But the variance changes enormously. With $b\approx$ average return, good trajectories get a positive push and bad ones a negative push — instead of "everything gets reinforced, winners slightly more." The natural choice is $b=V(s)$, making the learning signal the advantage:

$$A(s,a)=Q(s,a)-V(s)$$

— "how much better than typical for this state was this action?" Grading on a curve.

3

Learning $V$ with a second network (trained by TD, Ch 3) gives the actor–critic family: the actor $\pi_\theta$ proposes, the critic $V_\phi$ grades, each improving the other's signal. Every serious policy-gradient system — including the PPO inside RLHF — is an actor–critic.

Chapter 06

From REINFORCE to PPO

REINFORCE has a fatal workflow problem: every gradient step needs fresh rollouts (the expectation is under the current policy), and in robotics a rollout costs real time on real hardware. We want to squeeze many updates from each batch of experience — without the policy walking off a cliff. The fix, PPO, is the default policy optimizer of the modern era, and you have already used it: it trained the RLHF models of Layer 3.

6.1 · Importance ratios — recycling old experience

Derivation — evaluating a new policy on old data
1

The general identity (one line, from Layer 1's expectation definition): for distributions $q$ (data source) and $p$ (target),

$$\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\big[f(x)\big]=\sum_x q(x)\,\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\Big[\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,f(x)\Big]$$

Samples from the wrong distribution can estimate the right expectation — if each sample is reweighted by how much more (or less) the target distribution likes it.

2

Apply with $q=\pi_{\text{old}}$ (the policy that collected the data) and $p=\pi_\theta$ (the one we're improving). Per action, the weight is the importance ratio, and the surrogate objective becomes:

$$r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)},\qquad L(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_t\big[\,r_t(\theta)\,A_t\,\big]$$

At $\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}$ all ratios are 1 and the gradient matches REINFORCE-with-baseline exactly — but now we may take several steps on the same batch.

3

The catch: the reweighting is only trustworthy while $\pi_\theta$ stays close to $\pi_{\text{old}}$. Let them drift apart and the ratios explode — a few wildly-weighted samples hijack the update, the policy lunges, performance collapses, and (unlike supervised learning) the broken policy now collects the next dataset. RL failures compound.

6.2 · The clipped objective

Definition — PPO-Clip
$$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_t\Big[\min\Big(r_t(\theta)\,A_t,\;\operatorname{clip}\big(r_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\big)\,A_t\Big)\Big]$$

with $\epsilon\approx0.2$. Outside the trust band $[1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon]$, the objective goes flat — and a flat objective has zero gradient: no incentive to push further.

Derivation — reading the clip, case by case
1

Good action ($A_t>0$): the objective rewards raising $r_t$ — but the clip caps the reward at $(1+\epsilon)A_t$. Beyond a ${+}\epsilon$ probability increase, the gradient is exactly zero: "yes, do this more — up to 20% more, then stop."

2

Bad action ($A_t\lt0$): symmetric: suppress it, but the min/clip pair zeroes the gradient once $r_t$ falls below $1-\epsilon$. No annihilating an action's probability on one batch's say-so.

3

The $\min$ makes the objective a pessimistic lower bound on the true surrogate — clipping only ever removes incentive, never adds it. The result: many safe epochs per batch of expensive experience. PPO is not the cleverest optimizer; it is the one that fails least dramatically — which, in a field where the policy curates its own future data, is the crown. (Layer 3's RLHF objective — reward minus a KL leash — is this same instinct; PPO enforces it per-update, mechanically.)

Interactive

The trust region you can see — PPO's clip, drawn

Horizontal axis: the ratio $r$ (how much the update has already changed this action's probability). Dashed: the naive objective $rA$, promising unbounded reward for unbounded change. Solid indigo: PPO's clipped version — identical near $r=1$, then flat (shaded) where the gradient dies. Flip $A$ negative and watch the geometry mirror. Where you'll meet this again

PPO fine-tuned the RLHF assistants of Layer 3 and trains locomotion policies for real quadrupeds and humanoids (usually in simulation — Layer 5's sim-to-real problem). And the advantage function returns in Layer 6: several VLA post-training recipes weight imitation by advantage estimates — "clone the demos, but clone the good ones harder."

Chapter 07

Imitation Learning

There is an easier way to teach a robot than reward and exploration: show it. Imitation learning looks like a humble return to Layer 1's supervised learning — and it is, which is why it works so well. But acting on your own predictions breaks supervised learning's core assumption in a precise, quantifiable way, and that breakage shapes how every modern robot policy is trained.

7.1 · Behavior cloning — supervised learning, again

Definition — behavior cloning (BC)

Collect expert demonstrations $\{(s_i,a_i)\}$ — for robots, typically teleoperation: a human drives the arm, and (observation, action) pairs are logged. Fit a policy by maximum likelihood:

$$\hat\theta=\arg\max_\theta\sum_i \log \pi_\theta(a_i\mid s_i)$$

This is exactly Layer 1's supervised template and Layer 3's next-token objective — with motor commands as the vocabulary. No reward function, no exploration, no Bellman: just "predict what the expert did here."

Intuition — the student driver

BC is a student watching the instructor drive and memorizing situation → response. The catch reveals itself the first time the student drives alone: the instructor never drifted toward the shoulder, so the tape contains no examples of shoulders — or of recovering from them. One small mistake and the student is somewhere the lesson never covered, where their behavior is undefined, which produces a bigger mistake, which leads somewhere stranger still. The errors do not add; they compound.

7.2 · Compounding errors, quantified

Derivation — why BC's cost grows like $T^2$, not $T$
1

Suppose the cloned policy errs with small probability $\varepsilon$ per step on states the expert visits — that is all supervised training can promise, because that is the only distribution it ever saw (Layer 1, Ch 7: guarantees hold on the training distribution).

2

Run the policy for $T$ steps. If the first error occurs at step $t$, the agent leaves the expert's state distribution — and off-distribution, the model has no guarantee: assume worst-case cost 1 for each of the remaining $T-t$ steps. The expected total cost is then bounded by

$$\sum_{t=1}^{T}\varepsilon\,(T-t)\;\le\;\varepsilon\,\frac{T^2}{2}=O(\varepsilon T^{2})$$
3

Supervised learning's usual bill is $O(\varepsilon T)$ — errors add. Acting makes it $O(\varepsilon T^{2})$ — errors breed, because each one shifts the distribution the next prediction faces (Ross & Bagnell, 2010). This single quadratic is the theoretical heart of robot learning: it says a 99%-accurate imitator is not "almost perfect," it is a time bomb on long tasks, and it tells us exactly what to fix — the distribution, not just the accuracy.

Interactive

Watch the drift — a cloned driver leaves the training distribution

The expert line hugs the road's center; the shaded band is the training distribution. In BC mode, small steering noise eventually nudges a rollout outside the band — where the policy has never been — and its noise triples: watch trajectories that survive 200 steps suddenly unravel. Switch to DAgger-style data (corrections recorded off-center) and the same noise gets caught and steered home: bounded error, $O(\varepsilon T)$. The difference between the modes is not a smarter model. It is which states appear in the dataset.

7.3 · DAgger, and the action-chunking trick

Definition — DAgger (Dataset Aggregation)

Iterate: (1) roll out the current policy; (2) have the expert label the states it actually visited — including its mistakes — with the correct action; (3) aggregate into the dataset and retrain. The training distribution chases the policy's own distribution, the off-distribution cliff disappears, and the regret bound drops to $O(\varepsilon T)$. The instructor grabs the wheel, and the grab is recorded.

DAgger needs an expert on call, which is expensive — so practical systems attack the same quadratic from a second angle: make fewer decisions. If each decision point is an opportunity to inject error, predict a chunk of the next $k$ actions at once (ACT and the ALOHA system do exactly this; diffusion policies and the flow-matching action heads of Layer 2's VLAs generate whole short trajectories per inference). The error-injection rate drops from every step to every $k$ steps:

Interactive

Action chunking — fewer decisions, less compounding

Eight rollouts trace a target motion (a reach-and-place arc), each decision injecting a small heading error. At $k{=}1$ — a fresh decision (and a fresh error) every step — the bundle frays into a random walk. At $k{=}8$ the same per-decision error budget buys an 8× smoother bundle: drift accumulates per decision, not per step. The cost is reactivity: a chunked policy is briefly blind between decisions, which is why real systems blend overlapping chunks. This dial — replan rate vs. stability — is set in every VLA you'll meet in Layer 6.

Where you'll meet this again

Teleoperated demonstrations + behavior cloning (with chunking) is the workhorse that trains today's manipulation policies — it is how the π0 and ACT lineages of Layer 2 are actually fed. The compounding-error analysis explains the field's data obsession (Open X-Embodiment), and DAgger's "label where the policy goes" idea returns whenever robot fleets log interventions by human supervisors.

Chapter 08

World Models

Everything so far learned from experience that actually happened — and real experience is the most expensive substance in robotics. There is a third paradigm beside trial-and-error and imitation: learn a model of how the world works, then practice inside it. Plan in it, dream in it, generate data from it. This is model-based RL, lately rebranded under a grander name it has earned: world models.

8.1 · Learning from imagination

Definition — dynamics model, and model-based RL

A world model (dynamics model) is a learned approximation of the environment's transition and reward structure:

$$\hat f_\phi:\;(s_t,a_t)\;\longmapsto\;\hat s_{t+1},\;\hat r_{t+1}$$

fit by supervised learning (Layer 1's template!) on logged transitions $(s,a,r,s')$. Model-based RL uses $\hat f$ to manufacture experience: roll out imagined trajectories and feed them to any algorithm from Chapters 3–6 — or plan through the model directly. Note the beautiful inversion: prediction error here needs no labels and no reward; the next timestep grades you for free (the same self-supervision trick as Layer 3).

Intuition — crash in your head, not in the car

You do not learn to drive by sampling crashes; you carry a physics engine in your skull and rehearse there. A chess player "seeing" five moves ahead is rolling out a learned model of the board. Model-free RL learns from scar tissue; model-based RL learns from daydreams — and daydreams are free, parallel, and resettable. The entire economic argument for world models in robotics is in that sentence: real robot-hours are scarce and breakable, imagined robot-hours cost GPU electricity.

Derivation — why imagination buys sample efficiency (the Dyna argument)
1

Recall how tabular Q-learning propagates information (Ch 3): each real transition performs one backup, pushing value one step backward along the path actually walked. Reward $d$ steps from the start needs value to hop backward $d$ times — many real visits.

2

Dyna (Sutton, 1990): after every real step, store the transition in a memory $\mathcal{M}$, then perform $n$ extra backups on transitions replayed from $\mathcal{M}$. Value now hops backward up to $n{+}1$ times per real step — the propagation that needed many episodes happens between footsteps:

$$\text{real experience: }1\text{ backup/step}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\text{Dyna: }1+n\text{ backups/step}$$
3

Same equations, same convergence target $Q^{\ast}$ — only the real-step bill shrinks, often by an order of magnitude. (Biology appears to agree: rodent hippocampi replay compressed maze trajectories during rest, and learning suffers if the replay is disrupted — brains seem to run something like Dyna at night.) Measure the speedup yourself below.

8.2 · Latent world models — dreaming in representation space

Definition — the latent world-model recipe (World Models 2018, Dreamer lineage)

For pixel observations, don't model pixels. Three learned parts:

$$\underbrace{z_t=e_\phi(o_t)}_{\text{encoder}}\qquad \underbrace{\hat z_{t+1}=g_\phi(z_t,a_t)}_{\text{latent dynamics}}\qquad \underbrace{\hat r_{t+1}=r_\phi(z_t,a_t)}_{\text{reward head}}$$

then train the policy (an actor–critic, Ch 5) entirely on imagined latent rollouts — thousands of dream-trajectories per second, no renderer, no robot. DreamerV3 solves dozens of domains this way with one set of hyperparameters; "learning inside the dream" is now a standard tool.

Intuition — a map is judged by the journeys it supports

Why latent? Predicting pixels wastes capacity on leaves and clouds that don't matter for action — and Layer 2/3 already taught encoders to keep what matters. MuZero pushes the logic to its endpoint: its "model" never reconstructs observations at all, predicting only the three things planning consumes — reward, value, policy. A subway map omits every street and is the better map for it. The quality bar for a world model is not photorealism; it is whether decisions made inside it transfer outside it.

Derivation — the compounding-error bound (imagination's speed limit)
1

Let the model's one-step prediction error be $\varepsilon$, and suppose errors get amplified by at most a factor $L$ per step as they feed forward (the dynamics' sensitivity — a Lipschitz constant; eigenvalues again, Layer 1 Ch 2). The error after $t$ imagined steps obeys the recursion:

$$e_{t+1}\;\le\;L\,e_t+\varepsilon$$

— yesterday's error, amplified, plus a fresh mistake.

2

Unroll it (the geometric series of Chapter 1, third appearance in this layer):

$$e_T\;\le\;\varepsilon\,(1+L+L^2+\cdots+L^{T-1})=\varepsilon\,\frac{L^{T}-1}{L-1}$$

If $L\le1$ (stable dynamics): error grows at most linearly, $e_T\le\varepsilon T$. If $L>1$ (sensitive dynamics): exponential divergence — the weather-forecast curse: tomorrow accurate, ten days out fiction.

3

This is Chapter 7's compounding disease wearing a new costume — there the policy's errors fed back through the world; here the model's errors feed back through themselves. Same arithmetic, same prescriptions: keep imagined horizons short, blend imagined rollouts with learned value bootstraps for the far future (exactly what Dreamer does), and — the strongest medicine — keep re-anchoring to reality. That medicine has a name, and it's next.

8.3 · Planning with an imperfect model — MPC

Definition — model predictive control (receding-horizon planning)

At each real step: (1) from the current real state, search the model for the best $H$-step action sequence, $\max_{a_{0:H-1}}\sum_t \gamma^t\hat r_t$; (2) execute only the first action(s); (3) observe where you actually are, and replan. Imagination proposes; reality vetoes, $H$ steps at a time.

Intuition — walking across town with your eyes open

An open-loop plan is crossing town blindfolded after memorizing the route: every small model error is carried forever, and by the derivation above it compounds. MPC is ordinary walking: a rough plan to the corner, executed while watching, re-aimed continuously. Feedback resets the error clock — between replans the error grows for at most $H$ steps, then reality zeroes it. The demo lets you turn one dial from pure MPC ($H{=}1$) to pure blind faith and watch the bound come true.

Where you'll meet this again — and where this is all going

Layer 5's simulators are hand-built world models, and the sim-to-real gap is exactly the $\varepsilon$ of our bound. The frontier is learned simulators: action-conditioned video world models (the Genie line; NVIDIA's Cosmos world foundation models for physical AI) that dream photorealistic robot experience for policies to train on, and representation-space predictors (the JEPA family) that bet, like MuZero, that dreaming in latents beats dreaming in pixels. The converging recipe for Layer 6 is already visible: a VLA for acting, a world model for rehearsing, imitation for grounding, RL for surpassing.

Chapter 09

Toward Robot Policies

Three toolkits now sit on the bench: reinforcement (learn from consequences, discover the new, needs exploration), imitation (learn from demonstration, safe and data-efficient, capped at the teacher), and world models (learn the world itself, rehearse for free, limited by model error). Real robot learning is the art of mixing them — under constraints a gridworld never had.

8.1 · Why robots can't just explore

The Q-learner in Chapter 3 fell off the cliff ten thousand times and reset, free of charge. A physical robot exploring torque-space breaks wrists — its own or the dishwasher's. Three constraints define the real setting:

  • Exploration is expensive and dangerous — so most learning must come from demonstrations, simulation, or logged data.
  • Reward is hard to specify. "Fold the laundry" has no reward function anyone can write; mis-specified proxies get gamed (Layer 3's reward-hacking, with hardware). Demonstrations sidestep this: the data is the objective.
  • Offline learning hits a wall: train value functions on a fixed dataset and the $\max_{a'}$ in the Bellman backup gleefully selects actions the dataset never contains — extrapolation error, the off-distribution disease of Ch 7 wearing a value-function costume. Offline-RL methods (conservative value estimates, behavior-constrained policies) all amount to stay near the data — PPO's trust region, DAgger's distribution-matching, and Layer 3's KL leash, one instinct in many notations.

8.2 · The modern recipe

Definition — how a robot policy is actually trained (2026 edition)

1. Pretrain the perception-language backbone on internet data (Layers 2–3). 2. Imitate: behavior-clone large teleoperation corpora with action chunking — the safe, scalable core. 3. Refine: where a success signal exists, push past the teacher — RL in simulation (the standard route for locomotion), advantage-weighted imitation, or human-preference fine-tuning à la RLHF. 4. Multiply the data: world models — simulators hand-built or learned (Ch 8) — manufacture the experience reality is too slow and too breakable to provide. Imitation supplies competence; reinforcement supplies improvement; imagination supplies scale.

The whole layer, in one paragraph

Acting was formalized as an MDP, with the discount taming infinite futures into a horizon. Value compressed the future into a number, and the Bellman equation — value now = reward + discounted value next — turned it into something solvable, then learnable from raw experience (TD's surprise signal, Q-learning's live agent). Exploration got its own theory: regret, and optimism as the principled cure. Policy gradients differentiated straight through "sample, then score" — the physics dropped out of the gradient — and PPO fenced the updates so recycled experience couldn't walk the policy off a cliff. Imitation brought it home: cloning is just Layer 1's supervised learning, its quadratic compounding-error flaw is provable, and the fixes — corrections, chunking, staying near the data — are the design rules of every real robot policy. And world models closed the circle: learn the world itself, practice in the dream (Dyna, Dreamer), respect imagination's speed limit — the compounding-error bound, again — and let replanning's feedback loop forgive what the model gets wrong. One thread ran through everything: an agent curates its own dataset by behaving, so all stability in this field is some way of respecting the distribution you trained on.

Where this goes next
