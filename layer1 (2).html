Foundations: the mathematics of learning

Vectors, matrices, derivatives, probability, and the first learning algorithms — every idea that deep learning is built on, developed from scratch with pictures you can touch. No prerequisites beyond high-school algebra and curiosity.

Scroll to begin · 8 chapters

Deep learning has a reputation for difficulty, but its mathematical core is a small set of ideas used over and over: data is vectors, models are transformations, learning is calculus, uncertainty is probability. This layer builds each one carefully. If Layer 2 is the engine, this is the metallurgy — and knowing it is the difference between memorizing formulas and seeing why they could not be otherwise.

How to read this

Blue panels are definitions — the precise statement of an idea. Green panels build intuition — read them even if you skip the math. Black-edged panels are step-by-step derivations; read them with a pen. Coral controls are interactive — every coral element is something you can drag, toggle, or run. Amber panels point forward: where you will meet this idea again in Layer 2 and beyond.

Chapter 01

Vectors & Spaces

Everything a learning machine touches — an image, a sentence, a robot's joint angles, the word "dog" — must first become a list of numbers. That list is a vector. Linear algebra is the art of doing useful things to those lists, and it is the native language of every chapter that follows.

1.1 · What a vector is

A vector is two pictures of the same object, and fluency means switching between them without noticing:

  • The data picture: a vector is an ordered list of numbers — a row in a spreadsheet. A 3-vector $\mathbf{x}=(x_1,x_2,x_3)$ might be (height, weight, age).
  • The geometry picture: a vector is an arrow from the origin to a point in space. Each number is a coordinate along one axis.
Definition — vectors and the operations they obey

A vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is an ordered tuple of $n$ real numbers. Two operations define the whole subject:

$$\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}=(x_1+y_1,\dots,x_n+y_n),\qquad c\,\mathbf{x}=(cx_1,\dots,cx_n)$$

Addition places arrows tip-to-tail; scaling stretches an arrow without turning it. Any combination $c_1\mathbf{x}+c_2\mathbf{y}$ built from these is a linear combination — the single most reused construction in machine learning.

Intuition — a vector is a recipe

Think of the standard basis vectors as ingredients: $\mathbf{e}_1$ is "one step east," $\mathbf{e}_2$ "one step north." The vector $(3,2)$ is a recipe: three parts east, two parts north. Linear combinations are just mixing recipes. When Layer 2 computes a weighted sum $\sum_i w_i x_i$ inside a neuron, or blends value vectors inside attention, it is mixing recipes — nothing more exotic than that. Data lives in space; learning rearranges the space.

The number of entries, $n$, is the dimension. Real data is high-dimensional: a small 224×224 photo is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{150528}$. You cannot draw that — but every 2D picture in this book is an honest shadow of it, because the algebra is identical in any dimension. That is the quiet superpower of linear algebra: prove it in 2D, use it in 150 thousand D.

1.2 · The dot product — an agreement meter

If vectors are the nouns of machine learning, the dot product is the verb. It takes two vectors and returns a single number measuring how much they agree.

Definition — dot product, two equivalent forms
$$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i b_i \;=\;\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta$$

The left side is arithmetic: multiply matching entries, add them up. The right side is geometry: the product of the lengths, scaled by the cosine of the angle $\theta$ between the arrows. The equality of these two is a theorem, derived below.

Intuition — read it as alignment

Fix both lengths to 1. Then $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=\cos\theta$: it is $+1$ when the arrows point the same way, $0$ when perpendicular (no shared direction at all), $-1$ when opposed. The dot product is an agreement meter. Hold onto this: in Layer 2, attention decides which words to look at by dotting queries with keys, and Word2Vec decides which words are similar by dotting their embeddings. The most important operation in modern AI is this agreement meter, computed billions of times per second.

Derivation — why arithmetic equals geometry
1

Take the triangle with sides $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$, and the connecting side $\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}$. The law of cosines (a generalized Pythagoras) says:

$$\lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\rVert^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^{2}+\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^{2}-2\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta$$
2

Expand the left side using only arithmetic ($\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2=\sum_i v_i^2$):

$$\lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\rVert^{2}=\sum_i(a_i-b_i)^2=\sum_i a_i^2+\sum_i b_i^2-2\sum_i a_ib_i=\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^{2}+\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^{2}-2\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}$$
3

Set the two expressions equal; the squared-length terms cancel, leaving

$$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta$$

Arithmetic on coordinates secretly computes an angle. This is why a computer, which only ever does arithmetic, can reason about geometry.

Interactive

The agreement meter — rotate a vector, watch the dot product

Watch three things at once: the dot product (bar at right), the cosine similarity, and the projection — the shadow $\mathbf{b}$ casts onto $\mathbf{a}$, drawn as the teal segment. The dot product is exactly "length of $\mathbf{a}$ × length of that shadow." When the shadow points backwards, the sign flips negative.

1.3 · Lengths, distances, and similarity

Definition — norm and distance

The Euclidean norm (length) of a vector, and the distance between two vectors:

$$\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert=\sqrt{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}}=\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_i x_i^2},\qquad d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert$$

And the scale-free version of the dot product, cosine similarity:

$$\cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert}\in[-1,1]$$

Distance asks "how far apart are these points?"; cosine asks "do these arrows point the same way, regardless of length?" Machine learning uses both, and the choice matters: two documents of very different lengths can still be about the same topic — cosine similarity sees that, raw distance does not.

Where you'll meet this again

Layer 2 measures word similarity by cosine (Ch 6), normalizes activations by their statistics (LayerNorm, Ch 2), and scales attention scores by a norm-derived factor $\sqrt{d_k}$ (Ch 5). Every one of those is this section wearing a different coat.

Chapter 02

Matrices & Transformations

A matrix looks like a boring grid of numbers. It is actually a machine that moves space — every point picked up and carried somewhere else, all at once, by one consistent rule. Neural networks are towers of these machines. Understand one matrix deeply and you understand what every layer of a network is doing.

2.1 · A matrix is a function

Definition — matrix-vector multiplication

An $m\times n$ matrix $A$ maps a vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ to a vector $A\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$:

$$ (A\mathbf{x})_i=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}\,x_j $$

Equivalently — and far more usefully — $A\mathbf{x}$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$, mixed with the entries of $\mathbf{x}$ as the amounts:

$$A\mathbf{x}=x_1\,\mathbf{a}_{\text{col }1}+x_2\,\mathbf{a}_{\text{col }2}+\cdots+x_n\,\mathbf{a}_{\text{col }n}$$
Intuition — the columns tell you everything

To know what a machine does, feed it the simplest possible inputs. Feed $A$ the basis vector $\mathbf{e}_1=(1,0)$: out comes exactly column 1. Feed it $\mathbf{e}_2$: out comes column 2. So the columns of a matrix are where the basis vectors land — and since every vector is a recipe of basis vectors (Ch 1), linearity carries the whole space along for the ride. A matrix is fully described by what it does to a handful of arrows. In the demo below, you are directly dragging where $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$ land, and the entire grid follows.

A transformation is linear when it keeps grid lines straight, parallel, and evenly spaced, and keeps the origin fixed: $A(c\mathbf{x}+d\mathbf{y})=cA\mathbf{x}+dA\mathbf{y}$. Rotations, stretches, shears, reflections, projections — all linear, all matrices. What a single matrix can never do is bend space. (Remember that: it is exactly why Layer 2 must insert nonlinear activation functions between matrices.)

2.2 · Matrix multiplication is composition

Why is matrix multiplication defined by that strange rows-into-columns rule? Because it is forced — it is the unique rule that makes "apply $B$, then apply $A$" equal a single matrix $AB$.

Derivation — the multiplication rule, forced by composition
1

We want a matrix $C$ such that $C\mathbf{x}=A(B\mathbf{x})$ for every $\mathbf{x}$. From Ch 2.1, column $j$ of any matrix is where $\mathbf{e}_j$ lands. So:

$$\mathbf{c}_{\text{col }j}=A(B\,\mathbf{e}_j)=A\,\mathbf{b}_{\text{col }j}$$
2

Column $j$ of $C$ is therefore $A$ applied to column $j$ of $B$. Writing that entry-by-entry gives exactly the textbook rule:

$$C_{ij}=\sum_{k}A_{ik}B_{kj}$$

Each entry of the product is a dot product — row $i$ of $A$ agreeing with column $j$ of $B$. The "weird" rule is just composition of machines, written in coordinates.

3

Composition order matters ($AB\neq BA$ in general — shear then rotate is not rotate then shear), and composition is associative, $(AB)C=A(BC)$, because "do C, then B, then A" needs no parentheses. Associativity is why a deep network of stacked layers is mathematically coherent.

Where you'll meet this again

A neural network layer is $\phi(W\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})$: a matrix machine, a shift, a bend. Attention's scores are the matrix product $QK^{\top}$ — every query dotted against every key in one multiplication. GPUs exist, commercially speaking, to do this one operation absurdly fast.

2.3 · Determinant, eigenvectors, rank

Three diagnostics tell you what kind of machine a matrix is.

Definition — determinant

For $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$, the determinant is $\det A = ad-bc$. It is the (signed) factor by which $A$ scales area — volume in higher dimensions. $|\det A|=2$ doubles areas; $\det A=0$ flattens space onto a line (or point); a negative sign means the plane was flipped over.

Definition — eigenvectors and eigenvalues

An eigenvector of $A$ is a nonzero vector whose direction survives the machine — it only gets scaled:

$$A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\,\mathbf{v}$$

The scale factor $\lambda$ is its eigenvalue. Eigenvectors are the skeleton of a transformation: the axes along which a complicated-looking map is just stretching.

Intuition — why anyone cares

Repeatedly applying a matrix (as recurrent networks do, and as gradient flow through deep networks effectively does) amplifies the eigen-directions with $|\lambda|\gt 1$ and crushes those with $|\lambda|\lt 1$. The vanishing/exploding gradients of Layer 2's Chapter 4 are an eigenvalue story. PCA — the classical workhorse of dimensionality reduction — is "find the eigenvectors of the data's covariance matrix": the directions along which the data genuinely varies.

Finally, rank counts the number of independent directions that survive the machine — the dimension of the output space actually reached. Full rank means nothing is lost and the matrix is invertible; rank-deficient means a collapse like the demo's "collapse" preset, where $\det A=0$ and the inverse cannot exist. Low-rank structure is also a gift: Layer 2's LoRA fine-tunes giant models by learning only a low-rank correction.

Chapter 03

Calculus of Change

Learning means improving, and improving means knowing which direction is "better." Calculus is the mathematics of that question. One idea — the derivative — asked three ways (one variable, chained variables, many variables) gives us everything gradient descent and backpropagation will ever need.

3.1 · The derivative — a local slope

Definition — the derivative

The derivative of $f$ at $x$ is the limit of "rise over run" as the run shrinks to nothing:

$$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

Geometrically it is the slope of the tangent line at $x$; operationally it answers: if I nudge the input a tiny bit, how much does the output move, and in which direction?

Intuition — a speedometer, and a zoom lens

Average speed over an hour is $\Delta\text{distance}/\Delta\text{time}$. Your speedometer shows the same ratio over an instant — that is a derivative. Equivalently: zoom into any smooth curve far enough and it becomes indistinguishable from a straight line; the derivative is that line's slope. "Smooth functions are locally linear" is the single sentence on which all of deep learning's training rests: at any point, the loss surface looks like a tilted plane, and a tilted plane tells you which way is down.

Derivation — a derivative from scratch: $f(x)=x^2$
1

Form the rise-over-run for a finite nudge $h$:

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}=\frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}=\frac{x^2+2xh+h^2-x^2}{h}=\frac{2xh+h^2}{h}$$
2

Cancel the $h$ (legal while $h\neq0$): the ratio is $2x+h$. Now let the nudge vanish, $h\to0$:

$$f'(x)=2x$$

The slope of $x^2$ grows linearly with $x$ — gentle near the bottom, steep far away. (You will see this exact shape again as the MSE loss: small errors get small corrective pushes, large errors large ones.)

3

The same patient procedure produces a small toolkit that covers nearly everything: $\frac{d}{dx}x^{n}=nx^{n-1}$, $\frac{d}{dx}e^{x}=e^{x}$, $\frac{d}{dx}\ln x=\frac1x$, $(f+g)'=f'+g'$, $(fg)'=f'g+fg'$. Memorize the toolkit; remember it all came from one limit.

Interactive

The zoom lens — secant lines becoming a tangent

The amber line connects $(x,f(x))$ to $(x{+}h,f(x{+}h))$ — a secant, the average slope over the nudge. Shrink $h$ and watch it settle onto the coral tangent. Then switch to $|x|$ and put the point at $0$: the secant never settles. The kink has no single slope — no derivative — which is precisely the issue Layer 2 tiptoes around with ReLU.

3.2 · The chain rule — sensitivities multiply

Deep learning's functions are pipelines: $x\to g(x)\to f(g(x))$. The chain rule says how a nudge propagates through a pipeline.

Definition — chain rule
$$\frac{d}{dx}f\big(g(x)\big)=f'\big(g(x)\big)\cdot g'(x)$$
Intuition — gear ratios

If a bike's pedal gear turns the chainring 2× per pedal turn, and the chainring turns the wheel 3× per chainring turn, the wheel turns $2\times3=6$× per pedal turn. Sensitivities along a chain multiply. That is the entire chain rule — and the entire mechanism of backpropagation, which multiplies layer-by-layer sensitivities backward through a network. It is also the seed of Layer 2's vanishing-gradient problem: multiply many gear ratios smaller than 1 and the far end barely turns.

Derivation — sketch, and a worked example
1

A nudge $\Delta x$ produces $\Delta g\approx g'(x)\,\Delta x$ (local linearity). That output nudge produces $\Delta f\approx f'(g)\,\Delta g$. Substitute:

$$\Delta f\approx f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\,\Delta x\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}\to f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$$
2

Worked example — the function $\sigma(2x+1)$ where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid with $\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)$ (derived in Layer 2):

$$\frac{d}{dx}\,\sigma(2x+1)=\sigma'(2x+1)\cdot 2=2\,\sigma(2x+1)\big(1-\sigma(2x+1)\big)$$

Outer sensitivity times inner sensitivity. A neural network just iterates this, thousands of layers of gears deep.

3.3 · Many variables — the gradient, and descent

A loss function takes millions of inputs (all the parameters) and returns one number. The derivative idea survives intact: hold everything else still, nudge one variable, record the sensitivity. Do that for each variable and stack the answers into a vector.

Definition — partial derivatives and the gradient
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+h\,\mathbf{e}_i)-f(\mathbf{x})}{h},\qquad \nabla f(\mathbf{x})=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\dots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right)$$
Derivation — the gradient points uphill, steepest
1

Walk from $\mathbf{x}$ in a unit direction $\mathbf{u}$. By local linearity, the rate of change is the directional derivative, a dot product:

$$D_{\mathbf{u}}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u}=\lVert\nabla f\rVert\cos\theta$$
2

This is the agreement meter of Chapter 1! It is maximized when $\theta=0$ — walking with the gradient — and most negative at $\theta=\pi$, walking against it. Hence:

$$\textbf{steepest ascent}=\nabla f,\qquad \textbf{steepest descent}=-\nabla f$$
3

That single fact licenses the master algorithm of this whole curriculum, gradient descent: repeat $\;\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}-\eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$ with a small step size $\eta$. Everything in Layer 2's training chapter is refinement of this one line.

Interactive

Descending a curve — watch step size make or break it

Click anywhere on the curve to place the ball, then run. Small $\eta$: a safe but slow roll. Large $\eta$: overshooting, oscillation, even explosion. And notice the two valleys — start in the wrong basin and descent happily settles in the worse minimum. It only ever knows the local slope; it has no map. These are not bugs in the demo; they are the true open problems of optimization that Layer 2's momentum and Adam exist to soften.

Chapter 04

Probability

Data is noisy, sensors lie a little, and the future is uncertain. Probability is the calculus of uncertainty — the rules for holding beliefs of intermediate strength and updating them honestly when evidence arrives. Machine learning is, at bottom, applied probability.

4.1 · Thinking in probabilities

Definition — the three rules of probability

A probability assigns each event $A$ a number $P(A)\in[0,1]$, with $P(\text{certain})=1$, and for mutually exclusive events $P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)$. From these, two workhorse rules:

$$\textbf{Product rule: } P(A,B)=P(A\mid B)\,P(B)\qquad\quad \textbf{Sum rule: } P(A)=\sum_{b}P(A,B{=}b)$$

where $P(A\mid B)$ — "$A$ given $B$" — is the probability of $A$ once you know $B$ happened: $P(A\mid B)=P(A,B)/P(B)$.

Intuition — probability is area

Picture all possible worlds as a square of area 1. An event is a region; its probability is its area. Conditioning on $B$ means zooming in: throw away everything outside $B$, and re-measure areas as fractions of $B$ alone. The sum rule says a region's area is the sum of its slices. Every probability identity in this chapter is provable by staring at overlapping regions — and the Bayes demo below is literally that picture, drawn with people instead of area.

Two events are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other: $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$, equivalently $P(A,B)=P(A)P(B)$. Independence is the assumption that lets likelihoods of datasets factor into products — which is why logarithms (turning products into sums) appear everywhere in ML.

4.2 · Bayes' theorem — the mathematics of changing your mind

Derivation — Bayes from one line of algebra
1

Write the product rule both ways around — the joint probability doesn't care about order:

$$P(H\mid E)\,P(E)=P(H,E)=P(E\mid H)\,P(H)$$
2

Divide by $P(E)$:

$$P(H\mid E)=\frac{P(E\mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)}$$

Read with names: the posterior (belief in hypothesis $H$ after evidence $E$) equals the likelihood (how well $H$ predicts $E$) times the prior (belief before), normalized by the overall probability of the evidence.

3

The denominator expands by the sum rule over all hypotheses, $P(E)=\sum_h P(E\mid h)P(h)$ — it is just the normalizer making posteriors sum to 1.

Intuition — why test results surprise people

A disease affects 1% of people. A test catches 90% of true cases, with a 9% false-alarm rate. You test positive — how worried should you be? Most people say "around 90%." Bayes says: out of 1000 people, ~10 are sick and ~9 of them test positive; but ~89 of the 990 healthy people also test positive. Your positive result is one of ~98, of which only 9 are real: about a 9% chance. The prior (rarity of the disease) dominates. Evidence doesn't replace prior belief — it reweights it. Play with exactly this in the demo.

Interactive

Bayes, drawn as a population — priors vs. evidence

Each dot is a person; rows of fate, columns of luck. Filled dots tested positive. The posterior is simply: of the filled dots, what fraction are coral (sick)? Drag the prevalence up and watch the same test become trustworthy — the posterior is a tug-of-war between prior and likelihood.

Where you'll meet this again

All of machine learning can be read as Bayes at scale: a model's parameters are the hypothesis, the dataset is the evidence, and training seeks parameters with high posterior. MLE (next chapter) is Bayes with a flat prior; Layer 2's regularization is Bayes with an informative one; and a VLA deciding what it sees from noisy pixels is doing this arithmetic implicitly in every forward pass.

4.3 · Random variables, expectation, variance

Definition — random variable, expectation, variance

A random variable $X$ is a quantity whose value is determined by chance, described by a distribution $p(x)$. Its expectation (long-run average) and variance (average squared spread) are:

$$\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_x x\,p(x)\;\;\text{(or }\textstyle\int x\,p(x)\,dx\text{)},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2\big]$$
Derivation — the shortcut formula for variance
1

Expand the square inside the expectation, writing $\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]$ (a constant):

$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2-2\mu X+\mu^2]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-2\mu\,\mathbb{E}[X]+\mu^2$$

using linearity of expectation, $\mathbb{E}[aX+b]=a\mathbb{E}[X]+b$ — itself immediate from the definition.

2

Substitute $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu$ and simplify:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mu^2=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mathbb{E}[X]^2$$

"Mean of the square minus square of the mean." This identity powers Layer 2's batch-norm statistics, Adam's second-moment estimates, and the $\sqrt{d_k}$ attention-scaling derivation.

4.4 · The Gaussian, and why it is everywhere

Definition — the normal (Gaussian) distribution
$$\mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

Two parameters: the mean $\mu$ (where the bell sits) and the variance $\sigma^2$ (how wide it is). About 68% of the mass lies within $1\sigma$ of the mean, 95% within $2\sigma$.

Why does this one curve rule statistics? The Central Limit Theorem: sum (or average) many independent random influences — whatever their individual shapes — and the result's distribution approaches a Gaussian. Measurement noise is a sum of countless tiny pushes, so noise is Gaussian; that assumption is what made MSE the "correct" regression loss in Layer 2, and Gaussian noise is literally the raw material that diffusion models sculpt images from.

Interactive

The Central Limit Theorem — order out of randomness

At $n=1$ the histogram just mirrors the (deliberately ugly) source. Raise $n$ — averaging more draws per sample — and the bell emerges regardless of the source's shape, with the dashed Gaussian fit hugging it. Averages of anything become Gaussian. That is the CLT, and it is why the bell curve earned the name "normal."

Chapter 05

Estimation & Maximum Likelihood

Probability runs forward: given a model, what data should I expect? Statistics runs the film backwards: given data, what model was likely behind it? The bridge between them — maximum likelihood — is the single principle from which nearly every loss function in deep learning is derived.

5.1 · The likelihood, and its maximum

Definition — likelihood and the MLE

Suppose data $x_1,\dots,x_N$ were drawn independently from a distribution $p(x\mid\theta)$ with unknown parameter $\theta$. The likelihood of $\theta$ is the probability of the data, viewed as a function of $\theta$:

$$L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(x_i\mid\theta)$$

The maximum likelihood estimate is the parameter making the observed data most probable: $\hat\theta=\arg\max_\theta L(\theta)$. In practice we maximize $\log L$ — the log turns the product into a sum and moves the maximum nowhere, since $\log$ is increasing.

Intuition — which world makes my data least surprising?

Imagine every candidate $\theta$ as a parallel world. In each world, ask: "how surprised would I be to see exactly my data?" MLE picks the world of minimum surprise. It does not say that world is true — only that, among the candidates, it explains what you saw best. Minimizing a loss in deep learning is this: Layer 2 shows that MSE is Gaussian MLE and cross-entropy is categorical MLE. "Training a network" and "maximum likelihood" are, for the standard losses, the same sentence.

Derivation — MLE for a coin (Bernoulli)
1

A coin lands heads with unknown probability $\theta$. In $N$ flips you saw $k$ heads. Each head contributes $\theta$, each tail $(1-\theta)$:

$$L(\theta)=\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{N-k}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\ell(\theta)=\log L=k\log\theta+(N-k)\log(1-\theta)$$
2

At a maximum the slope is zero. Differentiate and set to zero (using $\frac{d}{d\theta}\log\theta=1/\theta$):

$$\ell'(\theta)=\frac{k}{\theta}-\frac{N-k}{1-\theta}=0\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;k(1-\theta)=(N-k)\,\theta$$
3

Solve:

$$\hat\theta=\frac{k}{N}$$

The principle of minimum surprise recovers exactly the answer common sense wanted: the observed frequency. But now it is a theorem, and the same machinery will keep working where common sense has no guess to offer.

Interactive

The likelihood curve — flip coins, watch the peak move

The curve is the (normalized) likelihood $\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{N-k}$ over all candidate biases; the coral marker sits at its peak $k/N$, the dashed teal line at the hidden truth. Watch two things as flips accumulate: the peak homes in on the truth, and the curve narrows — more data doesn't just improve the estimate, it sharpens the confidence. With 3 flips, many worlds explain your data; with 300, very few survive.

5.2 · MLE for the Gaussian — where "the average" comes from

Derivation — the sample mean is an MLE
1

Data $x_1,\dots,x_N$ from $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with $\sigma$ known. The log-likelihood (dropping constants in $\mu$):

$$\ell(\mu)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-\mu)^2+\text{const}$$
2

Maximizing $\ell$ means minimizing the sum of squared distances — squared error appears by itself, uninvited. Set the derivative to zero:

$$\frac{d\ell}{d\mu}=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_i(x_i-\mu)=0\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\hat\mu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i x_i$$
3

The humble average is the maximum-likelihood estimate under Gaussian noise. This is the seed of the whole "least squares" tradition — and the reason Layer 2 could derive MSE from first principles. When you compute a mean, you are fitting a model.

Where you'll meet this again

Every loss in Layer 2 is a negative log-likelihood: MSE ↔ Gaussian, cross-entropy ↔ categorical, the VAE's objective ↔ a likelihood bound, diffusion's noise-prediction ↔ a likelihood bound too. "Pick a loss" really means "state your noise model."

Chapter 06

Learning from Data

We now have all the parts: vectors to hold data, transformations to model relationships, derivatives to improve, likelihood to define "good." Click them together and you get the first real learning algorithms — and the template that every neural network still follows.

6.1 · The supervised learning setup

Definition — supervised learning

Given a dataset of input–output pairs $\{(\mathbf{x}_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ drawn from some unknown distribution, choose a family of functions $f_{\boldsymbol\theta}$ (the model), a loss $\mathcal{L}$ measuring prediction error, and find

$$\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}\big(f_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf{x}_i),\,y_i\big)$$

Three independent choices — model family, loss, optimizer — and every learning system in this curriculum, up to and including a robot's VLA policy, is an instance of this template.

Intuition — learning is curve-fitting with consequences

Strip the mystique: supervised learning is fitting a curve through labeled points and hoping the curve keeps being right between and beyond them. The drama is in the hoping — that is Chapter 7. A VLA "learns to pick up blocks" by fitting a (very large) curve from camera pixels to motor torques through millions of demonstration points. Same template, bigger curve.

6.2 · Linear regression — the first model

The simplest useful family: a line, $f(x)=wx+b$. The Gaussian-MLE argument of Chapter 5 hands us the loss — squared error. Now calculus finds the best line exactly.

Derivation — least squares, solved in closed form
1

Define the loss as a function of the two parameters:

$$\mathcal{L}(w,b)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(wx_i+b-y_i\big)^2$$

It is a smooth bowl in $(w,b)$ — quadratic, hence one minimum, found where both partial derivatives vanish.

2

Set $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial b=0$. With $\bar{x},\bar{y}$ the means:

$$\frac{2}{N}\sum_i(wx_i+b-y_i)=0\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;b=\bar{y}-w\bar{x}$$

The best line passes through the data's center of mass $(\bar x,\bar y)$.

3

Substitute into $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w=0$ and solve (a few lines of algebra):

$$w=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})^2}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)}$$

The slope is "how much $x$ and $y$ vary together, per unit of how much $x$ varies alone." In matrix form for many features this becomes the normal equation $\hat{\mathbf{w}}=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\mathbf{y}$ — same derivation, vector clothes.

6.3 · Logistic regression — from numbers to decisions

Many questions want a probability, not a number: will this customer churn? is this pixel part of the red block? Bolting a probability onto a line takes one function — the sigmoid, $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$ — which squashes any real number into $(0,1)$.

Definition — logistic regression
$$P(y=1\mid x)=\sigma(wx+b)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(wx+b)}}$$

Fit $w,b$ by maximum likelihood: maximize $\prod_i P(y_i\mid x_i)$, equivalently minimize the binary cross-entropy $-\sum_i\big[y_i\log\hat p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)\big]$.

Derivation — why the sigmoid, of all squashers?
1

Model the log-odds as linear (odds = probability for, over probability against):

$$\log\frac{P(y{=}1\mid x)}{1-P(y{=}1\mid x)}=wx+b$$

This is the actual modeling assumption — "each unit of $x$ multiplies the odds by a constant factor $e^{w}$."

2

Solve for the probability: exponentiate, $\frac{p}{1-p}=e^{wx+b}$, then rearrange $p=(1-p)e^{wx+b}$:

$$p=\frac{e^{wx+b}}{1+e^{wx+b}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(wx+b)}}=\sigma(wx+b)$$

The sigmoid is not an arbitrary squasher — it is the unique inverse of "linear log-odds." Its multi-class generalization is the softmax of Layer 2.

3

And a small miracle for training: the gradient of the BCE loss for one example works out to

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w}=(\hat p - y)\,x$$

— error times input, no sigmoid derivative surviving. The same cancellation Layer 2 finds for softmax + cross-entropy. Clean losses make clean gradients.

The punchline of this chapter

Logistic regression is a one-neuron neural network: weighted sum, sigmoid, cross-entropy, gradient descent. Layer 2's first chapter begins by stacking this exact object into layers. You have now built, from first principles, the atom of deep learning.

Chapter 07

Generalization

Fitting the data you have is easy — a wavy enough curve can hit every point exactly. The entire difficulty of machine learning is doing well on data you haven't seen. This chapter is about that gap: why it appears, how to measure it honestly, and the levers that shrink it.

7.1 · Overfitting and underfitting

Intuition — memorizing vs. understanding

A student can pass a practice exam two ways: understand the subject, or memorize the answer key. Both score 100% on practice; only one survives the real exam. A model with too much flexibility "memorizes the answer key" — it fits the noise, the accidents of this particular sample, not the signal. A model with too little flexibility can't even represent the signal. The honest test is always the same: held-out data the model never saw. Train on one split, evaluate on another; the gap between the two errors is the overfitting, made visible.

Definition — training vs. generalization error

The training error is the average loss on the fitting data; the generalization (test) error is the expected loss on fresh data from the same distribution:

$$\mathcal{E}_{\text{train}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\text{train}}\mathcal{L}(f(\mathbf{x}_i),y_i),\qquad \mathcal{E}_{\text{gen}}=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}\big[\mathcal{L}(f(\mathbf{x}),y)\big]$$

Underfitting: both errors high (model too simple). Overfitting: training error low, generalization error high (model too flexible for the data available).

7.2 · The bias–variance decomposition

The U-shape isn't an accident; it is arithmetic. Expected squared error provably splits into three named parts.

Derivation — bias² + variance + noise
1

Setup: truth $y=g(x)+\varepsilon$ with noise variance $\sigma^2$; our learned model $\hat f$ depends on the random training sample, so $\hat f(x)$ is itself a random variable. Write $\bar f(x)=\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)]$ for the average model over re-drawn datasets. Decompose the error by inserting $\bar f$:

$$\hat f(x)-y=\underbrace{\big(\hat f(x)-\bar f(x)\big)}_{\text{model's wobble}}+\underbrace{\big(\bar f(x)-g(x)\big)}_{\text{average miss}}-\varepsilon$$
2

Square and take expectations. All three cross-terms vanish — each contains a zero-mean factor ($\hat f-\bar f$ has mean zero by construction; $\varepsilon$ has mean zero and is independent of the rest):

$$\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat f(x)-y)^2\big]=\underbrace{\big(\bar f(x)-g(x)\big)^2}_{\textbf{bias}^2}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat f(x)-\bar f(x))^2\big]}_{\textbf{variance}}+\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\textbf{noise}}$$
3

Read it as a budget. Bias: error from the model family being too rigid to capture the truth, no matter the sample (the degree-1 board). Variance: error from the model swinging with the luck of the sample (the degree-12 contortionist — re-draw the data and it fits a wildly different curve). Noise: the floor nobody beats. Simple models spend on bias; flexible models spend on variance; learning well is balancing the budget.

A modern asterisk

Deep networks complicate this classic picture: wildly overparameterized models often generalize well ("double descent"), with implicit regularization from SGD doing quiet work. The decomposition stays true — it is arithmetic — but which regime you land in is subtler. Layer 2's regularization chapter picks up this thread.

7.3 · Regularization — buying smoothness

Definition — ridge (L2) regularization

Add a penalty on weight size to the loss, with strength $\lambda$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{ridge}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i\big(f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x}_i)-y_i\big)^2+\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$$

Large wiggly fits need large coefficients; taxing $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$ makes wiggles expensive, so the optimizer buys them only when the data truly pays for them. L1 ($\lambda\sum|w_i|$) instead pushes weights exactly to zero — automatic feature selection.

Intuition — and the Bayesian reading

Regularization is a thumb on the scale toward simple explanations — Occam's razor as a term in the loss. The Bayesian view makes it precise: minimizing the L2-penalized loss is exactly maximizing the posterior with a Gaussian prior "weights are probably small." (Take the negative log of prior × likelihood and the penalty appears.) The same idea reappears in Layer 2 as weight decay in AdamW, dropout, and early stopping: different taxes, same principle — don't let the model spend complexity it can't justify.

Chapter 08

Information Theory

How much does a message really say? Claude Shannon's 1948 answer gave "information" a unit and a calculus — and, decades later, handed deep learning its most important loss function. This short chapter closes the loop back to Layer 2.

8.1 · Surprise and entropy

Intuition — information is surprise

"The sun rose this morning" carries no information — you already knew. "It snowed in the Sahara" carries a lot. Information should measure surprise: rarer events say more. And surprise should add up: two independent surprises together should sum. The unique function turning "probabilities multiply" into "surprises add" is the logarithm — which forces the definition below, up to the choice of base (base 2 measures in bits).

Definition — surprise (self-information) and entropy

The surprise of an event with probability $p$ is $I(p)=\log_2\frac{1}{p}$, so that $I(pq)=I(p)+I(q)$. The entropy of a distribution is its average surprise:

$$H(p)=\mathbb{E}[I]=-\sum_{x}p(x)\log_2 p(x)$$

Entropy is largest for the uniform distribution (every outcome equally surprising — maximum uncertainty) and zero for a certain one. It is also the provable minimum average number of yes/no questions — bits — needed to communicate a sample.

8.2 · Cross-entropy and KL divergence — the bridge to Layer 2

Definition — cross-entropy and KL divergence

If reality follows $p$ but you act (compress, predict, bet) as if it follows $q$, your average surprise is the cross-entropy; the overhead versus knowing the truth is the KL divergence:

$$H(p,q)=-\sum_x p(x)\log q(x),\qquad D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\,\|\,q)=H(p,q)-H(p)=\sum_x p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$$
Derivation — KL is never negative
1

Use the bound $\log t\le t-1$ (the log sits below its tangent at $t{=}1$, with equality only at $t{=}1$). Apply it inside $-D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ with $t=q(x)/p(x)$:

$$-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=\sum_x p(x)\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\le\sum_x p(x)\Big(\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}-1\Big)$$
2

The right side telescopes: $\sum_x q(x)-\sum_x p(x)=1-1=0$. Hence

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\,\|\,q)\ge 0,\qquad\text{with equality iff } q=p$$

Wrong beliefs always cost extra surprise; the only free lunch is the truth. So minimizing cross-entropy $H(p,q)=H(p)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ over $q$ drives $q$ toward $p$ — which is exactly what Layer 2's classification training does: the network's predicted distribution is $q$, the labels are $p$, and "minimize cross-entropy loss" means "make your beliefs match reality, measured in bits."
