01

SLAM: the chicken-and-egg problem

To do anything at all, a mobile robot needs two pieces of basic information:

  • Where am I? ⟹ Localization
  • What is around me? ⟹ Mapping

The trouble is that each answer assumes the other. Localizing is easy if you have a map to match your sensor readings against; mapping is easy if you know where you are when each measurement was taken. A robot dropped into an unknown building has neither — it must estimate both at once. That joint problem is Simultaneous Localization and Mapping:

The SLAM problem
Given sensor measurements \(\mathbf z_{0:T}\) (e.g., images) and control inputs \(\mathbf u_{0:T-1}\) (e.g., velocity), estimate the robot state trajectory \(\mathbf x_{0:T}\) (e.g., pose) and build a map \(\mathbf m\) of the environment.

All three problems as optimization

Here is the idea that powers this whole lecture: each problem can be written as minimize how much your variables disagree with your data. The motion model \(f\) and observation model \(h\) from Lecture 4 tell us what the data should look like; the residual between prediction and reality, squared and summed, is the objective.

Mapping (trajectory known, map unknown) — find the map that best explains the measurements:

$$\min_{\mathbf m}\ \sum_{t=0}^{T}\lVert\mathbf z_t-h(\mathbf x_t,\mathbf m)\rVert_2^2$$

Localization (map known, trajectory unknown) — find the trajectory that explains the measurements and is consistent with the controls:

$$\min_{\mathbf x_{0:T}}\ \sum_{t=0}^{T}\lVert\mathbf z_t-h(\mathbf x_t,\mathbf m)\rVert_2^2+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\lVert\mathbf x_{t+1}-f(\mathbf x_t,\mathbf u_t)\rVert_2^2$$

SLAM (both unknown, initial state \(\mathbf x_0\) given) — optimize over everything at once:

$$\boxed{\ \min_{\mathbf x_{1:T},\,\mathbf m}\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\mathbf z_t-h(\mathbf x_t,\mathbf m)\rVert_2^2+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\lVert\mathbf x_{t+1}-f(\mathbf x_t,\mathbf u_t)\rVert_2^2\ }$$
Read each term as a tug-of-war. The first sum pulls the variables toward agreeing with the sensors; the second pulls consecutive states toward agreeing with the motion model. Neither can be satisfied perfectly when the data are noisy, so the optimum is the compromise that displeases everyone the least — that is exactly what least squares means. (Why squares? They are smooth, they punish big errors hard, and — as we will see — they are what maximum-likelihood estimation under Gaussian noise demands.)
02

Warm-up: localization with linear models

Before tackling poses on manifolds, solve the simplest honest version — everything linear, everything in \(\mathbb R^n\):

  • State: \(\mathbf x_t\in\mathbb R^n\)
  • Motion model: \(\mathbf x_{t+1}=f(\mathbf x_t,\mathbf u_t)=F\mathbf x_t+G\mathbf u_t\)
  • Observation model: \(\mathbf z_t=h(\mathbf x_t)=H\mathbf x_t\)

Given \(\mathbf x_0=\mathbf 0\), measurements \(\mathbf z_{1:T}\), and inputs \(\mathbf u_{0:T-1}\), the localization objective is:

$$\min_{\mathbf x_{1:T}}\ c(\mathbf x_{1:T}):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\mathbf z_t-H\mathbf x_t\rVert_2^2+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\lVert\mathbf x_{t+1}-F\mathbf x_t-G\mathbf u_t\rVert_2^2$$

Step 1 — Stack everything into one big vector

The key bookkeeping fact is that splitting a squared norm over stacked blocks changes nothing:

$$\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf x_1\\\mathbf x_2\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf y_1\\\mathbf y_2\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert_2^2=\lVert\mathbf x_1-\mathbf y_1\rVert_2^2+\lVert\mathbf x_2-\mathbf y_2\rVert_2^2$$

So run it backwards: a sum of squared residuals is the squared norm of one stacked residual vector. Stack all measurement residuals, then all motion residuals:

$$c(\mathbf x_{1:T})=\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf z_1-H\mathbf x_1\\\vdots\\\mathbf z_T-H\mathbf x_T\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert_2^2+\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf x_1-F\mathbf x_0-G\mathbf u_0\\\vdots\\\mathbf x_T-F\mathbf x_{T-1}-G\mathbf u_{T-1}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert_2^2$$

Step 2 — Pull the unknowns out front

Each stacked residual is linear in the unknown super-vector \(\mathbf x_{1:T}=(\mathbf x_1,\dots,\mathbf x_T)\), so it factors as a block matrix times \(\mathbf x_{1:T}\) plus a constant. The measurement block is block-diagonal in \(H\); the motion block has \(-I\) on the diagonal and \(F\) below it (each motion residual couples two consecutive states — remember this shape, it is the sparsity story of section 09):

$$c(\mathbf x_{1:T})=\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}H&&\\&\ddots&\\&&H\\\hline -I&&\\ F&\ddots&\\&\ddots&-I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf x_1\\\vdots\\\mathbf x_T\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbf z_1\\\vdots\\-\mathbf z_T\\\hline F\mathbf x_0+G\mathbf u_0\\ G\mathbf u_1\\\vdots\\ G\mathbf u_{T-1}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert_2^2=\lVert A\,\mathbf x_{1:T}+\mathbf b\rVert_2^2$$

(The motion rows got multiplied by \(-1\) to put the \(-I\) on the diagonal — harmless inside a norm.) The entire estimation problem is now one quadratic in one vector.

Step 3 — Differentiate and descend

Expanding \(\lVert A\mathbf x+\mathbf b\rVert_2^2=(A\mathbf x+\mathbf b)^\top(A\mathbf x+\mathbf b)\) and using \(\nabla_{\mathbf x}(\mathbf x^\top M\mathbf x)=2M\mathbf x\) for symmetric \(M\):

$$\nabla c(\mathbf x_{1:T})=2A^\top(A\,\mathbf x_{1:T}+\mathbf b)$$

Gradient descent: initialize \(\mathbf x_{1:T}^{(0)}\) (dead reckoning is the natural choice) and iterate downhill with step size \(\alpha^{(k)}\):

$$\boxed{\ \mathbf x_{1:T}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf x_{1:T}^{(k)}-2\alpha^{(k)}A^\top\big(A\,\mathbf x_{1:T}^{(k)}+\mathbf b\big)\ }$$
Since the objective is a convex quadratic, the minimum also has a closed form — set the gradient to zero and get the normal equations \(A^\top A\,\mathbf x_{1:T}=-A^\top\mathbf b\). Gradient descent is shown here because it survives the jump to nonlinear models later, where no closed form exists. 03

Case study: orientation tracking (Project 1)

Now the same least-squares recipe on a curved space — the unit quaternions of Lecture 3. A rigid body undergoes pure rotation; we want its orientation over time from an IMU:

  • State: orientation \(q_t\in\mathbb H_*\) of the body frame relative to the world frame.
  • Control: body-frame angular velocity \(\mathbf u_t\in\mathbb R^3\) from gyroscope measurements (rad/s) over the interval \(\tau_t\).
  • Motion model — quaternion kinematics from Lecture 4, discretized with the exponential: $$q_{t+1}=f(q_t,\tau_t\mathbf u_t):=q_t\circ\exp\big(\big[0,\ \tau_t\mathbf u_t/2\big]\big)$$
  • Observation model — the accelerometer \(\mathbf z_t\in\mathbb R^3\) (m/s²) should approximately match the world-frame gravity acceleration \(-g\mathbf e_3\) expressed in the body frame: $$\mathbf z_t\approx h(q_t):=q_t^{-1}\circ[0,\ -g\mathbf e_3]\circ q_t$$
Decode \(h\): the sandwich \(q\circ[0,\mathbf x]\circ q^{-1}\) rotates \(\mathbf x\) by \(R(q)\) (Lecture 3), so the reversed sandwich \(q^{-1}\circ[0,\mathbf x]\circ q\) applies \(R(q)^\top\) — world to body. Hence \(h(q_t)=-gR(q_t)^\top\mathbf e_3\): exactly the strapdown IMU model of Lecture 4 with zero body acceleration and zero bias. The accelerometer acts as a noisy "which way is down" sensor; the gyro stitches orientations together between those hints.

Measuring error between orientations

The motion residual \(\mathbf x_{t+1}-f(\mathbf x_t,\mathbf u_t)\) made sense in \(\mathbb R^n\). Subtracting quaternions does not — the difference of two unit quaternions is not a rotation. The right notion of distance comes straight from Lecture 3's log map: measure the angle of the relative rotation:

$$d(q_1,q_2)=\lVert\boldsymbol\theta_{12}\rVert_2=\big\lVert\,2\log\!\big(q_1^{-1}\circ q_2\big)\big\rVert_2$$

where \(q_{12}=q_1^{-1}q_2\) is the relative rotation and \(2\log\) extracts its axis–angle vector \(\boldsymbol\theta_{12}\). Two identical orientations give \(d=0\); orientations \(30^\circ\) apart give \(d=\pi/6\), no matter about which axis.

The optimization problem

Starting at \(q_0=[1,\mathbf 0]\), estimate \(q_{1:T}\) from gyro inputs \(\mathbf u_{0:T-1}\) and accelerometer measurements \(\mathbf z_{1:T}\). Because every \(q_t\) must remain a valid orientation, the problem is constrained:

$$\min_{q_{1:T}}\ c(q_{1:T}):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\mathbf z_t-h(q_t)\rVert_2^2+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\big\lVert 2\log\!\big(q_{t+1}^{-1}\circ f(q_t,\tau_t\mathbf u_t)\big)\big\rVert_2^2\qquad\text{s.t. }\ \lVert q_t\rVert_2=1\ \ \forall t$$

Same anatomy as before — an observation term and a motion term — but the motion residual now uses the geometric difference \(2\log(q_{t+1}^{-1}\circ f(\cdot))\), which is zero exactly when \(q_{t+1}\) equals the gyro prediction.

Projected gradient descent
A simple way to honor the constraint: take an ordinary gradient step, then project back onto the unit sphere (normalize each quaternion): $$q_{1:T}^{(k+1)}=\Pi_{\mathbb H_*}\Big(q_{1:T}^{(k)}-\alpha^{(k)}\nabla c\big(q_{1:T}^{(k)}\big)\Big)$$ The step drifts slightly off the manifold of unit quaternions; \(\Pi_{\mathbb H_*}\) (division by the norm) puts it back. This is the flat-space/curved-space dance of Lecture 3 in optimization form.

Case study: building a panorama (Project 1)

Once orientations \(R_t\) are tracked, the camera images can be stitched into a panorama. The trick: pretend every image lies on a unit sphere around the camera, use the orientation to place it, then unwrap the sphere onto a flat rectangle. Two pipelines, straight from the lecture:

Image → sphere → world

  1. Find the longitude \(\lambda\) and latitude \(\phi\) of each pixel from its row and column, using the horizontal (\(60^\circ\)) and vertical (\(45^\circ\)) fields of view — pixels are evenly spread across the FOV.
  2. Convert spherical \((\lambda,\phi,1)\) to Cartesian coordinates assuming depth 1: $$\mathbf v=(\cos\phi\cos\lambda,\ \cos\phi\sin\lambda,\ \sin\phi)$$
  3. Rotate to the world frame with the tracked orientation: \(\mathbf v_W=R\,\mathbf v\).

World → cylinder → flat image

  1. Convert the world Cartesian directions back to spherical \((\lambda,\phi)\).
  2. Inscribe the sphere in a cylinder: the point at \((\lambda,\phi)\) maps to height \(\phi\) on the cylinder at longitude \(\lambda\) along its circumference.
  3. Unwrap the cylinder to a rectangle of width \(2\pi\) and height \(\pi\) radians. (This equidistant choice is one of many sphere-to-plane projections — equal-area, Miller, and friends trade distortions differently.)

05

Factor graphs: drawing the problem

Look back at the SLAM objective. It is a sum of small terms, and each term touches only one or two variables: a motion term touches \((\mathbf x_t,\mathbf x_{t+1})\); an observation term touches \((\mathbf x_t,\mathbf m_j)\). That locality is structure worth drawing:

Definition
A factor graph is a bipartite graph describing the data (observations \(\mathbf z_t\), inputs \(\mathbf u_t\)) and the variables (states \(\mathbf x_t\), landmarks \(\mathbf m_j\)) of a SLAM problem.
  • Nodes — the variables to be estimated: robot states \(\mathbf x_t\) and landmark states \(\mathbf m_j\).
  • Factors — small black dots on edges, each relating two variables through one piece of data and its model.

The two factor types

Each factor stores an error function — how much its two variables disagree with its datum:

  • Motion factor: error between the state \(\mathbf x_{t+1}\) and its motion prediction \(f(\mathbf x_t,\mathbf u_t)\): $$\mathbf e_f(\mathbf x_{t+1},\mathbf x_t)=\mathbf x_{t+1}\ominus f(\mathbf x_t,\mathbf u_t)$$
  • Observation factor: error between the observation \(\mathbf z_{t,j}\) and its prediction \(h(\mathbf x_t,\mathbf m_j)\): $$\mathbf e_h(\mathbf x_t,\mathbf m_j)=\mathbf z_{t,j}\ominus h(\mathbf x_t,\mathbf m_j)$$
The symbol \(\ominus\) means "difference that respects the geometry of the space". For flat vectors it is ordinary subtraction, \(\mathbf y\ominus\mathbf x=\mathbf y-\mathbf x\) for \(\mathbf x,\mathbf y\in\mathbb R^d\); for orientations it is the relative-rotation vector \(\mathbf y\ominus\mathbf x=2\log(\mathbf x^{-1}\mathbf y)\) for \(\mathbf x,\mathbf y\in\mathbb H_*\) — precisely the quaternion distance of section 03. One symbol, so that every formula below works on flat and curved variables alike.
06

Factor graph SLAM: front-end and back-end

A complete SLAM system is a loop between two modules:

  • Front-end: constructs the factor graph from raw data — odometry, laser-scan matching, image feature matching decide which nodes exist and which factors connect them.
  • Back-end: optimizes the graph to estimate the variables \((\mathbf x_{0:T},\{\mathbf m_j\})\), feeding the refined node positions back to the front-end.

The back-end optimization problem

Strip away the SLAM specifics and the back-end sees only a graph: variables \(\mathbf x_i\) on the vertices \(i\in\mathcal V\), and error functions \(\mathbf e(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)\) on the edges \((i,j)\in\mathcal E\). The general problem:

$$\boxed{\ \min_{\{\mathbf x_i\}}\ \sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal E}\phi_{ij}\big(\mathbf e(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)\big)\ }$$

where \(\phi_{ij}:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R\) is a distance function turning each error vector into a scalar penalty — typically the quadratic form \(\phi_{ij}(\mathbf e)=\mathbf e^\top\Omega_{ij}\,\mathbf e\) with a positive-definite matrix \(\Omega_{ij}\).

What Ω buys you
\(\Omega_{ij}\) is a trust dial per factor, and even per direction. Writing \(\Omega_{ij}=W_{ij}^\top W_{ij}\) gives \(\phi_{ij}(\mathbf e)=\lVert W_{ij}\mathbf e\rVert_2^2\) — a weighted least squares. A precise lidar factor gets a large \(\Omega\) (its errors are expensive); a sloppy wheel-odometry factor gets a small one. Probabilistically, \(\Omega_{ij}\) is the inverse covariance (information) matrix of that measurement's noise, and \(\mathbf e^\top\Omega\mathbf e\) is its Mahalanobis distance — minimizing the sum is then exactly maximum-a-posteriori estimation under Gaussian noise. Least squares is not a hack; it is Gaussian inference.
07

Pose graphs & loop closure

The most common factor graph has no landmarks at all — just poses tied together by relative-pose measurements:

  • Variables: robot poses \(T_i\in SE(3)\).
  • Measurements: relative poses \(\bar T_{ij}\) from odometry and loop closures.
  • Factors: relative pose error vectors $$\mathbf e(T_i,T_j)=\log\big(\bar T_{ij}^{-1}\,T_i^{-1}\,T_j\big)^\vee$$

Read the factor inside-out, with Lecture 3 in hand: \(T_i^{-1}T_j\) is the relative pose your current estimates predict; \(\bar T_{ij}^{-1}(T_i^{-1}T_j)\) is the leftover transform between prediction and measurement — the identity if they agree; and \(\log(\cdot)^\vee\) converts that leftover into a flat 6-vector (a twist) suitable for least squares. It is \(\ominus\) for \(SE(3)\).

Loop closure — the moment SLAM earns its keep
Odometry factors only chain consecutive poses, and a chain can be perfectly self-consistent while drifting arbitrarily far from the truth. A loop closure — recognizing a previously seen place — creates a factor between non-successive poses. That single edge contradicts the accumulated drift, and the optimizer must resolve the contradiction by bending the whole trajectory back into shape.

Pose graph optimization, with \(\phi_{ij}(\mathbf e)=\mathbf e^\top W_{ij}^\top W_{ij}\mathbf e=\lVert W_{ij}\mathbf e\rVert_2^2\):

$$\min_{\{T_i\}}\ \sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal E}\big\lVert W_{ij}\log\big(\bar T_{ij}^{-1}T_i^{-1}T_j\big)^\vee\big\rVert_2^2$$

08

Solving the graph: from descent to Levenberg–Marquardt

Collect all variables into one super-vector \(\mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf x_1^\top\cdots\mathbf x_n^\top\end{bmatrix}^\top\) and face the back-end problem \(\min_{\mathbf x}\sum\phi_{ij}(\mathbf e(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j))\). The models are nonlinear now, so no normal equations solve it in one shot. Instead:

  • Initial guess \(\mathbf x^{(0)}\): from odometry (encoders, point-cloud registration) for poses and landmark initialization (e.g., triangulation of image features) for the map. Nonconvex problems need a decent start.
  • Descent method: iterate \(\ \mathbf x^{(k+1)}=\mathbf x^{(k)}+\alpha^{(k)}\,\delta\mathbf x^{(k)}\) with some downhill direction \(\delta\mathbf x^{(k)}\).

Where the LM equation comes from

Take \(\phi_{ij}(\mathbf e)=\lVert W_{ij}\mathbf e\rVert_2^2\) and linearize each error around the current iterate: \(\mathbf e(\mathbf x^{(k)}+\delta\mathbf x)\approx\mathbf e(\mathbf x^{(k)})+J_{ij}\,\delta\mathbf x\), where

$$J_{ij}=\frac{\partial\,\mathbf e(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)}{\partial\mathbf x}\bigg|_{\mathbf x=\mathbf x^{(k)}}$$

is the Jacobian of that factor with respect to all variables (mostly zeros — only the \(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j\) columns are alive). The objective becomes a quadratic in \(\delta\mathbf x\); setting its derivative to zero gives the Gauss–Newton normal equations, and adding a damping term \(\lambda D\) gives the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm:

$$\boxed{\ \Big(\sum_{ij}J_{ij}^\top W_{ij}^\top W_{ij}J_{ij}+\lambda D\Big)\,\delta\mathbf x^{(k)}=-\sum_{ij}J_{ij}^\top W_{ij}^\top\,\mathbf e\big(\mathbf x_i^{(k)},\mathbf x_j^{(k)}\big)\ }$$
The damping \(\lambda\) is a confidence dial on the linearization. \(\lambda\to0\): pure Gauss–Newton — huge, bold steps that solve the linearized problem exactly (one step suffices if the true problem is linear, as Practice 8 shows). \(\lambda\) large: the \(\lambda D\) term dominates and the step shrinks toward a small gradient-descent step — slow but safe. LM adapts \(\lambda\) on the fly: shrink it when a step lowers the cost, grow it when a step fails. That is exactly what the widget above runs.

Sparsity: the structure that makes SLAM fast

Why can libraries optimize graphs with millions of variables? Because almost every entry of the linear system is zero, and the zeros come straight from the graph. Each factor touches two variables, so each block-row of the stacked Jacobian \(J\) has exactly two nonzero blocks. For the lecture's 7-pose graph — odometry chain \(\bar T_{12},\dots,\bar T_{67}\) plus one loop closure \(\bar T_{52}\):

The Hessian \(J^\top J\) inherits the pattern: its \((i,j)\) block is nonzero only if poses \(i\) and \(j\) share a factor — the Hessian sparsity pattern is the adjacency matrix of the graph. An odometry chain gives a tridiagonal Hessian; the loop closure adds the two amber off-diagonal blocks at \((2,5)\) and \((5,2)\). And \(J^\top J\) is exactly the information matrix of the estimate — large where the data pin variables down, structured where they are coupled. Sparse Cholesky solvers feast on this structure; that is the entire reason million-variable SLAM runs in real time.

Landmark-based SLAM

Put the map back in. The variables are now poses \(\{T_t\}\) and landmarks \(\{\mathbf m_j\}\), and the objective combines both factor types — relative-pose factors between consecutive poses, observation factors between a pose and each landmark it saw:

$$\min_{\{T_t\},\{\mathbf m_j\}}\ \sum_t\big\lVert W_{ij}\log\big(\bar T_{t,t+1}^{-1}T_t^{-1}T_{t+1}\big)^\vee\big\rVert_2^2+\sum_{t,j}\big\lVert V_{ij}\big(\mathbf z_{t,j}-h(T_t,\mathbf m_j)\big)\big\rVert_2^2$$

with \(h\) being, e.g., the camera or lidar observation model of Lecture 4, and \(W,V\) the weight (square-root information) matrices of each factor type.

The arrow-shaped Hessian

Order the variables poses-first, landmarks-second, and the Hessian takes a characteristic shape:

The huge landmark–landmark block is block-diagonal — no factor ever connects two landmarks directly. That makes it trivially invertible, which is exactly what the Schur complement of the next section exploits: real systems eliminate the (cheap) landmark block first and solve a small pose system. With thousands of poses and landmarks the overall Hessian is overwhelmingly empty — the lecture shows a real one that is white space except for a thin diagonal and a fringe of pose–landmark couplings.

11

Variable marginalization: the Schur complement

What if we only need a subset of the variables — say the poses but not the landmarks, or the current pose but not the old ones? We could solve the full system and throw the rest away, but there is a cheaper, exact route. Start from the Gauss–Newton normal equations \(J^\top J\,\delta\mathbf x=-J^\top\mathbf e\), and split the variables into the part to keep, \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_a\), and the part to remove, \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_b\):

$$\begin{bmatrix}\Omega_{aa}&\Omega_{ab}\\\Omega_{ab}^\top&\Omega_{bb}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf x}_a\\\tilde{\mathbf x}_b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf c_a\\\mathbf c_b\end{bmatrix}$$

Block Gaussian elimination, step by step

Step 1. The second block row says \(\Omega_{ab}^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_a+\Omega_{bb}\tilde{\mathbf x}_b=\mathbf c_b\) — so \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_b\) could be expressed from \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_a\). To remove it from the first row, pre-multiply the whole system by a block elimination matrix:

$$\begin{bmatrix}I&-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\\0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Omega_{aa}&\Omega_{ab}\\\Omega_{ab}^\top&\Omega_{bb}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf x}_a\\\tilde{\mathbf x}_b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I&-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\\0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf c_a\\\mathbf c_b\end{bmatrix}$$

Step 2. Multiply out. The first row becomes \(\big(\Omega_{aa}-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\Omega_{ab}^\top\big)\tilde{\mathbf x}_a+\big(\Omega_{ab}-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\Omega_{bb}\big)\tilde{\mathbf x}_b\) — and the \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_b\) coefficient cancels to zero by construction:

$$\begin{bmatrix}\Omega_{aa}-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\Omega_{ab}^\top&0\\\Omega_{ab}^\top&\Omega_{bb}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf x}_a\\\tilde{\mathbf x}_b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf c_a-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\mathbf c_b\\\mathbf c_b\end{bmatrix}$$

Step 3. Read off the decoupled small system — \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_a\) solves it alone:

$$\boxed{\ \big(\Omega_{aa}-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\Omega_{ab}^\top\big)\,\tilde{\mathbf x}_a=\mathbf c_a-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\mathbf c_b\ }$$

The matrix \(\Omega_{aa}-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\Omega_{ab}^\top\) is the Schur complement of \(\Omega_{bb}\). Solving it gives exactly the same \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_a\) as the full system — nothing is approximated, the \(b\) variables are merely eliminated.

The probabilistic reading: marginalization

The same algebra has a probability meaning. The normal equations describe a Gaussian over \((\tilde{\mathbf x}_a,\tilde{\mathbf x}_b)\) with information matrix \(\Omega\) and information vector \(\mathbf c\):

$$\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf x}_a\\\tilde{\mathbf x}_b\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal N\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}\Omega_{aa}&\Omega_{ab}\\\Omega_{ab}^\top&\Omega_{bb}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\!\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf c_a\\\mathbf c_b\end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix}\Omega_{aa}&\Omega_{ab}\\\Omega_{ab}^\top&\Omega_{bb}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\right)$$

Integrating \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_b\) out gives the marginal of \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_a\) — and its information matrix is precisely the Schur complement:

$$p(\tilde{\mathbf x}_a)=\int p(\tilde{\mathbf x}_a,\tilde{\mathbf x}_b)\,d\tilde{\mathbf x}_b=\phi\Big(\tilde{\mathbf x}_a;\ \big(\Omega_{aa}-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\Omega_{ab}^\top\big)^{-1}\big(\mathbf c_a-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\mathbf c_b\big),\ \big(\Omega_{aa}-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\Omega_{ab}^\top\big)^{-1}\Big)$$
The price: fill-in. The correction term \(\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\Omega_{ab}^\top\) is generally dense over the neighbors of the eliminated variable: marginalizing creates nonzero off-diagonals between every pair of variables that touched it. In graph terms, eliminating a node connects all of its neighbors into a clique. Sparsity — the thing that made SLAM fast — erodes with every marginalization. Try it below.

12

Smoothing vs filtering

Marginalization defines a spectrum of estimators — trade variables for density:

approachvariables keptHessian
Smoothing
≡ MAP optimization		many — entire robot trajectory and mapsparse \(J^\top J\)
Fixed-lag smoothingfewer — only a recent time windowdenser, after Schur-marginalizing old variables
Filteringfewest — only the current pose and landmarksdensest, after marginalizing all old variables

Smoothing keeps every variable and enjoys maximal sparsity — modern SLAM's default, and exactly the factor-graph optimization of this lecture. Filtering (the Kalman family, coming in later lectures) keeps almost nothing and pays with a dense information matrix — every marginalized pose smeared its correlations onto whatever remained. Fixed-lag smoothing sits in between. There is no free lunch: fewer variables ⇔ denser couplings, by the fill-in mechanism you just played with.

Problem 1linear least squares

A scalar robot has \(F=G=H=1\), \(x_0=0\), inputs \(u_0=u_1=1\), and measurements \(z_1=1.2\), \(z_2=1.9\). (a) Write the localization cost \(c(x_1,x_2)\) and the stacked form \(\lVert A\mathbf x+\mathbf b\rVert_2^2\). (b) Solve exactly via the normal equations.

Show solution

(a) Two measurement residuals and two motion residuals:

$$c=(1.2-x_1)^2+(1.9-x_2)^2+(x_1-0-1)^2+(x_2-x_1-1)^2$$

Stacking (measurement rows first, then sign-flipped motion rows):

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\-1&0\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\qquad \mathbf b=\begin{bmatrix}-1.2\\-1.9\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

(Check row 3: \(-x_1+1=-(x_1-1)\) ✓ same square; row 4: \(x_1-x_2+1=-(x_2-x_1-1)\) ✓.)

(b) Normal equations \(A^\top A\,\mathbf x=-A^\top\mathbf b\):

$$A^\top A=\begin{bmatrix}3&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\qquad -A^\top\mathbf b=\begin{bmatrix}1.2\\2.9\end{bmatrix}$$

With \(\det=5\): \(x_1=\tfrac{2(1.2)+1(2.9)}{5}=\tfrac{5.3}{5}=1.06\), \(x_2=\tfrac{3(2.9)+1(1.2)}{5}=\tfrac{9.9}{5}=1.98\).

Interpret. Dead reckoning says \((1,2)\); the measurements say \((1.2,1.9)\); the optimum \((1.06,\,1.98)\) is the weighted compromise. No single factor is satisfied exactly — least squares spreads the disagreement.

Problem 2gradient descent

For Problem 1, start at the dead-reckoning guess \(\mathbf x^{(0)}=(1,2)\) and take one gradient-descent step with \(\alpha=0.1\), using \(\nabla c=2A^\top(A\mathbf x+\mathbf b)\).

Show solution

Step 1 — Residual. \(A\mathbf x^{(0)}+\mathbf b=(1-1.2,\ 2-1.9,\ -1+1,\ 1-2+1)=(-0.2,\ 0.1,\ 0,\ 0)\). The motion residuals are zero — dead reckoning satisfies the motion model perfectly; only the sensors object.

Step 2 — Gradient.

$$\nabla c=2A^\top\begin{bmatrix}-0.2\\0.1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}=2\begin{bmatrix}-0.2\\0.1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-0.4\\0.2\end{bmatrix}$$

Step 3 — Step. \(\mathbf x^{(1)}=(1,2)-0.1\,(-0.4,\ 0.2)=(1.04,\ 1.98)\).

One step already lands next to the exact optimum \((1.06, 1.98)\) — on a well-conditioned quadratic, descent moves fast at first and crawls at the end. The widget in section 02 is this exact computation with \(T=8\).

Problem 3orientation model

A body is rolled \(90^\circ\) about its x-axis: \(q=\big[\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2},\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2},0,0\big]\). Compute the predicted accelerometer reading \(h(q)=q^{-1}\circ[0,-g\mathbf e_3]\circ q\) (take \(g=9.81\)).

Show solution

Step 1 — Recognize the sandwich. \(q^{-1}\circ[0,\mathbf v]\circ q\) applies \(R(q)^\top\) — it expresses the world vector \(\mathbf v=-g\mathbf e_3=(0,0,-9.81)\) in the body frame.

Step 2 — Rotation matrix route. \(R(q)=R_x(90^\circ)\), so \(R^\top=R_x(-90^\circ)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&-1&0\end{bmatrix}\), and

$$h(q)=R_x(-90^\circ)\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\-9.81\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\-9.81\\0\end{bmatrix}$$

Step 3 — Quaternion route (verify). \(q^{-1}=[\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2},-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2},0,0]\). Multiply out \(q^{-1}\circ[0,(0,0,-g)]\circ q\) with the product rule \([a_s,\mathbf a][b_s,\mathbf b]=[a_sb_s-\mathbf a^\top\mathbf b,\ a_s\mathbf b+b_s\mathbf a+\mathbf a\times\mathbf b]\); the result is \([0,(0,-9.81,0)]\). ✓

Interpret. After rolling \(90^\circ\), the body's y-axis points down, so gravity shows up along \(-y\). This is how the accelerometer reveals roll and pitch — and why it says nothing about yaw (rotations about the gravity axis leave \(h(q)\) unchanged, which is why Project 1 needs the gyro for heading).

Problem 4quaternion distance

The gyro predicts \(f(q_t,\tau\mathbf u_t)=\big[\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2},0,0,\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\big]\) (a \(90^\circ\) yaw), but the current estimate is \(q_{t+1}=\big[\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},0,0,\tfrac12\big]\) (a \(60^\circ\) yaw). Compute the motion-factor error \(\mathbf e=2\log\!\big(q_{t+1}^{-1}\circ f\big)\) and its norm.

Show solution

Step 1 — Relative rotation. \(q_{t+1}^{-1}=[\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},0,0,-\tfrac12]\) (a \(-60^\circ\) yaw). Both quaternions rotate about z, so the product just adds half-angles: \(-30^\circ+45^\circ=15^\circ\):

$$q_{t+1}^{-1}\circ f=[\cos15^\circ,\ 0,\ 0,\ \sin15^\circ]$$

Step 2 — Log and double. \(\log\) of a unit quaternion returns the half-angle axis vector, so \(\mathbf e=2\log(\cdot)=\big(0,0,\tfrac{\pi}{6}\big)\).

Step 3 — Norm. \(\lVert\mathbf e\rVert=\tfrac{\pi}{6}\approx0.524\) rad \(=30^\circ\) — exactly the yaw discrepancy between estimate and prediction. The factor is the geometric difference, as promised: \(d(q_{t+1},f)=90^\circ-60^\circ\).

Problem 5panorama

A camera with the panorama pipeline of section 04 has orientation \(R=R_z(40^\circ)\) (pure yaw). Where do (a) the image-center pixel \((\lambda,\phi)=(0^\circ,0^\circ)\) and (b) the top-center pixel \((0^\circ,22.5^\circ)\) land on the panorama?

Show solution

(a) Spherical→Cartesian: \((0,0)\mapsto(1,0,0)\). Rotate: \(R_z(40^\circ)(1,0,0)=(\cos40^\circ,\sin40^\circ,0)\). Cartesian→spherical: \(\lambda'=\operatorname{atan2}(\sin40^\circ,\cos40^\circ)=40^\circ\), \(\phi'=\arcsin0=0^\circ\). Lands at \((40^\circ,0^\circ)\).

(b) \((0,22.5^\circ)\mapsto(\cos22.5^\circ,0,\sin22.5^\circ)\). Yaw only mixes x and y, leaving z alone: \(R_z(40^\circ)\) gives \((\cos22.5^\circ\cos40^\circ,\ \cos22.5^\circ\sin40^\circ,\ \sin22.5^\circ)\) — so \(\phi'=22.5^\circ\) unchanged, and \(\lambda'=40^\circ\). Lands at \((40^\circ,\,22.5^\circ)\).

Conclusion. Pure yaw shifts the whole image horizontally on the panorama by exactly the yaw angle and preserves every latitude — the proof behind what the panorama-painter widget shows when you sweep ψ.

Problem 6pose-graph factor

In \(SE(2)\), the odometry between two poses was measured as \(\bar T_{12}=(2,0,90^\circ)\) (forward 2 m, turn left \(90^\circ\)). The current estimates are \(T_1=(0,0,0^\circ)\) and \(T_2=(1.9,\,0.2,\,90^\circ)\). Compute the factor error \(\mathbf e=\log(\bar T_{12}^{-1}T_1^{-1}T_2)^\vee\).

Show solution

Step 1 — Predicted relative pose. \(T_1\) is the identity, so \(T_1^{-1}T_2=(1.9,\,0.2,\,90^\circ)\).

Step 2 — Invert the measurement. For a 2D pose \((R,\mathbf p)\), the inverse is \((R^\top,-R^\top\mathbf p)\): rotation \(-90^\circ\), translation \(-R(-90^\circ)(2,0)=-(0,-2)=(0,2)\). So \(\bar T_{12}^{-1}=(0,\,2,\,-90^\circ)\).

Step 3 — Compose. Rotation: \(-90^\circ+90^\circ=0^\circ\). Translation: \((0,2)+R(-90^\circ)(1.9,0.2)=(0,2)+(0.2,-1.9)=(0.2,\,0.1)\).

Step 4 — Log. The discrepancy pose is \((0.2,\,0.1,\,0^\circ)\) — a pure translation, whose \(SE(2)\) log is exactly the translation:

$$\mathbf e=(0.2,\ 0.1,\ 0)$$

Zero rotation error, 20 cm and 10 cm of position disagreement — that is what the optimizer will try to squeeze out, weighted by \(W_{12}\).

Problem 7loop closure

A 1D pose graph: \(p_0=0\) fixed, odometry factors \(p_1-p_0\approx1.1\) and \(p_2-p_1\approx1.05\), and a loop-closure factor \(p_2-p_0\approx2.0\), all with weight 1. (a) Find the optimal \((p_1,p_2)\). (b) What happens as the closure weight \(w\to\infty\)?

Show solution

(a) Minimize \(c=(p_1-1.1)^2+(p_2-p_1-1.05)^2+(p_2-2)^2\). Setting both partials to zero:

$$\tfrac{\partial c}{\partial p_1}=0:\ 2p_1-p_2=0.05\qquad \tfrac{\partial c}{\partial p_2}=0:\ 2p_2-p_1=3.05$$

Substituting \(p_2=2p_1-0.05\) into the second: \(3p_1=3.15\Rightarrow p_1=1.05,\ p_2=2.05\).

Interpret. Dead reckoning says \((1.1,\,2.15)\); the closure says \(p_2=2.0\). The optimum lands at \((1.05,\,2.05)\): the 0.15 m of contradiction got spread across all three factors (each residual ends up at exactly \(\pm0.05\)). Loop closure does not just fix the last pose — it redistributes drift along the whole chain, which is what you see in the pose-graph widget.

(b) With weight \(w\) on the closure, the second equation becomes \((1+w)p_2-p_1=1.05+2w\). As \(w\to\infty\): \(p_2\to2.0\) exactly, and the first equation gives \(p_1\to(0.05+2)/2=1.025\). An infinitely trusted closure becomes a hard constraint; the odometry factors split the remaining disagreement between themselves.

Problem 8Gauss–Newton

For Problem 7 (weight 1), start at dead reckoning \(\mathbf p^{(0)}=(1.1,\,2.15)\) and take one Gauss–Newton step: build \(J\) and \(\mathbf e\), solve \(J^\top J\,\delta=-J^\top\mathbf e\). Where do you land? What would the LM damping \(\lambda D\) change?

Show solution

Step 1 — Residuals at \(\mathbf p^{(0)}\). \(e_1=p_1-1.1=0\), \(e_2=p_2-p_1-1.05=0\), \(e_3=p_2-2=0.15\). Only the closure complains.

Step 2 — Jacobian. Rows are \(\partial e_i/\partial(p_1,p_2)\): \(J=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\-1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\).

Step 3 — Normal equations.

$$J^\top J=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\qquad -J^\top\mathbf e=\begin{bmatrix}0\\-0.15\end{bmatrix}$$

With \(\det=3\): \(\delta=\big(\tfrac{2\cdot0+1\cdot(-0.15)}{3},\ \tfrac{1\cdot0+2\cdot(-0.15)}{3}\big)=(-0.05,\,-0.1)\).

Step 4 — Land. \(\mathbf p^{(1)}=(1.1-0.05,\ 2.15-0.1)=(1.05,\,2.05)\) — the exact optimum of Problem 7, in one step. The factors are linear, so the linearization is exact and Gauss–Newton finishes immediately.

LM. Adding \(\lambda D\) inflates the diagonal, shrinking and rotating the step toward gradient descent: safer when the linearization is untrustworthy (real pose graphs, where \(\mathbf e\) involves \(\log\) and rotations), at the cost of more iterations. \(\lambda\) adapts: success → shrink it toward Gauss–Newton; failure → grow it.

Problem 9Schur complement

Two poses both observe one landmark (no direct pose–pose factor). The normal equations are

$$\begin{bmatrix}2&0&-1\\0&2&-1\\-1&-1&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde x_1\\\tilde x_2\\\tilde x_b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

with \(b\) the landmark. (a) Marginalize the landmark via the Schur complement and solve for \((\tilde x_1,\tilde x_2)\). (b) Verify against the full solve. (c) Where is the fill-in?

Show solution

(a) Blocks: \(\Omega_{aa}=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}\), \(\Omega_{ab}=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\), \(\Omega_{bb}=3\), \(\mathbf c_a=(1,1)\), \(c_b=1\). Schur complement:

$$\Omega_{aa}-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}\Omega_{ab}^\top=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}-\frac13\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac53&-\tfrac13\\-\tfrac13&\tfrac53\end{bmatrix}$$ $$\mathbf c_a-\Omega_{ab}\Omega_{bb}^{-1}c_b=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}+\frac13\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac43\\\tfrac43\end{bmatrix}$$

By symmetry \(\tilde x_1=\tilde x_2=s\): \(\big(\tfrac53-\tfrac13\big)s=\tfrac43\Rightarrow s=1\). So \((\tilde x_1,\tilde x_2)=(1,1)\).

(b) Full system: try \((1,1,1)\): row 1: \(2-1=1\) ✓, row 3: \(-1-1+3=1\) ✓. Same answer — marginalization is exact.

(c) \(\Omega_{aa}\) was diagonal — the poses were not directly connected. The Schur correction \(\tfrac13\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\) put \(-\tfrac13\) in the off-diagonal: eliminating the landmark created an edge between the two poses that saw it. That \(-\tfrac13\) is fill-in — the algebraic shadow of the clique.

Problem 10fill-in / cliques

In a landmark SLAM graph, pose \(\mathbf x_t\) is connected to landmarks \(L_1,L_2,L_3\) and to pose \(\mathbf x_{t+1}\); independently, \(\mathbf x_{t+1}\) is connected to \(L_3\). If \(\mathbf x_t\) is marginalized, how many edges does the resulting clique have, and how many are new?

Show solution

Step 1 — Neighbors. \(\mathbf x_t\) touches four variables: \(\{L_1,L_2,L_3,\mathbf x_{t+1}\}\).

Step 2 — Clique size. Elimination connects every pair of neighbors: \(\binom42=6\) edges total — \(L_1L_2,\ L_1L_3,\ L_2L_3,\ \mathbf x_{t+1}L_1,\ \mathbf x_{t+1}L_2,\ \mathbf x_{t+1}L_3\).

Step 3 — New ones. Only \(\mathbf x_{t+1}\)–\(L_3\) existed before, so 5 of the 6 are new fill-in — including landmark–landmark edges, which a fresh SLAM graph never has. Each marginalized pose densifies the map block a little more; do it every step (filtering) and the landmark block becomes fully dense. That is the sparsity cost quantified.

Problem 11smoothing vs filtering

A SLAM session has poses \(\mathbf x_0,\dots,\mathbf x_{1000}\) (3 DoF each) and 500 landmarks (2 DoF each). Count the variables kept by (a) full smoothing and (b) filtering (current pose + landmarks). (c) Which has the sparser information matrix, and why?

Show solution

(a) Smoothing keeps everything: \(1001\times3+500\times2=3003+1000=4003\) scalar variables.

(b) Filtering keeps one pose and the map: \(3+1000=1003\) variables — four times fewer.

(c) Smoothing, by far. Its information matrix has nonzeros only where factors exist — the tridiagonal-plus-fringe arrow shape of section 10, with a diagonal landmark block. Filtering reached its small size by Schur-marginalizing 1000 old poses, and each marginalization spread fill-in among the variables those poses touched; after all of them, the landmark–landmark block is essentially dense (\(\sim1000\times1000\) nonzeros). Fewer variables, denser couplings — the trade-off has no free lunch, which is exactly why modern SLAM defaults to smoothing and sparse solvers.
