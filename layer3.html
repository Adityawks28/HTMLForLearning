Vision & Language: foundation models

The pretrained giants a robot borrows for eyes and inner voice: Vision Transformers and self-supervised backbones, the full large-language-model stack from tokenizer to RLHF, and the fusion architectures that bolt them together. Built on Layers 1–2; pointed straight at the VLA.

Scroll to begin · 8 chapters

Layer 2 ended with a promise: a vision-language-action model is "a VLM with an action head." This layer pays the debt — it builds the VLM. The plot has three acts: teach transformers to see (Ch 1–3), follow the language model from raw bytes to helpful assistant (Ch 4–6), then weld eye to brain (Ch 7–8). Almost nothing here is new mathematics; it is Layer 1's probability and Layer 2's attention, deployed at scale with a few brilliant tricks.

Chapter 01

Images Become Tokens

For a decade, "vision model" meant convolution. Then the Vision Transformer (ViT, 2020) asked a heretical question: what if an image is just a sentence — a sequence of patch-tokens — and the same transformer that reads text simply reads it? At sufficient scale, the answer turned out to be yes, and it changed everything downstream.

1.1 · Patches as words

Definition — patch embedding

Split an $H\times W$ image into non-overlapping $P\times P$ patches. Each patch is flattened into a vector of $P^2 C$ pixel values ($C$ channels) and mapped by one learned matrix $E$ into the model dimension $d$:

$$\mathbf{z}_i = E\,\operatorname{vec}(\text{patch}_i)+\mathbf{p}_i,\qquad i=1,\dots,n,\qquad n=\frac{HW}{P^2}$$

where $\mathbf{p}_i$ is a learned position embedding. A $224\times224$ image with $P{=}16$ becomes $n=196$ tokens — a paragraph's worth of "visual words." A learnable [CLS] token is prepended to collect a whole-image summary.

Intuition — cutting a photo into scrabble tiles

A patch is to an image what a word is to a sentence: a chunk small enough to have local meaning (an edge, an eye, a wheel-rim) and large enough not to be pixel noise. Once the photo is a bag of tiles plus a position label on each, the transformer needs no new ideas — attention already knows how to let every tile look at every other tile and ask "are we part of the same thing?" That question, asked in parallel across 196 tiles and 12+ layers, is visual perception in a ViT.

Derivation — why position embeddings are not optional
1

Recall Layer 2: attention computes $\operatorname{softmax}(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d_k})\,V$ from token vectors alone. Shuffle the input tokens with any permutation $\pi$ and every dot product $q_i\cdot k_j$ is unchanged — only re-indexed. The output is the same set of vectors, shuffled the same way: attention is permutation-equivariant.

2

So without $\mathbf{p}_i$, a ViT literally cannot tell an image from a jigsaw-scrambled version of it — the sky could sit below the grass. Adding a distinct learned vector per position breaks the symmetry: now "blue patch at the top" and "blue patch at the bottom" are different tokens.

3

Cost of the token view: self-attention compares every pair, so compute scales as $O(n^2 d)$. Halving the patch size quadruples $n$ and ×16's the attention cost — which is why patch size is the first dial anyone touches, and why high-resolution vision is expensive. Feel this trade-off in the demo.

1.2 · The Vision Transformer, assembled

After patch embedding, a ViT is the Layer 2 transformer encoder, unchanged: $L$ blocks of (LayerNorm → multi-head self-attention → residual) then (LayerNorm → MLP → residual), with a linear classifier read off the [CLS] token. ViT-Base: $P{=}16$, $d{=}768$, $L{=}12$, 12 heads, ~86M parameters. That architectural boringness is the point — one architecture for text and pixels is what later makes single-model fusion (Ch 7) natural.

You already built this

Every component here was derived in Layer 2: attention and $\sqrt{d_k}$ (Ch 5), residuals and LayerNorm (Ch 2–3), the MLP block (Ch 1). The only new idea in ViT is the tokenizer — the first 10 lines of the model.

1.3 · Inductive bias vs. data

Intuition — hard-wired instinct vs. learned experience

A CNN is born knowing two facts about images: nearby pixels matter to each other (locality) and a cat in the corner is still a cat (translation weight-sharing). These inductive biases are training wheels — superb when data is scarce. A ViT is born knowing nothing: patch 3 has no built-in idea it neighbors patch 4. On small datasets it underperforms CNNs; but feed it tens of millions of images and it learns locality where useful and ignores it where not — early heads discover convolution-like local attention on their own, late heads attend globally. The recurring lesson of the foundation-model era, first taught here: at sufficient scale, learned structure beats hand-designed structure. Bet on data.

Chapter 02

Self-Supervised Vision

ViTs are data-hungry, and labeled data is the most expensive substance in machine learning. Self-supervised learning (SSL) removes the bottleneck: let the image label itself. The result — backbones like DINO and DINOv2 — is the visual encoder that robots actually borrow, trained on raw, unlabeled internet imagery.

2.1 · Learning without labels

Intuition — learning by predicting your own redactions

Layer 2 met two SSL families: contrastive (pull two augmented crops of the same image together — SimCLR/CLIP-style) and masked (hide patches, predict them — MAE). Both run the same con: take one data point, manufacture a question and its answer from it, and grade yourself. The supervision was inside the data all along — what is hidden or transformed is predictable from what remains, if and only if you understand the content. Understanding is forced as a side effect.

Definition — the augmentation-invariance objective

Given augmentations $u=\operatorname{aug}_1(x)$, $v=\operatorname{aug}_2(x)$ (random crops, color jitter, blur) of one image, learn an encoder $f$ whose outputs agree:

$$\min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_x\,\big[\,\mathcal{D}\big(f_\theta(u),\,g(v)\big)\,\big]$$

for some distance $\mathcal{D}$ and target-producer $g$. Everything interesting lives in the choice of $g$ — because the naive choice $g=f_\theta$ has a fatal bug, derived below.

2.2 · DINO — learn from a calmer copy of yourself

Definition — self-distillation with a momentum teacher

DINO trains two networks of identical architecture. The student $\theta_s$ sees one crop and is trained by gradient descent to match the output distribution of the teacher $\theta_t$ on another crop (cross-entropy between their softmax outputs). The teacher is never trained — it is an exponential moving average (EMA) of the student's own history:

$$\theta_t \;\leftarrow\; m\,\theta_t + (1-m)\,\theta_s,\qquad m\approx 0.996$$
Intuition — your past self, smoothed

The teacher is the student's own parameters, averaged over thousands of recent steps — a calmer, steadier version of itself, immune to any single noisy gradient. The student chases this stable target; the target slowly absorbs the student's progress; repeat. It sounds circular — and the demo below shows why it isn't: an EMA filters the noise out of a trajectory while keeping its direction, so the student is always chasing a cleaner version of where it was already going. DINO's reward for this trick is famous: with zero labels, its attention maps segment objects — the [CLS] token's attention literally outlines the dog. Perception emerged from self-agreement alone.

At $m=0.5$ the teacher just shadows the noise; at $m=0.99$ it glides — same information, no jitter. Push $m\to0.999$ and see the cost: the teacher lags far behind a fast-moving student. The momentum dial trades stability against freshness.

2.3 · The collapse problem — and the two-line fix

Derivation — why "agree with yourself" can implode
1

The objective only rewards agreement. Consider the cheating solution: both networks output the same constant distribution $c$ for every input. Then student and teacher agree perfectly on every pair, the loss hits its minimum — and the representation carries zero information. Nothing in the loss forbids it. This is collapse, and naive self-distillation reliably finds it.

2

Fix 1 — centering. Subtract a running mean $\mathbf{c}$ from the teacher's logits before its softmax: $\operatorname{softmax}\big((z_t-\mathbf{c})/\tau_t\big)$. If the teacher starts always shouting dimension $k$, the center absorbs it and the advantage is cancelled — no single output can dominate.

3

Fix 2 — sharpening. Centering alone pushes toward the uniform distribution (a subtler collapse: maximal entropy, still zero information — recall Layer 1, Ch 8). So the teacher also uses a low temperature $\tau_t \approx 0.04$, sharpening its targets toward confident peaks. Centering fights peaky collapse, sharpening fights uniform collapse; held in tension, the only way to minimize the loss is to output different, confident distributions for different images — i.e., to actually encode content.

Where you'll meet this again

DINOv2 scales this recipe (plus a masked-patch objective) into the default plug-in vision backbone — frozen DINOv2 features power depth estimation, segmentation, and many robot perception stacks. EMA targets reappear in Layer 4's reinforcement learning as "target networks" stabilizing Q-learning: the same calmer-copy-of-yourself trick, keeping a different bootstrap from chasing its own tail.

Chapter 03

Vision Tasks, Rebuilt

Classification says "there is a cat." Robots need more: where is the cat, which pixels are cat, can I ask about an arbitrary region? Two transformer-era systems rebuilt these tasks from first principles — DETR turned detection into set prediction, and SAM turned segmentation into a promptable, reusable foundation skill.

3.1 · DETR — detection as set prediction

Classic detectors were pipelines of heuristics: thousands of anchor boxes, then non-maximum suppression to delete duplicates. DETR (2020) replaced the pipeline with a question: detection's true output is a set of (box, class) pairs — so predict a set, directly.

Definition — DETR's recipe

A CNN/ViT encodes the image into tokens. A transformer decoder receives $N$ learned object queries (e.g. $N{=}100$ blank slots); each query cross-attends into the image tokens and emerges as one prediction $(\hat p_i,\hat b_i)$ — a class distribution and a box. Slots that find nothing predict the special class $\varnothing$ ("no object").

Derivation — bipartite matching makes the loss well-defined
1

The puzzle: predictions are a set (no order), ground truth is a set. Which prediction should be graded against which truth? If slot 7 and slot 23 both claim the same dog, who owns it? An ordering-based loss would be arbitrary.

2

Answer: grade against the best possible one-to-one assignment. Pad ground truth with $\varnothing$ to size $N$, define a pairwise cost (how badly prediction $i$ explains truth $j$):

$$\mathcal{C}_{ij}=-\hat p_i(c_j)+\mathcal{L}_{\text{box}}(\hat b_i,b_j)$$

and find the permutation minimizing total cost:

$$\hat\sigma=\arg\min_{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{C}_{i,\sigma(i)}$$

solved exactly by the Hungarian algorithm. Then train with the ordinary losses under that assignment.

3

The matching is one-to-one, so two slots can never both be credited for one dog — duplicates become costly and the network learns not to produce them. Non-maximum suppression, the hand-written deduplicator, is no longer needed: the loss itself enforces uniqueness. A heuristic became a theorem about assignments.

Intuition — the seating chart

Think of predictions as guests and ground-truth objects as seats (extra guests get the $\varnothing$ standing-room). The loss asks: if everyone sat in the best possible seat, how unhappy would the room be? — and grades that arrangement. Each guest learns to claim a different seat, because sharing is impossible by construction. Drag the guests in the demo and watch the chart re-solve.

3.2 · SAM — segmentation as a promptable skill

Definition — the Segment Anything Model

SAM (2023) decomposes segmentation into three parts: a heavy ViT image encoder run once per image; a featherweight prompt encoder for points, boxes, or coarse masks; and a fast mask decoder that cross-attends prompt against image embedding and emits a pixel mask in milliseconds. Trained on ~1.1B masks (SA-1B), it segments objects it has never been told about.

Intuition — point at it

SAM's interface is the oldest one humans have: pointing. "Segment the thing here." That makes it a foundation skill rather than a task-specific model — and because the expensive encoding happens once, you can ask many questions of one image cheaply. For robotics this pattern is gold: a VLA that wants "the red block's pixels" can point a coordinate (or pipe in a detector's box) and get a mask, no retraining. Note the shape of the design — big frozen encoder + small promptable decoder — because Chapter 7 reveals it as the recurring blueprint of the foundation-model era.

Where you'll meet this again

DETR's "learned queries that each claim one output slot" reappears in robot policies that emit action chunks, and in every perception stack that needs duplicate-free object lists for planning (Layer 5). SAM-style promptability — one frozen brain, many cheap questions — is how Layer 6's systems keep real-time budgets.

Chapter 04

The LLM Stack I — Tokens & the Objective

Now the other hemisphere. A large language model is two decisions stacked on a transformer: how text becomes tokens, and what the model is asked to do with them. Both decisions are simpler than their consequences.

4.1 · Byte-pair encoding — a learned dictionary

Characters make sequences too long; whole words make the vocabulary unbounded (and "unhappiness" would share nothing with "happy"). Byte-pair encoding (BPE) finds the middle: learn the vocabulary from data, by compression.

Derivation — the BPE algorithm (it fits in three steps)
1

Start with the alphabet: every word in the corpus is a sequence of single characters. The initial vocabulary is just the characters.

2

Count and merge. Find the most frequent adjacent pair of tokens in the corpus (say t+h). Add the merged token th to the vocabulary, and replace every occurrence. Record the merge rule.

3

Repeat step 2 for a budget of $V$ merges (GPT-style models: tens of thousands). Frequent words fuse into single tokens; rare words remain spelled from pieces — so the vocabulary is finite, yet no string is unrepresentable. To tokenize new text, replay the merge rules in learned order. That's the whole algorithm: greedy compression, run once, frozen forever.

Intuition — and why you should care about its scars

BPE is a frequency mirror of its training corpus, and the model inherits its scars: rare words shatter into many tokens (costing context and quality), numbers split unpredictably (part of why arithmetic is hard for LLMs), and whitespace/code conventions tokenize oddly. When an LLM behaves strangely, the first suspect is often not the brain but the eyes — how the text was tokenized. Type in the demo and develop the instinct.

4.2 · The next-token objective — one loss to rule them all

Derivation — the objective from Layer 1's product rule
1

Any joint distribution factorizes exactly by the chain rule of probability (Layer 1, Ch 4 — applied repeatedly):

$$P(x_1,\dots,x_T)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}P\big(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1}\big)$$

No approximation: modeling "the next token given the past," for every position, is modeling the full distribution over all text.

2

Train by maximum likelihood (Layer 1, Ch 5): minimize the negative log-likelihood, which is exactly cross-entropy (Layer 1, Ch 8) between the data and the model at every position:

$$\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P_\theta\big(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}\big)$$
3

Quality is reported as perplexity $=e^{\mathcal{L}}$ — "on average, the model is as uncertain as a fair choice among $e^{\mathcal{L}}$ tokens." Every loss concept here was built in Layer 1; the only news is the target: all of human text.

Intuition — autocomplete as a world model

"Predict the next token" sounds clerical. But predict the next token of "The capital of France is" and you must store geography. Of "137 + 285 =" — arithmetic. Of "She hid the key because" — a theory of motive. Text is a shadow cast by the world; compressing the shadow well forces a model of the caster. That is the bet of the LLM era, and Layers 1–2 gave you every tool it is built from: the objective is MLE, the loss is cross-entropy, the machine is a transformer.

Chapter 05

The LLM Stack II — Decoding at Scale

Two engineering facts shaped the modern LLM more than any architecture choice: a cache that makes generation affordable, and an empirical law that tells you how to spend a compute budget. Both are worth deriving, because both will constrain every robot brain in Layers 4–6.

5.1 · Decoder-only generation & the KV cache

Definition — causal (decoder-only) attention

A decoder-only transformer applies a causal mask: position $t$ may attend only to positions $1..t$. Training is fully parallel (all $T$ next-token predictions at once, each seeing only its past); generation is sequential — sample a token, append, repeat.

Derivation — what caching saves, exactly
1

To generate token $t{+}1$, attention needs the new query $\mathbf{q}_{t+1}$ dotted against keys/values of all previous tokens. Naively, recompute every $\mathbf{k}_i,\mathbf{v}_i$ each step: step $t$ costs $t$ projections, so a $T$-token generation costs

$$\sum_{t=1}^{T} t=\frac{T(T+1)}{2}=O(T^2)\ \text{projections per layer.}$$
2

But $\mathbf{k}_i$ and $\mathbf{v}_i$ depend only on token $i$'s (fixed) representation — they never change once computed. So store them: the KV cache. Each step now computes one new $\mathbf{k},\mathbf{v}$ and reads the rest:

$$\sum_{t=1}^{T}1=T=O(T)\ \text{projections per layer}$$

— a $T/2$-fold saving in that work ($\times50$ at $T{=}100$). The dot products $\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}_i$ still number $t$ per step; those are the irreducible cost of attending to a growing past.

3

The bill moves to memory: the cache holds $2\cdot T\cdot L\cdot d$ values and its growth — not arithmetic — is what bounds context length and serving cost. "Compute vs. memory" is the central trade of LLM inference, and you can watch it in the demo.

Intuition — take notes, don't re-read the book

Generating without a cache is re-reading the entire conversation from scratch before every single word you say. The cache is a notepad: each token's key/value is written down once, and every later word just consults the notes. For a robot whose VLA must emit actions at 10–50 Hz, this notepad is the difference between possible and impossible — latency budgets in Layer 6 are largely KV-cache budgets.

5.2 · Scaling laws — how to spend a compute budget

Definition — the compute-loss scaling law (Chinchilla form)

Across decades of scale, an LLM's loss is well described by a smooth function of parameters $N$ and training tokens $D$:

$$L(N,D)\;\approx\;E+\frac{A}{N^{\alpha}}+\frac{B}{D^{\beta}}$$

with $E$ the irreducible entropy of text, and fitted constants ($\alpha\approx0.34$, $\beta\approx0.28$ in Hoffmann et al. 2022). Training compute is approximately $C\approx 6ND$ FLOPs.

Derivation — the compute-optimal recipe
1

Fix a budget $C=6ND$ and minimize $L$ over the split. Substitute $D=C/6N$:

$$L(N)=E+\frac{A}{N^{\alpha}}+\frac{B\,(6N)^{\beta}}{C^{\beta}}$$

The first term falls with $N$ (bigger brain), the second rises (bigger brain ⇒ fewer tokens affordable) — a U-shape in $\log N$, just like Layer 1's bias-variance curve but for compute allocation.

2

Set $dL/dN=0$: $\;\alpha A N^{-\alpha-1}=\beta B\,6^{\beta}N^{\beta-1}C^{-\beta}$, giving $N^{\ast}\propto C^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}$ and hence $D^{\ast}\propto C^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}$. With $\alpha\approx\beta$: scale parameters and data together, $N^{\ast}\sim D^{\ast}\sim\sqrt{C}$ — numerically, roughly 20 training tokens per parameter.

3

The 2022 punchline: earlier giants (e.g. 175B+ params) were badly undertrained — too much brain, too little reading. Chinchilla (70B params, 1.4T tokens) beat models 2–4× its size at equal compute. An empirical law, found by curve-fitting in the spirit of Layer 1's regression, redirected billion-dollar decisions.

Where you'll meet this again

Scaling laws are why VLA labs obsess over robot-data quantity (Layer 2's Open X-Embodiment) — the same $B/D^{\beta}$ term, with demonstrations as $D$. And the cache-vs-compute trade returns in Layer 6 as the dual-system design: a big slow VLM thinking at 1 Hz, a small fast policy acting at 50 Hz.

Chapter 06

Making Models Useful

A pretrained LLM is a savant of continuation, not an assistant: ask it a question and it may continue with more questions, because that's what its data did. The alignment stack — SFT, RLHF, DPO — converts "likely text" into "helpful behavior." It is also, quietly, a preview of Layer 4: this is your first reinforcement learning.

6.1 · Supervised fine-tuning — show, don't tell

Definition — SFT / instruction tuning

Continue training with the same next-token loss, but on a curated dataset of (instruction → good response) pairs, with loss applied on the response tokens. Same objective as Ch 4 — only the distribution changed: from "all text" to "text that answers."

Intuition — imitation has a ceiling

SFT is pure imitation: clone the demonstrations. It works startlingly well, but it can't express "this answer is better than that one" — only "this answer existed." Many qualities we want (honesty over confident nonsense, refusing well, taste) are easier to judge than to demonstrate. That gap — judging is cheaper than doing — is exactly what the next idea exploits. Hold the thought for Layer 4, where the same gap separates imitation learning from reinforcement learning in robotics.

6.2 · RLHF — learning a judge, then pleasing it

Derivation — a reward model from raw preferences
1

Collect comparisons: for a prompt $x$, humans pick the better of two responses, $y_w \succ y_l$. Assume each response has a latent quality score $r(x,y)$ and that humans choose noisily by the score gap — the Bradley–Terry model:

$$P(y_w \succ y_l)=\sigma\big(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)\big)$$

This is Layer 1's logistic regression, verbatim: linear-in-score log-odds, squashed by the sigmoid.

2

Fit a neural reward model $r_\phi$ by maximum likelihood on the comparisons (Layer 1, Ch 5 — minimize $-\log\sigma(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l))$). We now have a learned judge.

3

Train the LLM as a policy $\pi_\theta$ to maximize the judge's score — but tethered to the SFT model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, or it will find degenerate text that games $r_\phi$ (reward hacking):

$$\max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big]-\beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big)$$

Layer 1's KL divergence as a leash: explore better answers, but stay a bounded number of bits from the model that already speaks sensibly. Optimized with PPO — a policy-gradient method Layer 4 derives properly.

6.3 · DPO — cutting out the middleman

Derivation — from RLHF's objective to a plain loss
1

The KL-regularized objective above has a closed-form optimal policy (write the Lagrangian per response and normalize):

$$\pi^{\ast}(y\mid x)\;\propto\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\big(r(x,y)/\beta\big)$$

— reweight the reference by exponentiated reward. Invert it: $\;r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^{\ast}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\text{const}.$

2

Substitute this $r$ into the Bradley–Terry likelihood of step 6.2.1 — the constants cancel in the difference — and the reward model vanishes:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)$$
3

Read it: raise the preferred response's probability relative to the reference; lower the rejected one's — a simple classification-style loss directly on preference pairs. No reward model, no RL loop, same optimum under the same assumptions. A derivation replaced an infrastructure stack; that's what good math is for.

6.4 · Decoding — one distribution, many personalities

Derivation — what temperature actually does
1

The model emits logits $z_i$; sampling uses $\;P_i\propto e^{z_i/T}$. Take two tokens with gap $\Delta=z_1-z_2$: their odds are $e^{\Delta/T}$. So $T$ rescales every log-odds gap by $1/T$.

2

Limits: $T\to0$ blows every gap to infinity — all mass on the argmax (greedy). $T\to\infty$ erases every gap — uniform randomness. $T{=}1$ is the model's honest belief. Layer 2 met this softmax dial in distillation; here it sets the personality of generation.

3

Truncation handles the long tail: top-k keeps the $k$ likeliest tokens; top-p (nucleus) keeps the smallest set with cumulative mass $\ge p$ — adaptive: tight when the model is confident, generous when uncertain. Renormalize, then sample. Most failure modes of "the model said something weird" are decoding settings, not weights.

Where you'll meet this again

RLHF is reinforcement learning with a learned reward — Layer 4 builds the general machinery (policies, value functions, PPO) this chapter used as a black box. And a VLA choosing its next action token faces the same decoding question: greedy precision vs. sampled diversity, now with a gripper attached.

Chapter 07

Multimodal Fusion — Building the VLM

One model that sees (Ch 1–3). One model that reasons in language (Ch 4–6). The fusion problem sounds hard and turns out to be almost embarrassingly simple — because both sides already speak the same inner language: sequences of vectors.

7.1 · One space for two senses

Intuition — everything is tokens now

Here is the payoff of Chapter 1's "heretical" move. A ViT turns an image into ~196 vectors. An LLM consumes a sequence of vectors (its token embeddings). These are the same data type. So the entire grand problem of multimodal AI reduces to one question: how do you make image vectors legible to a language model? Layer 2's CLIP gave the first answer — train both encoders so matching image/text pairs score high under the dot product (the agreement meter of Layer 1, all the way down). CLIP proves a shared meaning-space is learnable. But CLIP can only compare; it cannot discuss. For that, the image tokens must enter the LLM itself.

7.2 · Two ways in: adapters and cross-attention

Definition — the projection-adapter VLM (LLaVA pattern)

Freeze (or lightly tune) a pretrained vision encoder $f_V$ and a pretrained LLM. Learn a small projector $W$ — a linear map or 2-layer MLP — carrying patch tokens into the LLM's embedding space, then splice them into the prompt as if they were words:

$$\big[\;W f_V(\text{image})_{1:n}\;;\;\text{embed}(\text{"What is on the table?"})\;\big]\;\longrightarrow\;\text{LLM}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{"A red block."}$$

Trained on image–caption pairs then visual-instruction data, with the usual next-token loss. The trainable part can be a thousandth of the system.

Definition — the cross-attention VLM (Flamingo pattern)

Alternatively, leave the LLM's input stream purely textual and insert fresh cross-attention layers between its blocks: text tokens form queries, image tokens form keys and values (a learned resampler first condenses patches to a fixed few). Only the inserted layers train. Vision becomes something the model consults rather than reads inline.

Derivation — why a mere linear map can possibly suffice
1

What must the projector preserve? The LLM consumes embeddings through attention — dot products (Layer 1's agreement meter). So image tokens are "legible" if their relative geometry (which vectors agree with which) lands meaningfully in the LLM's space.

2

Both encoders were pretrained on (descriptions of) the same world, so both spaces already encode similar neighborhood structure — "red things near red things, graspable near graspable." Aligning two spaces that share structure is mostly a rotation-and-stretch job, and Layer 1, Ch 2 told you what rotations and stretches are: one matrix. $W$ is a translator between two dialects of the same semantics, not a perception module.

3

The empirical confirmation (LLaVA, 2023): a 2-layer projector plus ~half a million instruction examples yields a conversational VLM on academic hardware. The heavy lifting was already done — by the pretraining of the two giants being joined. Pretrain big pieces once, weld with small learned joints: the era's recurring blueprint (recall SAM in Ch 3).

The LLaVA-pattern VLM: two pretrained giants, one small trained joint (coral). Production VLMs — Qwen-VL, PaliGemma, InternVL, and frontier assistants — are elaborations of this diagram.

7.3 · Grounding — where the model looks

Once image tokens sit in the same sequence as words, attention does the rest: generating the word "red", the model's queries score highest against the patches whose vectors carry redness. The text is grounded — each word causally traceable to evidence in pixels. You can watch a toy version of it:

From VLM to VLA

Stand back and look at what three layers have assembled.

8.1 · The last missing piece

A vision-language-action model, as Layer 2's finale previewed, is a VLM whose output head emits robot actions instead of (or alongside) words. You now own every part of that sentence: the vision encoder is Ch 1–2 of this layer; the language backbone is Ch 4–6; the splice between them is Ch 7; the transformer they all share is Layer 2; and every loss, gradient, and probability underneath is Layer 1. RT-2 literally fine-tunes a VLM to print action tokens from its existing vocabulary; π0-style models bolt a continuous action head onto a pretrained VLM. The robot brain is this chapter's diagram with one extra arrow.

The whole layer, in one paragraph

Images became sentences (patches as tokens), so one architecture could read everything. Vision learned without labels (a student chasing its calmer EMA self, saved from collapse by centering + sharpening), and classic tasks were rebuilt as set prediction (DETR) and promptable skills (SAM). Language modeling turned out to be Layer 1's chain rule plus MLE at planetary scale, made affordable by a notepad (the KV cache) and budgeted by an empirical law (scale brain and books together). Alignment turned likely text into helpful text — logistic regression on preferences, a KL leash, and DPO's beautiful shortcut. And fusion was almost an anticlimax: both giants already thought in vectors, so one small matrix could translate. What is still missing is the subject of the next layer: none of these models has ever acted and felt the consequences.

Where this goes next

Layer 4 — Decision Making — adds the missing ingredient: consequences. Reinforcement learning (policies, value functions, the PPO that RLHF borrowed), imitation learning and its compounding-error problem, and the demonstration-driven methods that train real robot policies. The VLM you built here is about to get hands.
