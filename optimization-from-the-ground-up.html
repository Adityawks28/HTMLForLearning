Optimization, from the ground up: how to roll downhill on purpose

Every method in this lecture is one idea wearing different outfits: stand on a hillside in fog, feel which way is down, take a step, repeat. The methods differ only in how cleverly they pick the step. Drag things, press buttons — you can see all of it move.

gradient (uphill) descent path Newton the minimum
§ 1

The goal: find the lowest point

Optimization is just looking for the bottom of a valley.

We're handed a function $f(\mathbf x)$ — read it as a landscape, where the height at location $\mathbf x$ is $f(\mathbf x)$. "Minimize $f$" means: find the location with the lowest height. Two flavors of "lowest":

in plain terms A global minimizer is the lowest point in the entire landscape. A local minimizer is the lowest point in its own little neighborhood — the bottom of one valley, even if a deeper valley exists somewhere else. The fog matters: standing in one valley, you can't see whether a deeper one is over the ridge.

The tool we'll lean on is the gradient $\nabla f(\mathbf x)$ — the vector of partial derivatives, i.e. the local slope. A critical point is where the ground goes flat: $\nabla f(\bar{\mathbf x})=\mathbf 0$. Here's the catch that trips up beginners: every minimizer is a critical point, but not every critical point is a minimizer. Flat ground can be a valley bottom, a hilltop, or a saddle (the seat-shaped point that's a valley one way and a hill the other).

§ 2

Which way is downhill?

The gradient points straight uphill. So its opposite is your best step.

A foundational fact: $\nabla f(\mathbf x)$ points in the direction of steepest increase. Therefore $-\nabla f(\mathbf x)$ points in the direction of steepest decrease — straight downhill. But you don't have to go exactly downhill to make progress; you just have to go somewhat downhill.

The descent-direction test

A direction $\delta\mathbf x$ takes you downhill (for a small enough step) exactly when

$$\nabla f(\mathbf x)^\top \delta\mathbf x \;<\; 0.$$

This is an inner product being negative: the step must make an angle greater than $90^\circ$ with the uphill gradient. The very best such step is $\delta\mathbf x=-\nabla f(\mathbf x)$.

Proof — why a negative dot product guarantees descent Fix the point $\mathbf x$ and a direction $\delta\mathbf x$, and watch the height as you walk along it: define the one-variable function $\varphi(\alpha)=f(\mathbf x+\alpha\,\delta\mathbf x)$. By the chain rule its slope at the start is $$\varphi'(0)=\nabla f(\mathbf x)^\top\delta\mathbf x.$$ If this is strictly negative, then $\varphi$ is decreasing at $\alpha=0$. By the definition of derivative, $\frac{\varphi(\alpha)-\varphi(0)}{\alpha}\to\varphi'(0)<0$, so the ratio is negative for all small enough $\alpha>0$ — which means there is an $\varepsilon>0$ with $\varphi(\alpha)<\varphi(0)$, i.e. $f(\mathbf x+\alpha\,\delta\mathbf x)∎
two definitions to nail down The gradient is the column of partial derivatives, $\nabla f(\mathbf x)=\big[\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\;\cdots\;\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_d}\big]^\top$. Among all unit-length descent directions, the one that makes $\varphi'(0)=\nabla f^\top\delta\mathbf x$ most negative — by Cauchy–Schwarz, the most aligned with $-\nabla f$ — is the steepest-descent direction $\;\delta\mathbf x=-\nabla f(\mathbf x)/\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf x)\rVert.$

§ 3

How do you know you've arrived?

Two checks: the ground is flat, and it curves upward.

The conditions on the slide become very intuitive once you picture the valley bottom:

in plain terms First check (necessary): the slope is zero, $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$. You can't be at a bottom if the ground tilts — you'd just slide.
Second check: the ground curves upward in every direction. The curvature is measured by the Hessian $\nabla^2 f$ (the matrix of second derivatives). "Curves up everywhere" is written $\nabla^2 f \succeq 0$ (positive semidefinite) as a necessary condition, and $\nabla^2 f \succ 0$ (positive definite) is enough to guarantee a local minimum.

Why both? Flat-and-curving-up is a valley bottom; flat-and-curving-down is a hilltop; flat-but-curving-up-one-way-and-down-another is a saddle. The Hessian is exactly the device that tells these flat points apart. (Recognize $\succ 0$? That's the positive-definite idea from the linear-algebra half — the same matrices that gave squared lengths now measure upward curvature.)

The conditions, compactly

Necessary (if $\bar{\mathbf x}$ is a local min): $\;\nabla f(\bar{\mathbf x})=\mathbf 0$ and $\nabla^2 f(\bar{\mathbf x})\succeq 0$.

Sufficient (then $\bar{\mathbf x}$ is a local min): $\;\nabla f(\bar{\mathbf x})=\mathbf 0$ and $\nabla^2 f(\bar{\mathbf x})\succ 0$.

§ 4

The dream case: convex

One bowl, no false bottoms — and "flat ground" is all you ever need to check.

Two things can be convex: the region you're searching, and the function you're minimizing. Both appear in the lecture.

Definition · convex set

A set $D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^d$ is convex if for every pair of points in it, the whole straight segment joining them stays inside:

$$\lambda\mathbf x+(1-\lambda)\mathbf y\in D\quad\text{for all }\mathbf x,\mathbf y\in D,\ \lambda\in[0,1].$$

In plain terms: no dents, no holes, no gaps. A disk, a box, a half-plane are convex; a crescent or a donut is not. This is the shape a constraint region must have for the projected method in §6 to behave.

A convex function is one big bowl: no separate valleys, no fog problem. The defining picture is the chord test — pick any two points on the graph and connect them with a straight line; for a convex function that chord never dips below the curve. Formally, $f$ is convex when its domain is a convex set and $f(\lambda\mathbf x+(1-\lambda)\mathbf y)\le\lambda f(\mathbf x)+(1-\lambda)f(\mathbf y)$ for all $\lambda\in[0,1]$ — the curve sits below the chord. Drag the two points and switch between a convex bowl and a wavy non-convex landscape to feel the difference:

why we care For a convex $f$, the first check alone is the whole story: $\nabla f(\bar{\mathbf x})=\mathbf 0\iff\bar{\mathbf x}$ is a global minimizer. No fog, no false bottoms, no need to test curvature point by point. This is why people work hard to pose problems as convex ones. The algebraic tests: a smooth $f$ is convex iff $\nabla^2 f(\mathbf x)\succeq 0$ everywhere (curves up at every point), equivalently iff every tangent line lies below the graph: $f(\mathbf y)\ge f(\mathbf x)+\nabla f(\mathbf x)^\top(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)$.
Proof — the chord test gives the tangent-below test Start from the chord definition and let the two points be $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf y$. For $\lambda\in(0,1]$, write the convex combination as $\mathbf x+\lambda(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)$. Convexity says $$f\big(\mathbf x+\lambda(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)\big)\le(1-\lambda)f(\mathbf x)+\lambda f(\mathbf y)=f(\mathbf x)+\lambda\big(f(\mathbf y)-f(\mathbf x)\big).$$ Subtract $f(\mathbf x)$ and divide by $\lambda>0$: $$\frac{f\big(\mathbf x+\lambda(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)\big)-f(\mathbf x)}{\lambda}\le f(\mathbf y)-f(\mathbf x).$$ Let $\lambda\to 0^+$. The left side is the definition of the directional derivative of $f$ at $\mathbf x$ along $\mathbf y-\mathbf x$, which equals $\nabla f(\mathbf x)^\top(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)$. Hence $\nabla f(\mathbf x)^\top(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)\le f(\mathbf y)-f(\mathbf x)$, i.e. $f(\mathbf y)\ge f(\mathbf x)+\nabla f(\mathbf x)^\top(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)$. ∎
Corollary — for convex $f$, a flat point is a global minimum Suppose $\nabla f(\bar{\mathbf x})=\mathbf 0$. Plug it into the tangent-below inequality: for every $\mathbf y$, $f(\mathbf y)\ge f(\bar{\mathbf x})+\mathbf 0^\top(\mathbf y-\bar{\mathbf x})=f(\bar{\mathbf x})$. So $\bar{\mathbf x}$ beats every other point — it is a global minimizer. The converse holds because every minimizer is a critical point. ∎
§ 5

Gradient descent: step downhill, repeat

The simplest method, and the workhorse behind most of machine learning.

We can rarely solve $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$ by hand, so we sneak up on it. Start somewhere, then repeatedly take a step in the downhill direction:

$$\mathbf x_{k+1}=\mathbf x_k-\alpha\,\nabla f(\mathbf x_k).$$

The number $\alpha$ is the step size (in machine learning, the "learning rate"). It is a Goldilocks knob:

the step-size trade-off Too small → you creep, taking forever. Too big → you overshoot the valley and bounce up the far wall, sometimes flying off to infinity. A safe choice is $\alpha=1/L$, where $L$ is how fast the slope can change (the Lipschitz constant of $\nabla f$). Try to break it below.
Definition · the general descent template & how to pick the step

Every method in this lecture is a special case of one loop: from $\mathbf x_k$, choose a descent direction $\delta\mathbf x_k$ and a step size $\alpha_k>0$, then

$$\mathbf x_{k+1}=\mathbf x_k+\alpha_k\,\delta\mathbf x_k.$$

Gradient descent is the choice $\delta\mathbf x_k=-\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)$; Newton (§7) and Gauss–Newton (§8) choose smarter directions. For the step size there are two standard recipes:

Exact line search: walk along $\delta\mathbf x_k$ to the lowest point on that line, $\;\alpha_k\in\arg\min_{\alpha>0}f(\mathbf x_k+\alpha\,\delta\mathbf x_k).$ Optimal per step, but usually too costly.

Approximate (backtracking) line search: start with a trial $\alpha$ and shrink it until $f$ drops by a "sufficient" amount. Cheap, and what's used in practice.

The fixed choice $\alpha=1/L$ used below is the simplest safe option, where $L$ is the Lipschitz constant of the gradient: the smallest number with $\;\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf x)-\nabla f(\mathbf x')\rVert\le L\,\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf x'\rVert\;$ for all $\mathbf x,\mathbf x'$. It caps how fast the slope can swing, so a step of $1/L$ can never overshoot.

This playground minimizes a stretched bowl $f(x,y)=\tfrac12\!\left(x^2+\kappa\,y^2\right)$. Drag the start point; scrub the steps slider to walk the path; raise the stretch $\kappa$ to see the dreaded zig-zag; push the step size past the stability limit to watch it diverge.

Definition · the convergence result, in words

If the bowl is bounded between two curvatures, $mI\preceq\nabla^2 f\preceq LI$, then with $\alpha=1/L$ the distance to the minimum shrinks by a constant factor each step:

$$\lVert\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x^\star\rVert\le\Big(1-\tfrac{m}{L}\Big)^{k}\lVert\mathbf x_0-\mathbf x^\star\rVert.$$

The ratio $\kappa=L/m$ is the condition number. A round bowl has $\kappa=1$ and converges in almost one step; a stretched bowl has large $\kappa$, the factor $1-m/L$ is close to $1$, and progress is painfully slow. That is what the zig-zag above is showing you.

The proof, step by step

Here is the complete derivation from the slides — it's worth doing in full, because the single trick (a mean-value-theorem step plus an eigenvalue bound) is reused verbatim for projected gradient descent in §6. Assume $f$ is twice continuously differentiable with $mI\preceq\nabla^2 f(\mathbf x)\preceq LI$ everywhere, and take $\alpha=1/L$.

Part 1 — the slope dies out: $\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)\rVert\to 0$
  1. Mean value theorem for the gradient. There is a point $\mathbf c_k$ on the segment between $\mathbf x_k$ and $\mathbf x_{k+1}$ with $\nabla f(\mathbf x_{k+1})=\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)+\nabla^2 f(\mathbf c_k)(\mathbf x_{k+1}-\mathbf x_k)$. Since the update is $\mathbf x_{k+1}-\mathbf x_k=-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)$, $$\nabla f(\mathbf x_{k+1})=\big(I-\alpha\,\nabla^2 f(\mathbf c_k)\big)\nabla f(\mathbf x_k).$$
  2. Bound the matrix by its eigenvalues. $\nabla^2 f(\mathbf c_k)$ is symmetric with eigenvalues $\lambda_i\in[m,L]$. So $I-\alpha\nabla^2 f(\mathbf c_k)$ has eigenvalues $1-\alpha\lambda_i$. With $\alpha=1/L$, the bound $m\le\lambda_i\le L$ gives $$0\;=\;1-\tfrac{L}{L}\;\le\;1-\tfrac{\lambda_i}{L}\;\le\;1-\tfrac{m}{L}\;<\;1.$$ Because the matrix is symmetric, its spectral norm equals its largest eigenvalue magnitude, so $\lVert I-\alpha\nabla^2 f(\mathbf c_k)\rVert\le 1-\tfrac{m}{L}$.
  3. Contraction, then iterate. Taking norms in step 1, $\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf x_{k+1})\rVert\le\big(1-\tfrac{m}{L}\big)\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)\rVert$. Applying this $k{+}1$ times, $$\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf x_{k+1})\rVert\le\big(1-\tfrac{m}{L}\big)^{k+1}\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf x_0)\rVert\;\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\;0,$$ since $0\le 1-\tfrac{m}{L}<1$. The slope vanishes linearly. ∎
Part 2 — the iterates land on the minimum: $\lVert\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x^\star\rVert\to 0$
  1. Mean value theorem again, now toward $\mathbf x^\star$. For some $\tilde{\mathbf c}_k$ between $\mathbf x_k$ and $\mathbf x^\star$, $\;\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)-\nabla f(\mathbf x^\star)=\nabla^2 f(\tilde{\mathbf c}_k)(\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x^\star)$. The minimizer is critical, $\nabla f(\mathbf x^\star)=\mathbf 0$, so $\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)=\nabla^2 f(\tilde{\mathbf c}_k)(\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x^\star)$.
  2. Substitute into the update. $\;\mathbf x_{k+1}-\mathbf x^\star=(\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x^\star)-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)=\big(I-\alpha\nabla^2 f(\tilde{\mathbf c}_k)\big)(\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x^\star).$
  3. Same eigenvalue bound finishes it. The matrix is again $\le 1-\tfrac{m}{L}$ in norm, so $\lVert\mathbf x_{k+1}-\mathbf x^\star\rVert\le\big(1-\tfrac{m}{L}\big)\lVert\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x^\star\rVert$, and therefore $$\lVert\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x^\star\rVert\le\big(1-\tfrac{m}{L}\big)^{k}\lVert\mathbf x_0-\mathbf x^\star\rVert\to 0.$$ ∎
§ 6

Projected gradient descent: optimizing inside a fence

What if the answer must obey constraints? Take a normal step, then snap back into the allowed region.

So far the search ran over all of $\mathbb{R}^d$. Often it can't: a probability must stay in $[0,1]$, a pose may be box-bounded, an estimate must lie in a convex feasible set $C$. The problem becomes $\min_{\mathbf x\in C}f(\mathbf x)$ with $C$ a closed convex set. The fix is beautifully simple — gradient-step as usual, then project back onto $C$:

$$\mathbf x_{k+1}=\Pi_C\big(\mathbf x_k-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)\big),\qquad \Pi_C(\mathbf z):=\arg\min_{\mathbf y\in C}\lVert\mathbf y-\mathbf z\rVert.$$

$\Pi_C$ is the closest-point map — exactly the orthogonal projection you proved properties of in the linear-algebra lecture, now onto a set instead of a subspace. Drag the start point; each step takes a gradient move (which may leave the box) and then snaps to the nearest feasible point. Watch it slide along the fence to the constrained optimum:

Two facts make this converge, and both are worth proving — they're short and they reuse the orthogonal-projection ideas you already have.

Definition · the variational characterization of projection

For a closed convex $C$, the point $\mathbf p=\Pi_C(\mathbf z)$ is characterized by an obtuse-angle condition: the vector from $\mathbf p$ to $\mathbf z$ makes a $\ge 90^\circ$ angle with every feasible direction,

$$\langle \mathbf z-\mathbf p,\ \mathbf y-\mathbf p\rangle\le 0\quad\text{for all }\mathbf y\in C.$$

(This is just the first-order optimality condition for minimizing $\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf y-\mathbf z\rVert^2$ over the convex set $C$: the gradient $\mathbf p-\mathbf z$ cannot point into any feasible direction.)

Fact 1 — projection never increases distances (non-expansive) Claim: $\lVert\Pi_C(\mathbf x)-\Pi_C(\mathbf y)\rVert\le\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf y\rVert$. Let $\mathbf p=\Pi_C(\mathbf x)$, $\mathbf q=\Pi_C(\mathbf y)$.
  1. Apply the characterization at $\mathbf p$ with the feasible point $\mathbf q$: $\;\langle\mathbf x-\mathbf p,\ \mathbf q-\mathbf p\rangle\le 0.$
  2. Apply it at $\mathbf q$ with the feasible point $\mathbf p$: $\;\langle\mathbf y-\mathbf q,\ \mathbf p-\mathbf q\rangle\le 0.$
  3. Add the two inequalities and group terms around $(\mathbf q-\mathbf p)$: $$\langle(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)-(\mathbf p-\mathbf q),\ \mathbf q-\mathbf p\rangle\le 0\;\Longrightarrow\;\lVert\mathbf p-\mathbf q\rVert^2\le\langle\mathbf x-\mathbf y,\ \mathbf p-\mathbf q\rangle.$$
  4. Cauchy–Schwarz on the right: $\lVert\mathbf p-\mathbf q\rVert^2\le\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf y\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf p-\mathbf q\rVert$. Divide by $\lVert\mathbf p-\mathbf q\rVert$ to get $\lVert\mathbf p-\mathbf q\rVert\le\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf y\rVert$. ∎
Fact 2 — the solution is a fixed point of the iteration Claim: $\mathbf x^\star$ solves $\min_{\mathbf x\in C}f$ iff $\mathbf x^\star=\Pi_C(\mathbf x^\star-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf x^\star))$ for any $\alpha>0$. The constrained optimality condition for convex $f$ is $\langle\nabla f(\mathbf x^\star),\mathbf y-\mathbf x^\star\rangle\ge 0$ for all $\mathbf y\in C$ (no feasible direction goes downhill). Now read the projection characterization with $\mathbf p=\mathbf x^\star$ and $\mathbf z=\mathbf x^\star-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf x^\star)$: $$\mathbf x^\star=\Pi_C(\mathbf z)\iff\langle\mathbf z-\mathbf x^\star,\mathbf y-\mathbf x^\star\rangle\le0\iff\langle-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf x^\star),\mathbf y-\mathbf x^\star\rangle\le0\iff\langle\nabla f(\mathbf x^\star),\mathbf y-\mathbf x^\star\rangle\ge0,$$ which is exactly the optimality condition (dividing by $\alpha>0$). ∎
Putting them together — convergence Both $\mathbf x_{k+1}=\Pi_C(\mathbf x_k-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf x_k))$ and $\mathbf x^\star=\Pi_C(\mathbf x^\star-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf x^\star))$ are projections. By Fact 1 (non-expansiveness), $$\lVert\mathbf x_{k+1}-\mathbf x^\star\rVert\le\big\lVert(\mathbf x_k-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf x_k))-(\mathbf x^\star-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf x^\star))\big\rVert=\big\lVert(\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x^\star)-\alpha(\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)-\nabla f(\mathbf x^\star))\big\rVert.$$ The right-hand side is identical to the quantity bounded in Part 2 of the gradient-descent proof, $\le(1-\tfrac{m}{L})\lVert\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x^\star\rVert$. So $\lVert\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x^\star\rVert\le(1-\tfrac{m}{L})^k\lVert\mathbf x_0-\mathbf x^\star\rVert\to0$: the projection comes for free — adding the constraint costs nothing in the convergence rate. ∎
§ 7

Newton's method: use the curve, not just the slope

Fit a parabola to where you stand, then jump straight to the bottom of that parabola.

Gradient descent only knows the slope. Newton's method also uses the curvature (the Hessian), which lets it take much smarter steps. The idea: near your current point, approximate $f$ by the best-fitting parabola (its second-order Taylor expansion), then leap to that parabola's minimum — and repeat.

$$\mathbf x_{k+1}=\mathbf x_k-\big(\nabla^2 f(\mathbf x_k)\big)^{-1}\nabla f(\mathbf x_k).$$

Press Step and watch the violet parabola snap onto the curve at the current point; the next point lands at its lowest spot. Notice how few steps it takes:

the catch Newton needs the Hessian $\nabla^2 f$ — and it must be positive-definite (a real bowl, not a saddle) for the jump to point downhill. In high dimensions, forming and inverting that matrix is expensive, and far from the optimum the raw jump can overshoot. The fix is a damped step $\mathbf x_{k+1}=\mathbf x_k-\alpha(\nabla^2 f)^{-1}\nabla f$ with a cautious $\alpha<1$ early on, switching to the full $\alpha=1$ once you're close. That's the "damped phase → quadratic phase" the slides describe.
§ 8

Gauss–Newton & Levenberg–Marquardt

The version that runs your SLAM solver — Newton's speed without the costly second derivatives.

A huge fraction of robotics problems are least squares: minimize a sum of squared errors, $f(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf e(\mathbf x)\rVert^2$, where $\mathbf e$ stacks all your measurement residuals. For this special shape, the gradient and Hessian are $$\nabla f=J^\top\mathbf e,\qquad \nabla^2 f=J^\top J+\textstyle\sum_i e_i\nabla^2 e_i,$$ where $J=\partial\mathbf e/\partial\mathbf x$ is the Jacobian. Near a good fit the residuals $e_i$ are small, so that messy second term nearly vanishes.

Gauss–Newton, in one line Throw away the expensive second-derivative term and approximate the Hessian by $J^\top J$. The Newton step becomes $$J^\top J\,\delta\mathbf x=-J^\top\mathbf e,$$ which needs only first derivatives. These are exactly the normal equations — the orthogonal-projection / least-squares system from the linear-algebra half. Each Gauss–Newton step projects the linearized residual.
The cleaner derivation — linearize the residual, not the cost There's a second route to the same system that makes the "projection" reading obvious. Instead of approximating $f$, approximate the residual vector itself with its first-order Taylor expansion around $\mathbf x_k$: $$\mathbf e(\mathbf x_k+\delta\mathbf x)\approx\mathbf e(\mathbf x_k)+J\,\delta\mathbf x.$$ Substitute into $f=\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf e\rVert^2$ to get a quadratic in $\delta\mathbf x$: $$f\approx\tfrac12\big\lVert\mathbf e(\mathbf x_k)+J\,\delta\mathbf x\big\rVert^2=\tfrac12\,\mathbf e^\top\mathbf e+\mathbf e^\top J\,\delta\mathbf x+\tfrac12\,\delta\mathbf x^\top J^\top J\,\delta\mathbf x.$$ Set the gradient with respect to $\delta\mathbf x$ to zero: $\;J^\top\mathbf e+J^\top J\,\delta\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$, i.e. $\;J^\top J\,\delta\mathbf x=-J^\top\mathbf e$ — the same normal equations. This is literally "project $-\mathbf e(\mathbf x_k)$ onto the column space of $J$," which is why each step is an orthogonal projection of the linearized residual. ∎

§ 9

The grand finale: one problem, every method

When the bowl is an exact quadratic, the smart methods finish in a single step.

The lecture closes by running all four methods on $f(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\sum_{j}\lVert A_j\mathbf x+\mathbf b_j\rVert^2$. Its gradient and Hessian are $$\nabla f(\mathbf x)=\Big(\textstyle\sum_j A_j^\top A_j\Big)\mathbf x+\textstyle\sum_j A_j^\top\mathbf b_j,\qquad \nabla^2 f=\textstyle\sum_j A_j^\top A_j\;(\text{constant!}).$$

Because the Hessian is constant and positive-definite, this is a perfect quadratic bowl, and three things fall out:

the punchline Convex condition: set $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$ and solve once for the exact answer $\mathbf x^\star=-(\sum_j A_j^\top A_j)^{-1}(\sum_j A_j^\top\mathbf b_j)$.
Newton: its parabola model is $f$ exactly, so a single full step lands on $\mathbf x^\star$.
Gauss–Newton: with $J_j=A_j$ it solves the identical system — also one step.
Gradient descent: still has to crawl, taking many steps at a rate set by the condition number of $\sum_j A_j^\top A_j$.

That's the whole lecture in one picture: the methods differ only in how much they exploit the shape of the bowl. Gradient descent uses the slope; Newton and Gauss–Newton use the curvature and so cut straight to the bottom of any quadratic — which is exactly what a linearized SLAM step is.

§ P

Practice

Short and concrete. Try each, then reveal the answer.

P1  Minimize $f(x)=x^2-4x+7$ by hand. Find the minimizer and confirm it really is a minimum.

$f'(x)=2x-4=0\Rightarrow x=2$ (first check: flat ground). $f''(x)=2>0$ everywhere (second check: curves up), so $x=2$ is a minimum, with $f(2)=3$. Since $f$ is convex, it's the global minimum.

P2  Is $f(x,y)=x^2+3y^2$ convex? Where is its minimum, and what is its condition number?

Hessian $\nabla^2 f=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&6\end{bmatrix}\succ 0$, so $f$ is convex. Minimum at $(0,0)$. Curvatures are $m=2$, $L=6$, so $\kappa=L/m=3$ — a mildly stretched bowl, gradient descent will zig-zag a little.

P3  One gradient-descent step on $f(x,y)=\tfrac12(x^2+9y^2)$ from $(4,2)$. Try $\alpha=0.1$, then $\alpha=0.25$. What goes wrong with the bigger step?

$\nabla f=(x,9y)=(4,18)$. With $\alpha=0.1$: $\mathbf x_1=(4,2)-0.1(4,18)=(3.6,0.2)$ — both shrink toward $0$, good. With $\alpha=0.25$: the $y$-update is $2-0.25\cdot18=-2.5$ — it overshot the valley and landed farther out. The stability limit here is $\alpha<2/L=2/9\approx0.22$, and $0.25$ breaks it: the $y$-coordinate diverges. (Match this against the playground in §5.)

P4  Newton's method on $f(x)=3x^2-12x+5$. Take one step from any start $x_0$. What do you notice?

$f'(x)=6x-12$, $f''(x)=6$. Newton step: $x_1=x_0-\dfrac{6x_0-12}{6}=x_0-(x_0-2)=2$. One step lands exactly on the minimum $x=2$, no matter where you start — because the parabola Newton fits to a quadratic is the function itself. This is the §9 finale in miniature, and the reason Gauss–Newton solves a linearized SLAM step in one shot.

P5  Projected gradient descent. Minimize $f(x)=\tfrac12(x-5)^2$ over the interval $C=[0,2]$ with $\alpha=0.5$, starting at $x_0=0.5$. Take two steps, and identify the constrained minimum.

Here $\Pi_C(z)=\min(\max(z,0),2)$ (clamp to $[0,2]$), and $f'(x)=x-5$. Step 1: gradient move $0.5-0.5(0.5-5)=0.5+2.25=2.75$, project $\to 2$. Step 2: from $2$, gradient move $2-0.5(2-5)=2+1.5=3.5$, project $\to 2$. It's stuck at $x=2$ — and that's correct: the unconstrained minimizer is $x=5$, outside $C$, so the constrained minimum sits on the nearest boundary, $x^\star=2$. Check the fixed-point fact: $\Pi_C(2-0.5\cdot(2-5))=\Pi_C(3.5)=2=x^\star$. ✓

P6  In the convergence proof, why does the step $\alpha=1/L$ make the contraction factor work? Show the eigenvalues of $I-\alpha\nabla^2 f$ land in $[0,\,1-m/L]$.

The eigenvalues of $\nabla^2 f$ lie in $[m,L]$, so those of $I-\alpha\nabla^2 f$ are $1-\alpha\lambda_i$ with $\lambda_i\in[m,L]$. Plug $\alpha=1/L$: the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_i=L$ gives $1-L/L=0$; the smallest $\lambda_i=m$ gives $1-m/L$. So every eigenvalue is in $[0,\,1-m/L]$. Two consequences: it's $\ge 0$ (no sign flips, so no overshoot/divergence), and it's $<1$ (strict shrink each step). A larger $\alpha$ would push the top eigenvalue below $0$ and eventually past $-1$ — that's exactly the divergence you trigger in the §5 playground.
